Mineral water

The most-used ingredient on the site, and the least examined. Its dissolved minerals change how tea, coffee, beans and dough behave, and how a bottle tastes at the table.

About

Water is the solvent every other ingredient on this site dissolves into, and it is never pure. Even good tap water carries tens to hundreds of milligrams per litre (mg/L) of dissolved minerals; a bottled mineral water can carry several thousand. Those ions decide whether beans soften, how fast tea and coffee extract, how tight a dough feels, and how the water tastes on its own. Choosing a water for a purpose, the way you would choose a wine, follows from that.

All mineral figures on this page are in mg/L. "About" marks a typical value; a range means sources disagree, often by 10 to 20 percent. Springs drift and brands change sources, so the label on the bottle in your hand is the authority.

Understanding water

Four numbers describe a water:

  1. Total dissolved solids (TDS). The weight of everything dissolved in a litre, often printed as "dry residue at 180 °C". It is the best single predictor of taste and feel. The Fine Water Society bands, used to order the catalogue below, are super low (under about 50), low (50 to 250), medium (250 to 800), high (800 to 1,500) and very high (over about 1,500).
  2. Hardness. The calcium and magnesium content. It gives body and chalky dryness, and drives most of the kitchen effects below.
  3. Alkalinity. Mostly bicarbonate, which buffers acid. It gives a soft, round finish and mutes acidity in coffee, tea and wine.
  4. pH. Below 7 tastes slightly tart; above 8 can feel soft. Carbon dioxide always lowers it. pH counts for little of a water's overall character.

Two more properties matter. Carbonation can be natural from the spring (usually finer bubbles), added at bottling, or the spring's own gas captured and re-added. Virginality, freedom from human influence, is usually read from nitrate: under about 2 mg/L suggests a well-protected source.

What each mineral does

EU label thresholds come from Directive 2009/54/EC, Annex III.

  • Calcium. Soft, faintly chalky or milky-sweet. Supports bones and teeth, and calcium from mineral water absorbs as well as calcium from milk. "Contains calcium" means more than 150. The adult daily need is about 1,000 to 1,200 mg.
  • Magnesium. Bitter and drying above about 50. Supports muscles, nerves and sleep; most Western diets fall short. With sulfate it is laxative. "Contains magnesium" means more than 50. The adult daily need is about 310 to 420 mg.
  • Bicarbonate. A smooth, slightly sweet finish. Buffers stomach acid, which is the basis of the heartburn evidence, and dulls an acidic wine. "Contains bicarbonate" means more than 600.
  • Sodium. Rounds and salts the water; clearly salty above about 200. Replaces sweat losses but counts toward a daily limit of about 2,300 mg. "Suitable for a low-sodium diet" means under 20; "contains sodium" means more than 200.
  • Sulfate. Bitter and dry. With magnesium it is laxative. "Contains sulfate" means more than 200.
  • Chloride. Salty, usually paired with sodium. "Contains chloride" means more than 200.
  • Silica. A silky mouthfeel; it explains the smoothness of Fiji and Socosani. Small studies suggest it increases aluminium excretion; clinical benefit is not established.
  • Fluoride. Protects teeth at low doses, causes dental fluorosis in children at high doses. "Contains fluoride" means more than 1 mg/L. Above 1.5 the label must warn that the water is not suitable for regular consumption by infants and children under 7. The legal maximum is 5 (Directive 2003/40/EC).
  • Nitrate. No taste. Under 10 is good, under 2 excellent; the legal maximum is 50.

Reading a label

  • "Very low mineral content" means a dry residue of 50 or less; "low mineral content" 500 or less; "rich in mineral salts" more than 1,500. Above 250 mg/L of free carbon dioxide, a water may be labelled "acidic".
  • An EU natural mineral water comes from a protected underground source, is bottled at source with a stable composition, and is barely treated. Spring water has looser rules. Purified or drinking water (Dasani, Aquafina, Smartwater, Essentia) is often treated municipal water.
  • A German Heilwasser (St. Gero, Staatl. Fachingen) is a licensed medicinal water that has proved its effect to the drug regulator.

Water in the kitchen

  • Beans and legumes. Calcium and magnesium cross-link the pectin in bean cell walls and slow softening, so dried beans can stay firm after hours in very hard water. Soft or filtered water avoids the problem. A pinch of baking soda raises the pH and speeds softening, at some cost to flavour and texture if overdone.
  • Tea. Low-mineral water extracts tea cleanly. Hard water, especially high in calcium bicarbonate, dulls the flavour and leaves a film on black tea. Green and white tea want under about 100 TDS; black tea and pu-erh tolerate more. See steeping tea.
  • Coffee. Some minerals are needed to extract flavour; too much bicarbonate flattens acidity, and distilled water extracts poorly. The 2009 SCAA standard targets TDS 150 (range 75 to 250), calcium hardness 68 as calcium carbonate (range 17 to 85), alkalinity about 40, pH 7.0 (range 6.5 to 7.5), sodium about 10, and no chlorine. Castle Rock is close on its own; a 50/50 blend of Mountain Valley and Saratoga lands near the target (a calculation, not a tested result).
  • Bread. Moderately hard water strengthens gluten slightly. Very soft water gives a slack, sticky dough; very hard water tightens it and slows fermentation. Chlorine can slow a sourdough starter. Water left standing for some hours loses much of its chlorine, but not chloramine.
  • Batter. Ice-cold sparkling water in a tempura or fried-fish batter adds bubbles that expand in the oil, and the cold limits gluten. The crust is lighter and lacier. The gas and the temperature matter more than the mineral content.
  • Ice and cocktails. Hard water clouds ice and scales kettles. Topo Chico and similar bold sparkling waters are the standard highball and ranch-water mixers.

How to taste water

In judging a water, mouthfeel counts most, mineral density next, and pH least. Work through appearance and bubbles, aroma (a good water smells of nothing), weight, taste, and finish.

  1. Serve every sample at the same temperature. Some sommeliers taste at room temperature, others at 12 to 17 °C. Never ice-cold: cold numbs the tongue.
  2. Use identical stemmed glasses, about 60 ml each.
  3. Go from the lowest TDS to the highest, and still before sparkling.
  4. Hold a small sip for three seconds.
  5. Reset between waters with a super-low water or a plain unsalted cracker. Taste blind where you can.

Vocabulary. Weight: light, round, full, thick. Texture: soft, silky, crisp, chalky, dry. Taste: sweet (calcium, alkaline), bitter (magnesium, sulfate), salty (sodium, chloride), tart (carbon dioxide), metallic, mineral. Bubbles: fine, lively, aggressive, fading. Finish: short, long, clean, drying. Faults: chlorine, plastic, musty.

The catalogue

Every water in one list, ordered from lightest to heaviest. Each entry gives what it is, the defining minerals, how to use it, and where to find it (US first, then Europe).

Super low (under about 50)

  • Lauretana (Italy). Still, from Piedmont, in Pininfarina-designed glass. Dry residue 14 to 15; sodium about 1. Among the lightest waters in Europe; a palate cleanser and whisky water. US via importers and eBay.
  • Svalbarði (Norway). Still iceberg water, TDS about 21. A luxury curiosity at about $60 to $100 a bottle.
  • Berg (Canada). Melted Newfoundland iceberg water, TDS about 10. Specialty and online only.
  • Spa Reine (Belgium). Still, dry residue about 33 to 38. Nearly neutral; tea and whisky. Benelux supermarkets.
  • Voss Still (Norway). Artesian, TDS 22 to 44, in a tall glass bottle. Soft and round. Easy in upscale US grocers. The sparkling version has added sodium bicarbonate (sodium about 90).
  • Lurisia (Italy). Still or lightly sparkling, dry residue about 30 to 38, sodium about 2 to 3. Low-sodium diets, sushi, tea. Italian importers.
  • Saratoga (USA). Still or sparkling, in cobalt glass; now sourced in Maine and Pennsylvania. TDS about 30 to 75; calcium 7.5 to 11; sodium about 7. Light and neutral: sushi, tea, a palate cleanser. Easy: Sam's Club, H-E-B, Walmart, Target.

Low (50 to 250)

  • Icelandic Glacial (Iceland). Still or sparkling, from Ölfus Spring. TDS 62 to 69; calcium about 6; pH 8.4 to 8.7. Very light and soft: all-day drinking, a light lunch, tea, whisky. Easy: Walmart, Target.
  • Waiākea (Hawaii, USA). Volcanic, still. TDS about 82; silica about 44; pH 7.8. Soft and faintly sweet: sushi, light dishes, red wine. Easy: Whole Foods, Target.
  • Hallstein (Austria). Artesian, 214 m below Obertraun. TDS 84 to 98; calcium about 20, magnesium about 10; sodium about 0.15; pH 8.3 to 8.6. Clean and soft with some structure; daily drinking with almost no sodium, tea, wine. Tops independent contaminant testing. Brand subscription only.
  • Castle Rock (California, USA). Spring water from Mount Shasta, TDS about 100. Daily drinking and coffee. Western US.
  • Volvic (France). Volcanic, still. Dry residue 108 to 130; silica about 32. Cheap and soft. Standard grocery.
  • Antipodes (New Zealand). Still or sparkling, from volcanic ignimbrite. TDS about 130; silica about 73; sodium about 12. Silky: fine dining, wine, delicate fish. Specialty Food Source.
  • Acqua Panna (Italy). Still, Tuscany. Dry residue 142 to 188; calcium about 31. Soft and velvety: wine service and desserts. Easy: Costco, Total Wine.
  • Wattwiller (France). Still or naturally sparkling, dry residue about 155. Gentle; everyday. France.
  • Proud Source (Idaho, USA). Spring water in aluminium cans and bottles. TDS about 152 to 167; calcium 42. Salads and light fish. Easy: Costco, Safeway.
  • Aqua Carpatica, still (Romania). Carpathian spring, square glass bottle. TDS about 170 to 200; calcium 46 to 52; magnesium 13 to 17; sodium under 1; near-zero nitrate. Clean and softly sweet; an everyday base for a low-sodium diet. Hard in the US; Ocado in the UK.
  • Mountain Valley (Arkansas, USA). Still or sparkling (added gas), green glass since 1871. TDS 220 to 230; calcium 67 to 74; sodium about 3; pH 7.3 to 7.8. Smooth and lightly chalky; the best widely available American everyday water; poached fish and fruit. Easy: Costco (12 × 1 L glass about $29), Target.
  • Valser Still (Switzerland). Total about 224; calcium about 54; sodium about 0.2. Swiss importers.
  • Fiji (Fiji). Artesian, in plastic. TDS 220 to 240; silica 85 to 99. Silky; its silica softens red-wine tannin. It has been criticised over arsenic, which was not verified here. Standard grocery.
  • Kona Deep (Hawaii, USA). Desalinated deep-ocean water. TDS about 250; sodium about 83; magnesium only about 3. A curiosity.
  • Solán de Cabras (Spain). Still. TDS about 252; calcium about 60; magnesium about 27; sodium about 5. A balanced table water. Spanish importers.

Medium (250 to 800)

  • Agua de Piedra (Mexico). Still or sparkling, from the Huasteca near Monterrey, in green glass. A 2025 lab report gives TDS 330; calcium 93; magnesium 14; sodium 13; sulfate 77; pH 8.1 (a retailer claims calcium 440, so check the label). Mexican and spicy food, ranch water. Texas and Southwest wine shops.
  • Iskilde (Denmark). Still. TDS 325 to 440; calcium about 62; sodium about 71. Sweet and smooth; a sommelier favourite; fish. Specialty only.
  • Evian (France). Still. Dry residue about 345; calcium about 80; magnesium about 26; bicarbonate about 360. A dependable everyday water. Easy everywhere.
  • San Mateo (Peru). Andean spring above 3,300 m. TDS not published; calcium about 90; magnesium about 11; sodium about 32. Peru only.
  • Perrier (France). Naturally carbonated. TDS about 475; calcium about 147; pH 5.5. Large, lively bubbles; curry and aperitifs. Everywhere.
  • Galvanina (Italy). Still or sparkling (added gas), Rimini. TDS about 475 to 580; calcium 113 to 135; sodium about 35. Rounded; Italian food. Costco carries the organic sparkling.
  • Topo Chico (Mexico). Sparkling, Monterrey. TDS 490 to 630; calcium 120 to 140; sodium 33 to 41; pH about 5.5. Bold bubbles; spicy food and highballs. Easy: H-E-B, Costco, Walmart.
  • Mattoni (Czechia). Naturally sparkling or still. Bicarbonate about 528; sodium about 70. Soft bubbles; aperitifs. Eastern European grocers.

High (800 to 1,500)

  • San Pellegrino (Italy). Sparkling (added gas), sulfate-calcium. TDS about 854 to 880; calcium about 160; magnesium about 50; sulfate about 400 to 420. Fine bubbles and a dry finish; tomato, cheese, cured meat. Easy: Costco.
  • Vittel (France). Still. Dry residue 841 to 1,084; calcium 202 to 240; sulfate 306 to 400. A calcium-sulfate everyday water. France and Germany.
  • Aqua Carpatica Sparkling (Romania). Naturally sparkling. Dry residue 1,000 to 1,080; calcium 260 to 296; magnesium 77 to 89; sodium about 5. One of the few very mineral-rich waters that is almost sodium-free. UK and EU retailers.
  • Badoit (France). Naturally lightly sparkling. Dry residue 1,100 to 1,200; bicarbonate 1,250 to 1,300; sodium 150 to 180; fluoride about 1.2. Very fine bubbles; a digestif. France and Belgium.
  • Rosport Viva (Luxembourg). Calcium 180; magnesium 67; sulfate 312; about 1,150 in total. Luxembourg.
  • Three Bays (Australia). Still or sparkling, 900 m aquifer. TDS about 1,300; pH about 8.3. A rare high-mineral water that stays alkaline and still. Aqua Maestro; Fine Liquids in Europe.
  • Ferrarelle (Italy). Naturally sparkling. Dry residue about 1,320; calcium about 400 to 440. Fine bubbles that fade fast; rich and cheese dishes. Italian specialty shops.

Very high (over about 1,500)

  • Socosani (Peru). Volcanic. Still TDS about 1,751; the sparkling (added gas) has silica about 108. Smoother than Fiji; pasta. Costco in the Los Angeles area, Amazon.
  • Valser Calcium + Magnesium (Switzerland). Total about 1,826; sulfate about 988. A sulfate teaching water. Swiss importers.
  • Contrex (France). Still. Dry residue about 2,078; calcium about 468; sulfate about 1,121; sodium about 9. Chalky and dry; a calcium water with almost no sodium. France and Belgium.
  • St. Gero Heilwasser (Germany). Medicinal water. Total about 2,470; calcium about 331; magnesium about 109; bicarbonate about 1,775. Digestion and acid buffering. German supermarkets.
  • Ararat (Armenia). Sparkling bicarbonate water. TDS about 2,478; pH about 6.6. Morning hydration and roast chicken. Armenian grocers.
  • Gerolsteiner Sparkling (Germany). Naturally carbonated by volcanic gas. TDS about 2,500; calcium 348; magnesium 108; sodium 118; bicarbonate 1,816; silica about 40. Salty-chalky with bold bubbles. The best value mineral supplement in a bottle: one litre gives about a third of daily calcium and a quarter to a third of daily magnesium. Recovery, rich and fried food. Easy: Whole Foods, Walmart, Target, every German supermarket.
  • Hépar (France). Still, sulfate-magnesium. Dry residue about 2,513; calcium about 549; magnesium about 119; sulfate about 1,530; sodium about 14. A researched constipation water. Scarce even in France.
  • Crazy Water (Texas, USA). Sold in numbered strengths: No. 2 about 685 (roasted fish), No. 3 about 1,369 (barbecue and steak), No. 4 about 2,763 (the gym). Salty-bitter and dry. Texas retail and online.
  • Apollinaris Selection (Germany). Sparkling. Sodium 470; bicarbonate 1,800. Salty-soft. Germany.
  • Staatl. Fachingen (Germany). Medicinal water, lightly carbonated. Total about 2,800; bicarbonate about 1,812 to 1,854; sodium about 560 to 600. The water from the heartburn trial; note the sodium. German supermarkets.
  • Châteldon (France). Naturally sparkling. Bicarbonate about 2,075; calcium about 355; fluoride about 2.6, above the infant warning line. A fine-dining classic for rich meat. US gourmet retailers.
  • Vichy Catalan (Spain). A 60 °C thermal spring, with the spring's own gas re-added. Dry residue 2,850 to 3,052; sodium 1,080 to 1,110; bicarbonate 1,990 to 2,135; fluoride measured at 0.82. Salty and soda-like; a sommelier favourite, a classic hangover water, steak and burgers. One litre carries about half the daily sodium limit. Aqua Maestro (12 × 1 L glass about $57), Central Market in Texas.
  • Jermuk (Armenia). Thermal, naturally carbonated. Total 3,000 to 4,400; sodium 700 to 1,200. Armenian grocers; Russia suspended imports in May 2026.
  • Gerolsteiner Ursprung (Germany). The most mineralised Gerolsteiner: calcium about 590; magnesium about 167; bicarbonate about 3,144; sodium about 223. Small glasses only. German retail.
  • Borjomi (Georgia). Naturally sparkling. TDS about 5,000 to 7,500; sodium about 1,550. The gym and hangovers. Eastern European grocers.
  • ROI (Slovenia). Naturally carbonated, bottled since 1647. TDS about 7,500; magnesium about 1,300; sodium about 1,600; sulfate about 2,100. Intensely salty and bitter; laxative. Sip 100 to 250 ml. Fine Liquids in Europe.
  • Donat Mg (Slovenia). Medicinal water. TDS about 13,000; magnesium about 1,000 to 1,040; sulfate about 2,100. The most-studied constipation water. UK and EU retailers.

Engineered and luxury

Essentia (TDS about 60), Smartwater and Dasani (about 28) are purified water, some with added electrolytes: a useful contrast to natural mineral water. Liquid Death and NevasWater are fun picks for a night out. Fillico and Bling H2O are luxury bottles whose value is the decoration.

Pairing water with food and drink

The rules:

  1. Match intensity. Delicate food takes a low-TDS still water; rich, fatty or salty food takes a high-mineral or sparkling water.
  2. Carbonation cuts fat.
  3. Bicarbonate softens acidity: good next to spicy or acidic food, bad next to a fine wine.
  4. Sodium amplifies savoury and clashes with dessert.
  5. Magnesium and sulfate add bitterness, so keep them away from dark chocolate, radicchio and coffee.
  6. Silica smooths, and softens red-wine tannin.

By dish:

  • Sushi, sashimi, oysters: Saratoga, Waiākea, Antipodes, Lurisia.
  • Light fish and salads: Proud Source, Icelandic Glacial, Iskilde.
  • Poached fish and fresh fruit: Mountain Valley.
  • Roasted fish: Crazy Water No. 2.
  • Roast chicken: Ararat.
  • Pasta and tomato: Socosani, Galvanina, San Pellegrino.
  • Steak, burgers, barbecue: Vichy Catalan, Crazy Water No. 3, Gerolsteiner.
  • Fried food and tempura: Gerolsteiner, Topo Chico, Agua de Piedra.
  • Mexican and spicy food: Agua de Piedra, Topo Chico.
  • Cheese and charcuterie: Ferrarelle, Gerolsteiner, San Pellegrino.
  • Curry: Galvanina or Perrier.
  • Dessert and chocolate: Acqua Panna or Hallstein.

By drink:

  • White wine: Antipodes, Acqua Panna, Hallstein. Chlorine in tap water overpowers wine.
  • Red wine: a silica-rich water such as Fiji, Antipodes or Waiākea.
  • Whisky: a super-low still water such as Icelandic Glacial, Lauretana or Saratoga.
  • Coffee and tea: see "Water in the kitchen" above.
  • Cocktails: Topo Chico and Agua de Piedra for highballs; Gerolsteiner for a mineral-forward spritz.

Water and health

Every water hydrates, and volume matters before brand. A high-mineral water is also a real mineral source. Sodium is the main trade-off: Vichy Catalan, Borjomi, ROI, Fachingen, Apollinaris and Ursprung are all high in it. Aqua Carpatica Sparkling, Hépar and Contrex give high minerals with little sodium.

What the evidence supports, with its strength:

  • Calcium from water is well absorbed (strong, small trials). A 2006 review (Heaney) found mineral-water calcium absorbs as well as or slightly better than milk calcium.
  • Magnesium-sulfate waters relieve constipation (moderate, placebo-controlled trials). Donat Mg, 500 ml a day for 6 weeks, improved stool frequency and consistency in 106 adults (Bothe et al., 2017, published online 2015; a co-author works for the producer). Hépar, 1 L a day, gave a response within 7 days in 226 patients (Dupont et al., 2019).
  • Bicarbonate-rich water reduces heartburn (moderate, one double-blind trial). In STOMACH STILL, 1.5 L a day of Staatl. Fachingen for 6 weeks gave about 85 percent responders against 64 percent for a low-mineral placebo water (2023; some authors had ties to the producer). That dose carries about 850 to 900 mg of sodium.
  • Magnesium in drinking water and heart disease (weak and mixed). Case-control meta-analyses find about 25 percent lower heart-disease mortality where tap water is rich in magnesium; a 25-year British cohort found no protection.
  • Hydration, cognition and kidney stones (moderate). Mild dehydration impairs attention and mood, and higher water intake reduces kidney-stone recurrence.

By situation:

  • Training. Under an hour, any still water. For long or hot sessions, sweat loses about 0.5 to 1.5 g of sodium per litre: add electrolytes, or small amounts of Borjomi or Vichy Catalan. For recovery, a calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate water such as Gerolsteiner, Ararat or St. Gero.
  • Heartburn. A high-bicarbonate still water such as Fachingen or St. Gero; mind the sodium. Sparkling water can make reflux worse.
  • Constipation. ROI, Donat Mg or Hépar, starting with 250 ml in the morning.
  • After a heavy meal or a late night. A small glass of Vichy Catalan, Badoit or Gerolsteiner, then a soft still water.
  • Low-sodium diet. Hallstein, Valser Still, Aqua Carpatica, Lurisia, Mountain Valley, Icelandic Glacial; for minerals, Aqua Carpatica Sparkling.

People with kidney disease, high blood pressure or heart failure should check with a doctor before drinking high-mineral waters daily. Infant formula needs a water labelled suitable for infant food. Calcium and magnesium can block absorption of tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics and of thyroid hormone, so separate them by a few hours.

Buying and tasting flights

Where to shop. US big-box stores cover most everyday and classic waters: Costco (Mountain Valley, Topo Chico, San Pellegrino, Acqua Panna, Galvanina, Proud Source), Target and Walmart (Gerolsteiner, Evian, Icelandic Glacial, Saratoga), Whole Foods and H-E-B. Aqua Maestro (Florida) ships 30+ brands in glass, sells sampler cases and runs a monthly club. Armenian, Georgian and Eastern European grocers carry Ararat, Jermuk, Borjomi and Mattoni. In Europe, Fine Liquids sells tasting boxes of 4 to 12 bottles, and REWE, Edeka and Knuspr cover the German waters. Prices here were seen on 2026-11-07 and not confirmed live.

Tasting flights. Each teaches one contrast.

  1. TDS ladder, still: Lauretana, Saratoga, Icelandic Glacial, Mountain Valley, Evian, Vittel, Hépar or Contrex.
  2. Calcium, magnesium, sodium: Contrex, then Hépar or Donat, then Vichy Catalan. Sweet-chalky, bitter-dry, salty.
  3. Bicarbonate against sulfate: Fachingen, Vichy Catalan and Borjomi against San Pellegrino, Hépar and Valser Calcium + Magnesium.
  4. Natural against added carbonation: Gerolsteiner, Badoit and Perrier against San Pellegrino, Galvanina and Mountain Valley Sparkling.
  5. Silica: Volvic, Waiākea, Antipodes, Fiji.
  6. Low against high sodium: Hallstein, Aqua Carpatica Sparkling, Gerolsteiner, Gerolsteiner Ursprung, Vichy Catalan.
  7. Source curiosities: Svalbarði or Berg, Kona Deep, Waiākea.

For deeper study, the Fine Water Academy runs an online certification of about $2,200, and the Doemens Academy near Munich runs a 10-day water sommelier course.

Myths and contaminants

  • Alkaline water does not change body pH. Stomach acid sits at pH 1.5 to 3.5, and the lungs and kidneys hold blood pH tightly. Bicarbonate does buffer stomach acid locally, which is the heartburn effect.
  • High TDS does not mean dirty, and low TDS is not purer. TDS measures minerals, not contaminants.
  • Check "electrolyte" claims against the numbers. Kona Deep has about 3 mg/L magnesium.
  • Price is not quality. Mountain Valley, Gerolsteiner, Topo Chico and Ararat are excellent and inexpensive.
  • PFAS ("forever chemicals") turn up in some bottled and tap waters; the US EPA set tap-water limits in 2024.
  • Micro- and nanoplastics. A 2024 PNAS study estimated about 240,000 plastic particles per litre of plastic-bottled water, about 90 percent of them nanoplastics. Glass and keeping water away from heat reduce exposure.
  • Natural contaminants such as arsenic and uranium occur in some springs. Every US bottler must provide a water quality report on request: read TDS, sodium, nitrate, PFAS and arsenic, and the year of the analysis.

Storage

Store bottled water cool, dark and away from solvents or strong odours, which can migrate through plastic. Heat and sunlight increase the release of plastic particles and compounds from plastic bottles, so never leave them in a hot car. Glass keeps longest and holds carbonation best. Once opened, sparkling water loses its gas within a day even when recapped; still water is best within a few days, refrigerated. Tap water left standing for a few hours loses much of its chlorine, but not chloramine. Kettles and stockpots used with hard water build white scale, which a soak in dilute citric acid or vinegar dissolves.

Substitutions

What to reach for when this ingredient is unavailable. Each row names the use case so the swap is judged in context.

  • Hard tap water (dried beans and legumes) → filtered or low-mineral bottled water, or a pinch of baking soda in the pot Calcium and magnesium slow pectin breakdown. Soft water lets beans soften on schedule; baking soda speeds it but can turn them mushy and soapy if overdone.
  • Tap water (green, white and oolong tea) → a low-TDS still water such as Icelandic Glacial, Hallstein or Saratoga Low mineral content extracts the delicate aromatics cleanly and avoids the surface film that hard water leaves on tea.
  • Tap water (coffee brewing) → Castle Rock, or a 50/50 blend of Mountain Valley and Saratoga Aims near the SCA target of TDS 150 with moderate hardness and low alkalinity. Too much bicarbonate flattens acidity; distilled water extracts poorly.
  • Ice water (tempura and fried-food batter) → ice-cold sparkling water or plain soda water The gas expands in hot oil for a lighter, lacier crust, and the cold limits gluten. Mineral content matters less than gas and temperature.
  • Sparkling mineral water (highballs, ranch water) → Topo Chico or Agua de Piedra; plain seltzer if neither is available Bold bubbles and a touch of salinity carry through citrus and spirits. Seltzer works but tastes flatter and less mineral.
