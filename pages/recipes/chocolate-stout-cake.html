Chocolate stout cake

A small two-layer 4-inch chocolate cake where dry Irish stout replaces the liquid, melted with butter and blooming the cocoa so its roasted-barley bitterness reads as deeper chocolate. Sour cream and dark brown sugar keep the crumb dense and damp, and a lightly sweetened whipped cream-cheese frosting sits on top like the head on a pint.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Pour the stout an hour ahead, or whisk it in a bowl for a minute, to knock out most of the carbonation. Foaming stout overflows the saucepan and measures inaccurately by weight.
  • Pull the cream cheese, sour cream, and egg from the fridge 30 minutes ahead. Cold cream cheese lumps in the frosting; a cold egg seizes the warm butter in the batter.
  • The layers must cool completely, about 1 hour, before frosting. Cream-cheese frosting melts and slides on a warm cake.

Method

  1. 1
    Heat the oven to 350°F (177°C) with a rack centered. Butter two 4-inch round pans with 1 tsp / 5 g of the butter, line the bottoms with parchment, and dust the walls with a little Dutch-process cocoa instead of flour so the sides bake without a pale seam.
  2. 2
    In a small saucepan over medium-low heat, warm the flat stout (6 tbsp / 90 g) with the remaining butter (5½ tbsp / 85 g) until the butter melts and the liquid steams, about 3 minutes; do not boil, which drives off the stout's volatile hop and roast aromatics. Off heat, whisk in the Dutch-process cocoa (¼ cup + 1 tbsp / 25 g), granulated sugar (½ cup / 100 g), and dark brown sugar (3 tbsp / 35 g) until smooth and glossy. The hot liquid blooms the cocoa, and the stout's roasted-barley compounds stack onto the cocoa's so the bitterness registers as chocolate depth rather than beer. Cool 5 minutes, until warm but not hot to the touch. Technique: Blooming cocoa
  3. 3
    In a small bowl, whisk the sour cream (3½ tbsp / 50 g), the egg (1 large / 50 g), and vanilla bean paste (¾ tsp / 4 g) until smooth, then whisk into the cocoa mixture. The sour cream brings lactic acid for the soda and milk fat for a close, damp crumb.
  4. 4
    Whisk the all-purpose flour (¾ cup / 90 g), baking soda (½ tsp / 3 g), and fine sea salt (¼ tsp / 1.5 g) together, then add to the wet and whisk only until no dry streaks remain. The batter is loose and shiny. Overmixing develops gluten and tunnels the crumb.
  5. 5
    Divide evenly between the pans, about 255 g each, and tap each once to release large bubbles. Bake 24 to 28 minutes, until the centers spring back and a tester pulls out with a few moist crumbs, or the center reads 205 to 208°F (96 to 98°C). The stout's sugars and the dark brown sugar brown fast, so a dark top is normal; judge by the center, not the color.
  6. 6
    Cool in the pans 10 minutes, then turn out onto a rack and peel off the parchment. Cool completely, at least 1 hour.
  7. 7
    While the layers cool, make the frosting. Beat the room-temperature cream cheese (4 oz / 115 g) with a hand mixer until smooth and lump-free, about 30 seconds. Beat in the sifted powdered sugar (scant ½ cup / 50 g), a pinch of fine sea salt (0.5 g), and the vanilla bean paste (½ tsp / 2 g). Then, on medium speed, stream in the cold heavy cream (¼ cup / 60 g) and beat until it thickens and holds a soft peak, 1 to 2 minutes. The whipped cream lightens the cheese into a foam that looks and eats like the head on a pint; stop at soft peaks, since overbeaten cream-cheese frosting turns grainy and weeps.
  8. 8
    Level any dome off the layers with a serrated knife. Set the first layer cut-side up and spread about a third of the frosting to the edge. Set the second layer on top, cut-side down, and pile the remaining frosting on top, swooping it just over the rim so it drips slightly. Leave the sides bare: the dark crumb under a pale cap is the point.
  9. 9
    Chill 20 minutes to firm the frosting before slicing. Cut with a hot, dry knife.
  10. 10
    The Adjustment: if the layers domed and cracked, the oven runs hot or the pans were overfilled; drop to 325°F (163°C) and bake a few minutes longer. If the crumb tastes soapy or metallic, the soda was measured heavy; level the ½ tsp exactly. If the beer reads too bitter, the stout was a hoppy American style rather than a dry Irish one. If the frosting is runny, the cream cheese was tub-style or too warm; chill 15 minutes and rebeat briefly.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Texture frosting→stout ganache

    Bring 4 tbsp / 60 g heavy cream and 1 tbsp / 15 g stout to a steam, pour over 3 oz / 85 g chopped 60 to 70% dark chocolate with a pinch of salt, rest 1 minute, and stir from the center to emulsify. Cool to spreadable, about 45 minutes, and use for filling and top. Darker and more bitter; loses the pint visual.

  • Occasion scale→one 9-inch springform

    Triple every cake ingredient (3 eggs), but keep the soda at 1½ tsp / 9 g. Bake 50 to 60 minutes at 350°F (177°C) to 205°F (96°C) center. Triple the frosting and spread it on top only.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • dry Irish stout → any non-hoppy stout or porter, including oatmeal or milk stout Avoid imperial and heavily hopped American stouts: hop bitterness concentrates in the bake and does not read as chocolate. Milk stout adds a little sweetness; fine as is.
  • sour cream → full-fat Greek yogurt (same weight) Slightly tangier and leaner; the crumb is a touch less rich.
  • vanilla bean paste → vanilla extract (same amount as the paste in each phase) 1:1 by volume. Fine in a pinch, but you lose the seeds and some aromatic depth, most noticeably in the frosting, which is never heated.

Notes

What the stout is doing. Dry Irish stout is brewed with roasted unmalted barley, which carries the same pyrazine and furan roast compounds as coffee and cocoa. Used as the liquid, it plays the role coffee plays in most chocolate cakes: it amplifies the chocolate without announcing itself. The alcohol mostly bakes off; what stays is roast, a faint malt sweetness, and a mild acidity that helps the soda. Warm it gently with the butter rather than boiling, so the aromatics survive into the batter.

Why Dutch cocoa and soda together. The cocoa is alkalized, so the soda reacts against the stout, sour cream, and dark brown sugar instead. Half a teaspoon is enough for 90 g of flour. The original deep 9-inch cake is soda-heavy by design, which is fine in a tall pan but domes and cracks small layers; pulling it back keeps the 4-inch layers flat enough to stack without much trimming.

Split sugar. Dark brown sugar''s molasses holds water in the crumb and echoes the stout''s caramel-malt notes. All-granulated reads lighter and drier; all-brown turns the crumb heavy.

The frosting is the pint head. Cream cheese alone is dense and sweet; whipping cold heavy cream into it at the end aerates it into a light, tangy foam that contrasts the dense, bitter crumb. Sugar is kept low on purpose, because the lactic tang is what cuts the chocolate. Bare sides make the cake read as a pint of stout: dark body, pale cap.

Storage. Refrigerate covered up to 3 days because of the cream cheese; let slices sit 20 minutes before eating so the butter crumb softens.
