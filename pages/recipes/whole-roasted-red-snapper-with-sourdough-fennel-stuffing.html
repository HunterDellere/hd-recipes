Recipe

Whole roasted red snapper with sourdough fennel stuffing

Two whole red snappers, scored and dry-brined, packed with a sourdough stuffing built on brown butter, sweated fennel and celery, melted anchovy, lemon zest, and a fistful of parsley and dill, then roasted hot at 220 °C until the skin blisters and the flesh at the spine reads 57 °C. Finished at the table with a brown-butter lemon caper sauce. It is the stuffing note of a traditional Thanksgiving carried by fish instead of turkey: crisp-edged bread soaked in butter and fish juices, and a centerpiece that carves in minutes.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • The day before: cut the sourdough into 2 cm cubes and leave them spread on a sheet pan, uncovered, overnight to stale. Dry bread absorbs the brown butter and fish juices without collapsing into paste. If you forget, toast the cubes at 150 °C / 300 °F for 20 to 25 minutes until dry but not colored.
  • The stuffing can be fully mixed the morning of and refrigerated in a covered bowl. Stuff the fish only immediately before it goes into the oven. A stuffed raw fish that sits warm is a bacterial incubator, and the bread turns soggy.
  • Shared oven: the fish roasts at 220 °C / 425 °F, the same temperature as the rack of lamb and the roasted carrots, so they can share the oven on separate racks. Give the fish the lower-middle rack, because two fish on one sheet pan block airflow, and keep the lamb above.
  • Buy fish that fit your pan. Two 1.1 to 1.4 kg snappers fit diagonally on a standard 46 x 33 cm (18 x 13 inch) half sheet pan. Ask the fishmonger to scale, gut, and remove the gills, and to leave the head and tail on.

Step 4 · Brown-butter lemon caper sauce

  1. 85 g unsalted butter, for the sauce

Method

  1. 1
    Dry-brine the fish. Pat both fish (2500 g total) bone-dry inside and out, then cut 3 diagonal slashes 1 cm deep on each side, down to but not through the bone. Season the outside, the slashes, and the cavity with the 20 g / 2 tbsp kosher salt. Lay the fish on a rack over a sheet pan and refrigerate uncovered for 30 to 60 minutes. The salt dissolves into surface moisture, diffuses into the flesh, and loosens the myosin so the flesh holds water during roasting. The rack lets the skin dry so it blisters instead of steaming. Technique: Dry brining
  2. 2
    Heat the oven. Set one rack in the lower-middle position and heat the oven to 220 °C / 425 °F. Line a half sheet pan with parchment paper. Butter a small 1 L (1 quart) baking dish for the extra stuffing.
  3. 3
    Brown the butter for the stuffing. Melt the 115 g / 8 tbsp butter in a wide 30 cm / 12-inch skillet over medium heat. Swirl as it foams. After 4 to 5 minutes the milk solids sink and turn amber, and the butter smells of toasted hazelnut. Pull the pan off the heat at the first deep-amber color, because the solids go from brown to burnt in about 20 seconds. Technique: Brown butter (beurre noisette)
  4. 4
    Sweat the aromatics and melt the anchovy. Return the pan to medium-low heat. Add the 300 g / 1 bulb diced fennel, 120 g / 2 ribs diced celery, 100 g / 2 shallots diced shallot, and 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt. The salt draws water from the vegetables so they soften without browning. Sweat, stirring with a wooden spoon, for 8 to 10 minutes until the fennel is translucent and tender. Add the 15 g / 4 cloves minced garlic and the 15 g / 4 to 5 fillets chopped anchovy, and stir for 1 minute. The anchovy dissolves into the fat and becomes a savory depth (free glutamate and inosinate), not a fishy flavor. Technique: Sweating
  5. 5
    Deglaze and moisten. Pour in the 120 g / ½ cup dry white wine and simmer for 2 minutes to drive off the raw alcohol, scraping the pan with the wooden spoon. Add the 120 g / ½ cup seafood stock and take the pan off the heat. Technique: Deglazing
  6. 6
    Build the stuffing. In a large bowl, combine the 300 g / 6 cups dried sourdough cubes with the hot vegetable mixture and every bit of the butter. Add the 6 g / 2 tbsp lemon zest, 15 g / ½ cup chopped parsley, 8 g / ¼ cup chopped dill, 5 g / 2 tbsp chopped fennel fronds, and 2 g / 1 tsp black pepper. Fold with the wooden spoon until the liquid is absorbed. The cubes should be damp through but still hold their shape. Taste a cube: it should be seasoned enough to eat as it is, because the fish juices dilute it in the oven. If it tastes flat, add salt a pinch at a time.
  7. 7
    Stuff the fish. Lay the fish diagonally on the lined sheet pan, head to tail. Fill each cavity loosely with stuffing, about 250 g / 2 cups per fish. Do not pack it: tight stuffing does not heat through before the fish overcooks. Put the remaining stuffing in the buttered baking dish.
  8. 8
    Oil and top. Rub the 30 g / 2 tbsp olive oil over the skin of both fish. Tuck 2 or 3 of the 120 g / 1 lemon slices into the slashes and lay the rest over the top of each fish.
  9. 9
    Roast. Put the sheet pan on the lower-middle rack and the dish of extra stuffing beside it or on the rack above. Roast for 30 to 40 minutes. Start checking at 28 minutes. Insert an instant-read thermometer into the thickest part of the fish, just behind the head and close to the spine. Pull the fish at 57 °C / 135 °F. The visual cue: the eye has turned fully white, the dorsal fin pulls out with little resistance, and the flesh in the deepest slash is opaque and flakes when pressed. Probe the center of the stuffing too; it must read 63 °C / 145 °F. The extra stuffing is done when its top is crisp and golden, after 25 to 30 minutes. Technique: High-heat dry roasting
  10. 10
    Rest and make the sauce. Rest the fish on the pan for 5 minutes; carryover heat brings the flesh to about 60 °C. Meanwhile, melt the 85 g / 6 tbsp butter in a small saucepan over medium heat and cook it to amber, 3 to 4 minutes. Add the 25 g / 2 tbsp capers; they sputter and crisp in 30 seconds. Take the pan off the heat and add the 45 g / 3 tbsp lemon juice. It foams hard, so stand back. Stir in the 5 g / 2 tbsp chopped parsley. Taste: the sauce should be sharp enough to make you blink, because it seasons rich fish and buttery bread at once. If it is flat, add more lemon juice. Technique: Brown butter (beurre noisette)
  11. 11
    Carve and serve at the table. Bring the fish to the table on the sheet pan or a warm platter, so guests see the whole fish. Scoop the stuffing out of each cavity into a bowl. With a table knife or fish spatula, cut along the line that runs down the middle of the side, from head to tail. Then cut behind the head and in front of the tail. Lift the top fillet away from the bones in two or three pieces. Lift the tail, and the whole skeleton peels up with the head attached. Discard it. The bottom fillet is now free. Spoon the hot brown-butter caper sauce over the fillets and the stuffing, and serve the extra stuffing alongside.
Safety
  • snapper pulled at 57 °C / 135 °F (below the USDA published-safe 63 °C / 145 °F for fish) Restaurant practice pulls lean whole fish at just-set so the flake stays moist; carryover during the 5-minute rest adds 2 to 3 degrees. Cooks who are pregnant, immunocompromised, very young, or elderly should roast to 63 °C. The stuffing inside the cavity must also reach 63 °C / 145 °F, because it has absorbed raw fish juices; probe its center separately. Reference →

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Occasion whole fish→two thick skin-on fillets with the stuffing baked separately

    Use 2 skin-on snapper, striped bass, or halibut fillets of 600 to 700 g each, at least 2.5 cm thick. Salt them at 1% of their weight 30 minutes ahead. Bake all the stuffing in a 2 L (8 x 8 inch) buttered dish at 220 °C / 425 °F for 25 to 30 minutes until crisp on top. Lay the fillets skin-side up on an oiled, parchment-lined sheet pan and roast on the rack above for 12 to 15 minutes, to 55 °C / 131 °F. Serve the fillets over spoonfuls of stuffing with the same brown-butter caper sauce. There are no bones to deal with at the table, but the stuffing loses the fish juices that a cavity gives it, so moisten it with an extra 60 g / ¼ cup stock.

  • Regional stuffing direction→Gulf Coast crab stuffing

    Reduce the sourdough to 200 g / 4 cups and fold 225 g / 8 oz picked lump crab meat into the finished stuffing, with 1 beaten egg to bind it. Omit the dill and add 2 g / ½ tsp cayenne. The crab makes the dish richer and more expensive; the stuffing must still reach 63 °C / 145 °F in the cavity.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • whole red snapper → branzino Branzino are smaller, about 500 to 700 g each, so use 4 fish and about 125 g / 1 cup stuffing per cavity. Roast at the same 220 °C / 425 °F for 20 to 25 minutes. Fit them on two sheet pans.
  • whole red snapper → black sea bass or striped bass Both have a similar firm white flake. Match the weight (1.1 to 1.4 kg each) and the timing is the same. Striped bass is often larger; one 2.5 kg fish takes 45 to 55 minutes.
  • seafood stock or low-sodium vegetable stock → water plus 5 g / 1 tsp white miso The miso replaces the savory body that stock gives. Whisk it into the water before adding it to the pan.
  • dry white wine → more stock plus 15 g / 1 tbsp white wine vinegar The vinegar supplies the acid the wine would have given.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → sourdough bread, staled make it yourself An open-crumb, well-fermented country loaf stales into cubes that drink up butter and fish juices yet keep their edges; supermarket sourdough often turns to paste.
  • → seafood stock or low-sodium vegetable stock make it yourselfstub Ask the fishmonger for the snapper heads and frames from other fish; a 40-minute stock gives the stuffing a gelatin-rich, sea-sweet base that carton stock cannot.

Notes

Why snapper. Red snapper has firm, lean, large-flaked white flesh and a thick skin that holds the fish together when stuffed and roasted. A 1.1 to 1.4 kg fish is the size where the cavity holds a meaningful amount of stuffing, and where the flesh reaches 57 °C at about the same time the stuffing reaches 63 °C. Much larger fish overcook at the tail before the center is done; smaller fish hold too little stuffing to matter.

Why the stuffing works inside a fish. As the fish roasts, its juices and the melted collagen from the skin and bones drain into the cavity. The dry bread absorbs them. That is the same mechanism that makes stuffing cooked inside a turkey taste better than dressing baked in a dish. The extra dish of stuffing gives the crisp, browned texture that the cavity cannot, so the table gets both.

The seasoning curve. Salt goes in at three points: the dry brine seasons the flesh, the salt in the sweat draws water from the vegetables and seasons the stuffing base, and the capers and anchovy carry salt into the sauce and the stuffing. Taste the stuffing before it goes into the fish; it should be fully seasoned on its own.

Acid and fat balance. The stuffing carries 115 g of brown butter and the sauce another 85 g. Lemon zest in the stuffing, lemon slices on the fish, and lemon juice and capers in the sauce keep that fat from reading heavy. If the plate feels rich, the fix is more lemon at the table, not less butter.

Make ahead. Stale the bread the day before. Make the stuffing up to 6 hours ahead and refrigerate it covered; let it stand 20 minutes at room temperature before stuffing so the center of the cavity heats in time. Never stuff the fish ahead.
