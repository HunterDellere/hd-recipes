Swedish meatballs (köttbullar) with cream gravy

Small, tender beef and pork meatballs bound with a milk-soaked breadcrumb panade and seasoned with allspice and white pepper, browned in butter, then served in a pan gravy of beef broth and cream. Quick-pickled pressed cucumber (pressgurka) and lingonberry preserves supply the sweet-sour contrast that makes the plate.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Make the pressgurka first: it needs at least 30 minutes in its brine to taste pickled, and it is better after an hour or two.
  • If serving with mashed or boiled potatoes, start them before shaping the meatballs; they take about as long as shaping and browning together.
  • Chill the shaped meatballs 15 minutes if your kitchen is warm. Firm, cold meatballs keep their round shape in the pan.

Step 1 · Pressgurka (pressed cucumber)

  1. 350 g English cucumber, sliced paper thin on a mandoline, skin on — (1 cucumber)
  2. 3 g kosher salt (for the cucumber)
  3. 35 ml distilled white vinegar (5%)
  4. 25 g granulated sugar
  5. 25 ml water (for the brine)
  6. 5 g flat-leaf parsley, finely chopped

Step 2 · Meatballs

  1. 10 g unsalted butter (for the onion)
  2. 100 g yellow onion, grated on the large holes of a box grater — (1 small)
  3. 30 g plain dry breadcrumbs
  4. 100 ml whole milk
  5. 250 g ground beef (80/20)
  6. 250 g ground pork
  7. 50 g egg — (1 large)
  8. 7 g kosher salt (for the meat)
  9. 1 g ground allspice
  10. 1 g white pepper, ground
  11. 30 g unsalted butter (for browning)
  12. 15 g neutral oil

Step 3 · Cream gravy

  1. 15 g all-purpose flour
  2. 300 ml beef broth, low-sodium or unsalted homemade
  3. 150 ml heavy cream
  4. 10 g soy sauce

Step 4 · To serve

  1. 120 g lingonberry preserves (lingonsylt)

Method

  1. 1
    Pressgurka. Toss the 350 g / 1 thinly sliced cucumber with the 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt in a glass bowl. Set a plate on top and weigh it down with a heavy jar for 15 minutes; the salt draws out water so the slices turn supple and take up the brine instead of diluting it. Meanwhile, stir the 35 ml / 2⅓ tbsp white vinegar, 25 g / 2 tbsp sugar and 25 ml / 1⅔ tbsp water together until the sugar dissolves. Squeeze the cucumber firmly by handfuls, discard the liquid, and toss the slices with the brine and the 5 g / 2 tbsp chopped parsley. Cover and refrigerate at least 30 minutes.
  2. 2
    Panade and onion. Melt the 10 g / 2 tsp onion butter in a small pan over medium-low heat and cook the 100 g / 1 small grated onion with its juice for 5 minutes, until soft and no longer sharp; let it cool. In a large bowl, stir the 30 g / ⅓ cup breadcrumbs into the 100 ml whole milk and leave 5 minutes to swell into a paste. The soaked crumbs hold milk inside the meatball as it cooks, so the texture stays soft and juicy instead of tightening.
  3. 3
    Mix. Add the cooled onion, the 250 g ground beef, 250 g ground pork, 50 g / 1 egg, 7 g / 2⅓ tsp kosher salt, 1 g / ½ tsp allspice and 1 g / ½ tsp white pepper to the panade. Mix with one hand for about 1 minute, just until evenly combined and slightly sticky. Mix too little and the meatballs crumble; knead much longer and they turn bouncy and dense. To check seasoning, fry a pinch of the mixture, taste, and adjust before shaping.
  4. 4
    Shape. With hands dipped in cold water, roll the mixture into small balls about 3 cm / 1¼ in across, about 20 g each; you should get around 36. Set them on a parchment-lined tray. Small meatballs are the Swedish standard: more browned surface per bite and they cook through quickly.
  5. 5
    Brown. Heat the oven to 90 °C / 200 °F to keep the cooked meatballs warm. In a wide skillet over medium heat, heat half of the 15 g neutral oil and half of the 30 g butter (about 7 g oil and 15 g / 1 tbsp butter) until the butter foams. Add half the meatballs in a single layer with space between them. Brown 8 minutes, shaking the pan every minute or so to roll them, until deep brown all over. Move to an ovenproof dish in the oven and brown the second half in the remaining 8 g oil and 15 g / 1 tbsp butter. Do not wash the pan; the browned bits are the base of the gravy. Technique: Maillard sear
  6. 6
    Gravy. Pour off all but about 20 g / 1½ tbsp of the fat. Over medium heat, whisk the 15 g / 2 tbsp flour into the fat and cook 1 minute, until it smells toasty. Whisk in the 300 ml / 1¼ cups beef broth gradually, scraping up the browned bits, then the 150 ml / ⅔ cup cream and the 10 g / 2 tsp soy sauce. Simmer 5 minutes, whisking, until the gravy coats a spoon. Taste and add a pinch of kosher salt and white pepper if needed; the soy and broth carry salt, so taste before adding any. Technique: Deglazing
  7. 7
    Finish. Return the meatballs to the gravy and simmer gently 2 minutes, turning them to coat. A meatball cut open should be cooked through with no pink, and read 71 °C / 160 °F in the center. Serve with potatoes, about 30 g / 2 tbsp of the 120 g lingonberry preserves per plate, and the drained pressgurka from the 350 g cucumber on the side.
Safety
  • Ground beef and pork mixture Ground meat carries surface bacteria mixed throughout, so cook the meatballs to 71 °C / 160 °F in the center. At this size they get there during browning plus the 2 minutes in the simmering gravy; check one with an instant-read thermometer. Reference →

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Regional gravy style→darker gravy with veal stock and a little lingonberry

    Use veal stock instead of beef broth and whisk 10 g / 2 tsp lingonberry preserves or redcurrant jelly into the gravy with the cream. Many Swedish households sweeten the gravy slightly this way; it rounds the soy and reads more like a restaurant gräddsås.

  • Occasion holiday table→julbord meatballs

    Roll the meatballs smaller, about 12 g each, brown them and serve without gravy, warm or at room temperature, alongside the rest of the Christmas buffet. Allspice can go up to 1.5 g / ¾ tsp.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • ground beef and ground pork → ground veal and pork A common Swedish blend; milder and more tender than beef. Use the same 50/50 split.
  • lingonberry preserves → whole-berry cranberry sauce Cranberry is the closest relative and works well; it is sweeter and less astringent, so use a little less.
  • distilled white vinegar → Swedish ättika (12%) Use 15 ml / 1 tbsp ättika and increase the water to 45 ml / 3 tbsp; this is the Swedish 1-2-3 brine (1 part ättika, 2 parts sugar, 3 parts water) at one-cucumber scale.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → beef broth, low-sodium or unsalted homemade make it yourself Homemade broth carries gelatin that gives the gravy body with less flour, and a deeper beef flavor than carton broth.
  • → plain dry breadcrumbs make it yourself Dried leftover white bread makes a clean, unseasoned crumb; store-bought crumbs are often seasoned or stale-tasting.

Notes

The panade. Breadcrumbs soaked in milk sit between the meat proteins and keep them from bonding tightly as they cook, and they hold water that would otherwise run out. That is the difference between a soft, fine-textured Swedish meatball and a firm, springy one. Keep the mixing brief for the same reason.

Allspice and white pepper. Allspice is the defining spice of köttbullar: warm and clove-like, but in a quantity you should notice only as a background. White pepper gives heat without black flecks. Grind allspice fresh from whole berries if you can; ground allspice loses its aroma within months.

Umami and acid. The browned fond, the beef broth and the soy sauce in the gravy supply glutamate (soy in cream gravy is ordinary Swedish practice, sold there as kinesisk soja). The acid lives on the side of the plate: the vinegar in the pressgurka and the tart lingonberries. Each forkful is meant to combine meat, gravy and one or the other, which is why neither is optional.

Lingonberries. In the US, Swedish lingonberry preserves are sold at IKEA food markets, Scandinavian delis and many large supermarkets; frozen lingonberries are rare outside Scandinavian shops. If you find frozen berries, stir 200 g berries with 70 g granulated sugar until dissolved (rårörda lingon, stirred lingonberries) and refrigerate; it keeps a week.

Pressgurka timing. It is crisp and sharp after 30 minutes and softer and sweeter after a day. It keeps 3 days refrigerated in a glass container.
