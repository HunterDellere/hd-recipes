Recipe

Ma la xiang guo

The Chongqing dry-pot stir-fry: every component is cooked on its own first (lotus root blanched, taro steamed then fried, shrimp passed through oil, flounder in a potato-starch shell, tofu skin rolls blistered, shimeji dry-seared), then the whole set is tossed through a beef-tallow base of bloomed doubanjiang, douchi, erjingtiao chilies, and red Sichuan peppercorn. No broth, no sauce pooling in the bowl; the fat carries the flavor and each piece keeps its own texture. Green Sichuan peppercorn and black vinegar go in at the end so the má and the acid both arrive fresh. Numbing, hot, savory, and layered.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Steam 300 g / 1½ cups jasmine rice first. The dish is intensely seasoned and needs the neutral rice; it also cannot wait once the final toss starts.
  • Tofu skin rolls (fresh or frozen yuba rolls, often sold as strips): if frozen, thaw overnight in the refrigerator. They need no soaking. Only dried fuzhu sticks need the 3-hour soak, and that swap is described under substitutions.
  • The frying happens in one pool of oil in one sequence, ordered by temperature and by how much each item dirties the oil: shrimp, then taro, then tofu skin rolls, then battered flounder. Have a rack over a sheet pan and an instant-read thermometer beside the wok before the oil goes in.
  • Wok size decides the final toss. A 30 to 33 cm wok cannot toss 1.7 kg of ingredients; the bottom steams while the top stays cold. Plan on two rounds: make the full base, lift half of it into a bowl, toss half of every component, plate, return the reserved base, and toss the second half. A 36 cm or larger wok on a strong burner can do it in one.
  • Grind the green Sichuan peppercorn in the last 10 minutes, not earlier. Hydroxy-alpha-sanshool, the numbing compound, is volatile and fades within an hour of grinding.
  • Wear gloves to peel the taro. Raw taro is full of calcium oxalate needles that itch and burn bare skin; cooking destroys them.

Method

  1. 1
    Peel and slice the 300 g lotus root 3 mm thick, dropping the slices straight into 1 L cold water with the 15 g / 1 tbsp distilled white vinegar; the acid slows the polyphenol oxidase that browns cut lotus. Bring 1.5 L water with the 10 g / 2 tsp kosher salt to a rolling boil, drain the lotus, blanch 90 seconds, then shock in ice water. Drain and spread on a towel to dry; wet slices spit in the wok and refuse to take the base. Technique: Ice bath shock
  2. 2
    Wearing gloves, peel the 340 g taro and cut it into 12 mm batons. Steam 12 minutes until a skewer slides in with slight resistance at the center. Spread on a plate to cool uncovered so the surface dries; a dry surface is what blisters in the oil.
  3. 3
    While the taro steams, toss the 340 g shrimp with the 1.5 g / ¼ tsp baking soda and 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt and rest 15 minutes. The alkaline surface raises the pH of the outer muscle so the myofibrils do not contract as tightly under heat, which is the snap in restaurant shrimp. Rinse under cold water, then dry completely between towels.
  4. 4
    Cut the 340 g flounder into 4 cm pieces, season with the 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt, and rest 10 minutes, then blot. Whisk the 30 g egg white, 32 g / ¼ cup potato starch, 5 g / 1 tsp Shaoxing wine, and 1 g / ¼ tsp fine sea salt until the batter falls from the whisk in a continuous sheet. If it drips like water, add potato starch a teaspoon at a time; if it clumps, a teaspoon of cold water. Keep it cold and re-whisk before each batch, since the starch settles. Technique: Velveting
  5. 5
    Heat the empty, dry wok over the highest flame until a drop of water skates and vanishes. Add the 200 g shimeji in a single layer and leave them untouched 2 minutes, then toss and cook 2 minutes more until the edges are deeply browned and they smell faintly of shellfish. Only now add the 1.5 g / ½ tsp kosher salt; salting earlier pulls water out and the mushrooms steam instead of sear. Lift to a bowl. Technique: Maillard sear
  6. 6
    Pour the 320 g peanut oil into the wok and bring it to 150°C / 300°F. Slide in half of the shrimp (170 g) and stir gently 40 seconds until they turn opaque and curl to a loose C; an instant-read thermometer in the thickest one reads 49°C / 120°F. Lift to a rack over a sheet pan and repeat with the second half of the 340 g shrimp. They are underdone on purpose; the final toss finishes them.
  7. 7
    Raise the oil to 170°C / 340°F. Fry the 340 g steamed taro in two batches, about 3 minutes each, until the edges blister and go golden; lift to the rack. Then fry the 250 g tofu skin rolls in two batches, 60 to 90 seconds each, until the surface pulls taut and blisters at the edges; lift. Let the oil recover to temperature between every batch, and skim out anything that floats. Technique: Deep-frying
  8. 8
    Drop the oil to 165°C / 330°F. Drag each piece of the 340 g salted flounder through the batter, let the excess sheet off, and lay it in away from you, 5 or 6 pieces per batch in the 320 g oil. Fry 90 seconds on the first face and 60 seconds on the second, until the shell is pale gold and crisp and the center reads 54°C / 130°F. Drain on the rack. Skim the batter debris between batches, since it burns and turns the oil bitter. Technique: Batter frying (wet-batter shallow-fry for jeon, pajeon, fritters)
  9. 9
    Pour the 320 g frying oil through a fine sieve into a heatproof container. Measure 40 g / 3 tbsp of it back into the wok and set the rest aside for another day. Wipe the rim of the wok.
  10. 10
    Add the 40 g / 3 tbsp beef tallow to the 40 g reserved oil and heat over medium until it reaches 120°C / 250°F, a bare shimmer. Add the 2 g star anise, 3 g cassia bark, 2 bay leaves, 3 g / 1 tsp fennel seed, and the black cardamom husk. Swirl 30 seconds until the fat smells of the spices; their aromatics are lipid-soluble and only the fat can pull them out. Technique: Blooming aromatics
  11. 11
    Add the 40 g / 2½ tbsp doubanjiang and 12 g / 1 tbsp douchi. Fry over medium-low, stirring constantly, 3 minutes until the fat runs deep red and the paste smells toasted rather than raw and briny. This is the step that decides the dish; the color and depth of doubanjiang live in fat-soluble compounds that a quick stir never releases. Do not let it scorch or the base turns acrid. Technique: Red oil rendering
  12. 12
    Add the 30 g ginger coins, 40 g smashed garlic, and the 40 g scallion whites. Stir 60 seconds until the garlic edges take a little color and the ginger is fragrant.
  13. 13
    Add the 15 g dried erjingtiao chilies and 6 g / 1 tbsp red Sichuan peppercorns. Stir 30 to 45 seconds, keeping the fat under 160°C / 320°F, until the chilies darken one shade and smell toasty. They go in last, after the paste, so they cannot scorch during the long doubanjiang fry; if they turn black, the whole base tastes bitter. Technique: Dry-toasting (grains, spices, seeds, dried chilies)
  14. 14
    Add the 8 g / 2 tsp crushed rock sugar, 30 g / 2 tbsp Shaoxing wine, 16 g / 1 tbsp light soy sauce, and 20 g / 1 tbsp oyster sauce. Bubble 60 seconds until glossy and thick enough to cling to a spoon. Taste a drop: it should be intensely salty, sweet, and savory on its own, because it is seasoning 1.7 kg of ingredients. If your wok is under 36 cm, lift half of the base into a bowl now.
  15. 15
    Raise the heat to high. Add the 340 g fried taro, 300 g blanched lotus root, 250 g fried tofu skin rolls, and 200 g seared shimeji (or half of each, if working in two rounds) and toss 60 seconds until every piece is glossed red. Fold in the 340 g shrimp and 340 g fried flounder from underneath, lifting with the wok spatula rather than stirring, so the crust stays intact. Add the 4 g / 2 tsp green Sichuan peppercorns, half ground and half whole, and toss 20 seconds more.
  16. 16
    Off the heat, pour the 10 g / 2 tsp Chinkiang black vinegar down the hot wall of the wok so it flashes and its harshest volatiles blow off before it hits the food, then drizzle in the 9 g / 2 tsp toasted sesame oil. Turn out onto a platter. If working in two rounds, return the reserved base to the wok, bring it back to high, and repeat with the second half of everything, splitting the green peppercorn, vinegar, and sesame oil between the rounds.
  17. 17
    Scatter the 9 g / 1 tbsp toasted sesame seeds, 8 g cilantro, and the reserved greens from the 40 g scallions over the platter. Taste one piece of each component: the order should be má tingle first, then chili warmth, then a deep salty-savory base, then a bright vinegar edge at the end. If it reads flat, a pinch of kosher salt and a few more drops of vinegar; if it reads harsh, a pinch more crushed rock sugar. Serve immediately with the 300 g steamed jasmine rice; the crusts soften within 10 minutes.
Safety
  • Shrimp pulled from the 150°C oil at 49°C / 120°F internal, below the USDA published-safe 145°F for seafood The oil pass is deliberately incomplete: the shrimp finish in the final high-heat toss, which carries them to opaque and snappy, and shrimp doneness is texture-driven rather than pasteurization-driven because their bacterial load is lower than groundfish. Buy from a counter that keeps them on ice and cook within a day. Anyone pregnant, immunocompromised, very young, or elderly should hold the shrimp in the oil until fully opaque and curled tight, about 90 seconds, accepting a firmer bite. Reference →
  • Flounder fried to 54°C / 130°F at the center, below the USDA published-safe 145°F for fin fish Flounder is a thin, lean flatfish that turns chalky past 60°C. The 54°C call is the just-set point where the flake is moist, and the fish takes further heat in the final toss. It depends on fresh, well-handled fish from a trusted counter. Cooks who are pregnant, immunocompromised, very young, or elderly, or working with fish of uncertain freshness, should fry 30 seconds longer per face to an opaque, fully flaking 63°C / 145°F. Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Protein protein→the Chongqing snack-shop set: luncheon meat, fish balls, and thin-sliced beef

    Drop the flounder. Fry 200 g sliced luncheon meat and 200 g halved fish balls in the 170°C oil until blistered, and velvet 250 g thin-sliced flank steak with 1 tsp potato starch, ½ tsp light soy, and 1 tsp Shaoxing, passing it through the 150°C oil for 30 seconds with the shrimp. The dish reads heavier, saltier, and closer to what a Chongqing xiang guo counter serves.

  • Texture vegetables→cauliflower, celery, and wood ear

    Replace the taro with 300 g cauliflower florets fried at 170°C for 2 minutes, and add 100 g rehydrated wood ear and 100 g celery cut on the bias with the lotus root in the final toss. Lighter and crunchier; the celery and wood ear take the base without absorbing it, so the dish stays cleaner.

  • Heat heat→milder, without losing the má

    Halve the chilies to 8 g and cut the doubanjiang to 30 g, adding 10 g light soy to hold the salt. Keep the full Sichuan peppercorn. The numbing tingle is the identity of the dish; the chili heat is the adjustable part.

  • Regional regional→Beijing street style with cumin

    Toast 3 g / 1½ tsp cumin seeds dry, grind coarse, and add with the green Sichuan peppercorn in the final toss. Northern xiang guo counters lean on cumin the way Xinjiang lamb skewers do; it pushes the dish smoky and resinous.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • tofu skin rolls (fresh or frozen) → dried fuzhu sticks, 85 g Soak 3 hours in cold water with a plate on top to keep them submerged, until pliable with no chalky core. Drain, cut on the bias into 5 cm lengths, and pat very dry before frying; wet fuzhu spits violently in oil. Fry the same 60 to 90 seconds.
  • potato starch → cornstarch, same weight The crust is slightly denser and goes soft a few minutes sooner in the final toss. Fry to the same color and temperature.
  • beef tallow → lard, or 40 g more of the strained peanut oil Lard keeps the rendered-animal-fat richness; peanut oil alone reads leaner and more like a stir-fry than a dry pot. Tallow is worth finding.
  • dried erjingtiao chilies → Mexican guajillo, same weight Similar fruit-forward, moderate-heat profile. Chiles de árbol are much hotter; use a third of the weight if that is what you have.
  • flounder fillet → cod or haddock cut into 3 cm pieces Thicker, flakier, and more forgiving in the toss. Fry 30 seconds longer per face to the same 54°C.
  • green Sichuan peppercorns → red Sichuan peppercorns, same weight Rounder and less citrusy, but the finishing má still lands. Grind half just before use, same as the green.
  • black cardamom (cao guo) → omit There is no substitute for its smoky, camphor note. Green cardamom is floral and sweet and pulls the base toward South Asia; leave it out rather than swap it.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → oyster sauce make it yourselfstub Fresh oyster liquor reduced with soy has a cleaner brininess and no caramel color or starch, and you control the sugar against the rock sugar already in the base.

Notes

Why tallow. Chongqing hot pot is built on beef tallow, and xiang guo is hot pot with the broth removed. Tallow is solid at room temperature and clings to every surface it coats, so the base stays on the ingredients instead of pooling on the platter, and its rendered-beef flavor is the register that separates a dry pot from an ordinary spicy stir-fry. A 1:1 blend with the strained frying oil keeps it fluid in the wok while retaining the flavor.

Why everything is cooked separately first. Taro, lotus root, shrimp, and battered fish have nothing in common thermally: taro needs 15 minutes of moist heat and then a blistering fry, shrimp are done in 40 seconds, fish crust needs oil at 165°C, and lotus root wants a 90-second blanch to lose its raw starch while staying crisp. Cooking them together in the base would mean choosing one of those and ruining the rest. The final toss lasts under two minutes and is only there to coat and reheat.

Why the frying order. Shrimp go first at the lowest temperature because they need the cleanest oil and the gentlest heat. Taro and tofu skin rolls shed almost nothing and tolerate 170°C. Battered flounder goes last because it sheds starch that would burn under everything after it. Straining the oil at the end and reusing 40 g in the base carries a little of the shrimp and fish flavor into the fat.

Why potato starch in the batter. Potato starch has larger granules and more amylose than cornstarch, gelatinizes at a lower temperature, and fries to a thinner, glassier shell that stays crisp longer once it meets the wet base. Cornstarch works, but the crust softens sooner in the toss.

Why the chilies go in after the paste. Doubanjiang needs three minutes of frying to release its color and depth; dried chilies scorch in one. Frying the paste first and adding the chilies and red peppercorn for the last 30 to 45 seconds gets both to their best point, and the paste-loaded wok keeps the fat temperature down while they toast.

Why two kinds of peppercorn. Red Sichuan peppercorn in the fat early gives a rounded, warm má that saturates the base. Green peppercorn ground at the end gives a sharper, citrus-edged tingle that fades within an hour of grinding, so it goes in during the final toss and half of it stays whole for texture.

Why the vinegar goes down the wok wall off heat. Chinkiang vinegar has harsh low-boiling volatiles and a fruity, malty body. Hitting the hot metal flashes off the harsh fraction and leaves the body, and doing it off heat keeps the aromatics that survive from cooking away before the dish reaches the table.
