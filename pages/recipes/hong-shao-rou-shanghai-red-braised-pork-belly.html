Hong shao rou (Shanghai red-braised pork belly)

Skin-on pork belly blanched clean, glazed in a rock-sugar caramel, then braised low for 90 minutes in Shaoxing wine with light and dark soy. The liquid is boiled down at the end until it coats every cube in a sticky mahogany lacquer and the fat trembles under a chopstick.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • The braise is 90 minutes of mostly passive simmering followed by a 10 to 15 minute reduction that needs your full attention. Plan the rest of the meal around that last stretch.
  • Start jasmine rice during the final 30 minutes of the braise so it is resting when the reduction finishes. The lacquer sets as it cools, so the pork goes to the table the moment it is glazed.
  • Crush the rock sugar and measure the wine, both soys, and the aromatics before you start the caramel. Rock sugar goes from amber to burnt in under a minute, and there is no time to measure once it turns.

What to prep, in order 4 phases ▾

Blanch

  1. 800 g skin-on pork belly, one slab with even layers of fat and lean; scrape the skin with a knife and pluck any stray hairs
  2. 10 g fresh ginger, sliced for blanching, 3 thin coins — (½-inch piece)
  3. 15 g Shaoxing wine (for blanching)

Caramel

  1. 15 g neutral oil (rice bran, canola, or grapeseed)
  2. 45 g rock sugar, crushed, crushed with a rolling pin or mortar to pieces no bigger than a pea

Braise

  1. 20 g fresh ginger, sliced for the braise, 6 thin coins, lightly smashed — (1-inch piece)
  2. 40 g scallions, cut into 6 cm lengths, white parts lightly smashed — (4 scallions)
  3. 2 g star anise optional — (1 pod; a background note only, many Shanghai cooks leave it out)
  4. 150 g Shaoxing wine
  5. 35 g light soy sauce
  6. 15 g dark soy sauce
  7. 450 ml hot water
  8. 1 g kosher salt

To serve

  1. 600 g cooked jasmine rice

Method

  1. 1
    Cut the 800 g pork belly into 3 cm cubes, each with skin, fat, and lean. Even sizes matter more than exact sizes: every cube needs to finish tender at the same moment.
  2. 2
    Blanch. Put the pork in a pot, cover with cold tap water by 3 cm, and add the 10 g / 3 coins blanching ginger and the 15 g / 1 tbsp blanching Shaoxing wine. Bring to a boil over high heat and boil 3 minutes; gray scum (coagulated blood proteins) will rise. Drain, discard the ginger, rinse each cube under warm water to remove clinging scum, and dry well with a towel. Starting cold lets the proteins release slowly and float off instead of setting on the meat, which keeps the final sauce clean and glossy rather than murky.
  3. 3
    Make the caramel. Wipe out the pot (or use a wok or a heavy 3 to 4 L pot with a lid). Over low heat, add the 15 g / 1 tbsp neutral oil and the 45 g crushed rock sugar. Stir with a wooden spoon as the sugar melts: it will look grainy, then turn to clear syrup, then to a light amber with large slow bubbles, about 4 to 6 minutes. Stop at the color of dark honey. Past mahogany it turns bitter, and there is no saving it. Technique: Dry caramel (sugar cooked to amber)
  4. 4
    Glaze the pork. Add the dried pork cubes all at once (stand back; the caramel spits on contact with any moisture) and raise the heat to medium. Toss for 2 to 3 minutes until every cube is coated in a thin amber film and the fat edges begin to render and sizzle.
  5. 5
    Build the braise. Add the 20 g braising ginger coins, the 40 g / 4 scallions, and the 2 g / 1 star anise pod if using, and toss 30 seconds until fragrant. Pour the 150 g / ⅔ cup Shaoxing wine around the edge of the pot so it hits hot metal and sizzles; let it bubble 1 minute to drive off the sharpest alcohol. Add the 35 g / 2 tbsp + 1 tsp light soy sauce and the 15 g / 1 tbsp dark soy sauce, then the 450 ml / 2 cups hot water, enough to just cover the pork. Hot water keeps the caramelized surface from seizing and the fat from turning waxy. The light soy is your first seasoning: the liquid should taste pleasantly salty, like a broth you would sip, because it concentrates threefold by the end.
  6. 6
    Braise low. Bring to a boil, then lower to the gentlest simmer, a few lazy bubbles breaking the surface. Cover and cook 90 minutes, turning the cubes every 30 minutes with a wooden spoon so the top layer does not dry out. A hard boil toughens the lean and emulsifies the fat into the sauce; a bare simmer lets the collagen in the skin and between the layers convert to gelatin while the lean stays intact. If the liquid drops below halfway up the pork, add 60 ml / ¼ cup hot water.
  7. 7
    Check doneness. A chopstick should slide through the skin and fat with no resistance, and the lean should yield when pressed. If the skin still grips, cover and give it 15 more minutes. Taste the liquid now: it should taste sweet-salty and rich, a little under-seasoned for a sauce. Hold back the 1 g / ¼ tsp kosher salt for now, because the reduction will concentrate it.
  8. 8
    Reduce to a lacquer. Fish out and discard the ginger, scallion, and star anise. Uncover, raise the heat to medium-high, and boil, stirring and turning the pork every minute with a wooden spoon. Over 10 to 15 minutes the liquid thickens from broth to syrup; the bubbles shrink and turn glossy. Near the end, stir constantly, because the sugars catch fast. Stop when the sauce coats each cube and only a few spoonfuls of thick glaze remain in the pot, about 60 ml / ¼ cup. The gelatin from the skin is what gives the glaze its body; you are concentrating it, not thickening with starch.
  9. 9
    Season and serve. Taste a piece with glaze. It should be deeply savory, sweet without being candied, and well salted. If it tastes flat, stir in up to 1 g / ¼ tsp kosher salt. Serve at once over 600 g / 4 cups hot jasmine rice; the glaze stiffens as it cools.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Occasion hard-boiled eggs→hong shao rou with braised eggs

    Boil 4 eggs for 8 minutes, peel, and score shallow lines in the whites. Add them with the hot water in step 5 and braise with the pork; they take on the color and a mahogany rind. Add 10 g / 2 tsp extra light soy sauce to season them.

  • Occasion make-ahead→braise the day before

    Stop after step 7, cool, and refrigerate in the braising liquid in a covered glass container overnight. Lift off the solid fat cap the next day (keep it for frying rice), then reheat gently and do the reduction. The flavor deepens and the sauce is cleaner.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • rock sugar → granulated sugar Use 40 g. Caramelizes faster and gives a slightly less glossy, slightly sharper sweetness; watch the color closely.
  • Shaoxing wine → dry sherry (fino or amontillado) The closest common substitute; 1:1. Avoid cooking wines labeled with added salt.
  • skin-on pork belly → skinless pork belly Works, but the glaze has less gelatin and less body. Shorten the braise to 70 minutes and reduce more carefully.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → light soy sauce make it yourselfstub A naturally brewed light soy carries the glutamate this braise depends on with a cleaner, less harsh salinity than cheap hydrolyzed brands.
  • → Shaoxing wine make it yourselfstub The wine is the main braising liquid here, so its quality shows directly in the finished glaze.

Notes

Why no finishing acid. Shanghai hong shao rou is a sweet-savory dish by design, and a splash of vinegar reads as a mistake to anyone who grew up with it. The Shaoxing wine brings some acidity of its own, and the balance comes from the table: plain rice, a sharp cold vegetable, pickles. That is where the acid belongs in this meal.

Glutamate and color come from two different soys. Light soy seasons; dark soy is aged longer with molasses and adds color and depth with little salt. Using only light soy gives a pale, salty dish; using only dark soy gives a dark, bitter one. The rock-sugar caramel adds a second layer of red-brown color and most of the gloss.

The skin is the point. Pork skin is mostly collagen. Ninety minutes at a bare simmer converts it to gelatin, which makes the skin trembling-soft and gives the reduced glaze its body without any starch. Skinless belly works but produces a thinner glaze.

Pork belly reaches well above 90 °C during the braise, far past the USDA 63 °C / 145 °F guideline for whole-muscle pork; doneness here is about tenderness, not safety.

Sources
  • Shanghai and Jiangnan home-cooking tradition of hong shao (red-cooking) — The Shanghai version is the sweet, glossy, soy-dark end of the red-braise family: rock sugar for gloss, Shaoxing wine as the main braising liquid, light soy for salt, dark soy for color. Hunan versions (often attributed to Mao) skip soy and lean on caramel and chili; this is the Jiangnan one.
