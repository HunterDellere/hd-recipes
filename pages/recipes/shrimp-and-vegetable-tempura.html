Recipe

Shrimp and vegetable tempura with tentsuyu

Straightened shrimp, kabocha, Japanese sweet potato, shiitake, and shiso coated in an ice-cold, barely mixed batter of low-protein flour, egg, and water, then fried in a sesame-scented oil from 170 to 180 °C in order from vegetables to seafood. Served at once with a warm dashi, soy, and mirin dipping sauce and grated daikon.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Make the tentsuyu and grate the daikon first. They hold for an hour, and once frying starts you will not have a free hand.
  • Chill the batter ingredients. Put the 150 g cake flour in the freezer and the 250 ml water in the refrigerator (or over ice) at least 30 minutes ahead. Cold batter develops little gluten and fries up thin and crisp.
  • Prepare and dry every piece of shrimp and vegetable before heating the oil, and set a wire rack over a sheet pan beside the stove. Water on a piece is what makes oil spit.
  • Oil safety: use a heavy pot filled no more than halfway, clip a thermometer to the side, keep a lid or sheet pan within reach to smother a flare-up, and never pour water on burning oil. Keep children and pets out of the area while frying.
  • Tempura is best within 2 or 3 minutes of leaving the oil. Fry in rounds and serve each round as it comes out, the way a tempura counter does, rather than holding everything to serve at once.

What to prep, in order 4 phases ▾

Tentsuyu (dipping sauce)

  1. 240 ml dashi (kombu and katsuobushi)
  2. 60 ml mirin (hon-mirin)
  3. 60 ml Japanese soy sauce (koikuchi)
  4. 200 g daikon, peeled and finely grated, lightly squeezed — (a 10 cm / 4 inch piece)
  5. 10 g fresh ginger, peeled and grated on a microplane — (a 3 cm / 1¼ inch piece)

Shrimp and vegetables

  1. 400 g large shrimp, shell-on, head off (16/20 per lb)
  2. 300 g kabocha squash, seeded, cut into 7 mm slices, skin left on — (about ¼ of a small squash)
  3. 250 g Japanese sweet potato (satsumaimo), scrubbed, cut on the diagonal into 7 mm slices — (1 medium; purple skin, pale yellow flesh)
  4. 120 g fresh shiitake mushrooms, stems removed, caps wiped clean (not washed) — (8 medium caps)
  5. 4 g shiso leaves optional — (8 leaves; sold year-round in Japanese markets)

Batter and frying

  1. 30 g cake flour (for dusting)
  2. 150 g cake flour (for the batter), frozen
  3. 50 g large egg, cold — (1 large egg)
  4. 250 ml ice-cold water
  5. 1800 ml neutral frying oil (rice bran or canola)
  6. 200 ml toasted sesame oil

To serve

  1. 120 g lemon, cut into 8 wedges — (1 lemon)
  2. 6 g fine sea salt (for dipping)

Method

  1. 1
    Make the tentsuyu. In a small saucepan, bring the 60 ml / ¼ cup mirin to a boil and boil 30 seconds to cook off the alcohol. Add the 60 ml / ¼ cup soy sauce and the 240 ml / 1 cup dashi, bring just to a simmer, and turn off the heat. The 4 to 1 to 1 ratio gives a sauce light enough to dip into freely; it should taste savory, slightly sweet, and less salty than straight soy. Keep it warm with the lid on.
  2. 2
    Grate the daikon and ginger. Grate the 200 g daikon on the finest side of a box grater or a Japanese oroshigane, then press it gently in a fine sieve to remove excess juice (it should hold its shape in a mound but still be moist). Grate the 10 g ginger on a microplane. Divide both into small mounds, one per diner. The diner stirs them into the sauce: daikon enzymes help digest the oil and its mild pungency cuts the richness.
  3. 3
    Prepare the shrimp. Peel the 400 g / 16 shrimp, leaving the last shell segment and tail on. Devein them with a paring knife. Cut the very tip off each tail at an angle and scrape the tail with the knife blade to push out the water trapped inside, which otherwise bursts and spatters in the oil. Make 4 or 5 shallow cuts across the underside of each shrimp, then lay it belly down and press along its back until you feel the fibers give with a faint snap. This straightens the shrimp so it fries long and flat instead of curling into a ball. Pat dry and refrigerate on a towel-lined plate.
  4. 4
    Prepare the vegetables. Cut the 300 g kabocha and 250 g sweet potato into 7 mm slices; thicker slices brown before the center softens. Soak the sweet potato slices 10 minutes in cold water to rinse off surface starch, which otherwise scorches, then dry them thoroughly. Trim the stems from the 120 g shiitake and cut a shallow cross into each cap if you like. Rinse the 4 g / 8 shiso leaves and pat them completely dry. Lay everything on a tray with dry kitchen towels.
  5. 5
    Heat the oil. Pour the 1800 ml neutral oil and 200 ml toasted sesame oil into a heavy pot or Dutch oven about 22 cm / 9 inches across, so the oil is at least 5 cm / 2 inches deep and the pot is no more than half full. Clip on a thermometer and heat over medium to 170 °C / 340 °F. The sesame oil gives the light, nutty aroma of Tokyo-style tempura shops. Technique: Deep-frying
  6. 6
    Dust the pieces. Put the 30 g / ¼ cup dusting cake flour in a shallow bowl and toss each piece of shrimp and vegetable in it, shaking off the excess; for the shiitake and shiso, dust only the gill side and the back of the leaf. The thin flour layer absorbs surface moisture so the batter clings instead of sliding off.
  7. 7
    Mix the first half of the batter. Beat the 50 g / 1 cold egg in a measuring cup, add the 250 ml ice-cold water, and stir to combine; keep it cold. In a chilled bowl set over a larger bowl of ice, pour in half the egg water (about 150 ml) and add half the frozen flour (75 g / ⅔ cup) all at once. Stir with chopsticks, jabbing rather than whisking, about 10 to 15 strokes, until the flour is just moistened. The batter should be thin, lumpy, and still show streaks of dry flour. Mixing in halves keeps it cold and fresh; batter that sits warms up and develops gluten, which makes a thick, bready, oily coat.
  8. 8
    Fry the dense vegetables at 170 °C / 340 °F. Dip the kabocha and sweet potato slices one at a time in the batter, let excess drip off for a second, and slide them into the oil away from you. Fry 5 or 6 pieces at a time; crowding drops the oil temperature and gives greasy tempura. Fry 3 minutes, turning once, until a skewer slides through easily and the coating is pale gold and crisp; tempura batter should stay light, not brown. Lift onto the wire rack, standing them on edge so steam escapes. Skim the floating crumbs (tenkasu) out between batches with a fine-mesh skimmer; left in, they burn and darken the oil.
  9. 9
    Fry the shiitake and shiso at 175 °C / 350 °F. Let the oil climb to 175 °C. Dip the 120 g shiitake gill side only into the batter and fry 90 seconds, cap side up first, then flip for 15 seconds. Draw each of the 8 shiso leaves across the batter so only the back is coated, lay it in the oil batter side down, and fry 20 seconds without turning, until the batter is set and the leaf is bright green and crisp. Mix the second half of the batter now with the remaining 150 ml egg water and 75 g / ⅔ cup flour, exactly as before.
  10. 10
    Fry the shrimp last at 180 °C / 355 °F. Seafood flavors the oil, so it always goes last. Let the oil climb to 180 °C. Hold each of the 16 shrimp by the tail, dip it in the batter up to the tail, and lower it into the oil, pulling it forward as it enters so it stays straight. For a lacier coat, drizzle a few drops of batter from your fingertips over the shrimp as it fries. Fry 4 or 5 at a time for 90 seconds to 2 minutes, until the coating is crisp and the shrimp are opaque and firm, about 63 °C / 145 °F at the thickest point. The bubbles get smaller and quieter as the water inside boils off; that is the sign it is done.
  11. 11
    Serve immediately. Pile the tempura on a plate lined with parchment, with a mound of daikon and ginger, 2 lemon wedges from the 120 g lemon, and a small pinch of the 6 g fine sea salt per diner. Give each person a small bowl of the warm tentsuyu. Dip vegetables and shrimp briefly in the sauce with daikon stirred in, or eat the shrimp and shiitake with a squeeze of lemon and a touch of salt. Both keep the coating crisp longer than soaking it does.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Texture vegetable set→kakiage (mixed fritters)

    Cut 150 g / 1 small onion into thin half-moons and 80 g / 1 carrot into matchsticks, toss with 60 g / 2 oz peeled small shrimp and 1 tbsp dusting flour, then fold into a third of the batter. Slide 7 cm / 3 inch rounds of the mixture off a flat ladle into 170 °C / 340 °F oil and fry 3 minutes, turning once. A good use for the last of the batter.

  • Occasion season→spring and summer vegetables

    Swap the kabocha and sweet potato for asparagus spears, green beans, Japanese eggplant cut into fans, or young lotus root slices. Thinner, wetter vegetables cook in 60 to 90 seconds at 175 °C / 350 °F. The batter and oil stay the same.

  • Occasion serving→tendon (tempura rice bowl)

    Simmer the tentsuyu down by a third with 1 extra tbsp mirin to make a thicker, sweeter sauce. Dip each piece of tempura briefly in it and lay over bowls of hot short-grain rice, then spoon a little more sauce over the top.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • cake flour → all-purpose flour plus potato starch or cornstarch Replace each 100 g cake flour with 80 g all-purpose flour and 20 g potato starch or cornstarch. The starch lowers the protein content toward cake flour's 7 to 8%, which is what keeps the coating thin and crisp. Japanese tempura flour (tempura-ko) also works; skip the egg if its package says it contains egg.
  • Japanese sweet potato → orange sweet potato Softer, wetter, and sweeter. Fry at 165 °C / 330 °F for 3½ minutes so the center cooks before the batter browns.
  • kabocha squash → butternut squash Peel it, since butternut skin is tough. It is wetter and less sweet than kabocha; slice 6 mm thick.
  • shiso leaves → Korean perilla leaves (kkaennip), or leave them out Korean perilla (kkaennip) is a close relative and fries the same way, though it tastes more of anise and less of mint.
  • toasted sesame oil → all neutral oil Lighter and cleaner, closer to Kansai-style tempura. Nothing else changes.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → dashi (kombu and katsuobushi) make it yourself A fresh dashi gives the tentsuyu its clean, smoky savoriness; it takes 15 minutes and can be made while the cake flour chills.

Notes

Why the batter is cold, lumpy, and made in halves. Wheat flour forms gluten when its proteins hydrate and are stirred. Gluten makes a chewy, bready coat that holds oil. Low-protein cake flour, near-freezing water, and a few strokes of the chopsticks all limit gluten, and the lumps show that the flour is not fully hydrated. In hot oil the water in the thin batter flashes to steam and leaves a lacy, brittle shell. Batter that has been sitting for 15 minutes or has warmed to room temperature fries thicker and greasier, which is why the second half is mixed fresh partway through.

Oil temperature and frying order. Dense, starchy vegetables need the lower end (170 °C) and longer frying so their centers cook before the coating browns. Shiitake and shiso cook quickly at 175 °C. Shrimp go in last at 180 °C for a short, hot fry that sets the coating before the shrimp overcook, and because seafood leaves its flavor in the oil. Going from cooler to hotter also suits the pot, since the oil climbs naturally between rounds. Each batch drops the temperature by 10 °C or more; wait for it to recover before the next one.

Glutamate and acid. The tentsuyu carries the savory side: kombu glutamate and katsuobushi inosinate in the dashi, plus soy sauce. Shiitake add their own guanylate. The finishing acid is the lemon wedge for the salt-and-lemon route; with the tentsuyu, grated daikon does the same job of cutting through the oil.

Reusing the oil. Let it cool fully, strain it through a fine sieve lined with a paper coffee filter, and store it in a glass jar away from light. Tempura oil can be used 2 or 3 more times for frying; discard it when it darkens, smells fishy or stale, or smokes at frying temperature. Keep the strained tenkasu crumbs in a glass jar in the refrigerator for a few days to scatter over udon or rice.
