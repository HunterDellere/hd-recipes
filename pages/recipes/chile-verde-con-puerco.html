Chile verde con puerco

Pork shoulder seared in lard and braised in a salsa of broiled tomatillos, poblano, Anaheim, and jalapeño chiles. The tomatillos supply both the acid and the body; charring them first trades their raw sourness for a sweeter, smoky depth.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Salt the pork at least 1 hour and up to 24 hours ahead (covered, in the fridge).
  • The chiles need 10 minutes covered after broiling so their skins loosen. Broil while the pork browns, or broil first and brown while they steam.
  • Serve with warm corn tortillas or rice. If you are making tortillas from masa, start the dough while the stew braises.

Step 1 · Salt the pork

  1. 1600 g boneless pork shoulder, cut into 3 cm cubes, thick fat cap trimmed
  2. 16 g kosher salt

Step 2 · Broiled salsa

  1. 680 g tomatillos, husked, rinsed of their sticky coating, halved
  2. 300 g poblano peppers — (2 large)
  3. 250 g Anaheim or Hatch green chiles — (4 chiles)
  4. 60 g jalapeños — (2; use 1 for mild)
  5. 250 g white onion, cut into 8 wedges — (1 medium)
  6. 30 g garlic cloves, unpeeled — (8 cloves)
  7. 20 g cilantro stems — (stems from 1 bunch; save the leaves)
  8. 250 ml chicken stock, for blending

Step 3 · Brown and braise

  1. 30 g lard
  2. 2 g ground cumin
  3. 2 g Mexican oregano
  4. 375 ml chicken stock, for the braise
  5. 2 bay leaves

Step 4 · Finish

  1. 30 ml lime juice
  2. 15 g cilantro leaves, roughly chopped
  3. 3 g kosher salt, to adjust

Step 5 · To serve

  1. 16 corn tortillas, warmed
  2. 120 g Mexican crema

Method

  1. 1
    Salt the pork. Toss the 1600 g cubed pork shoulder with the 16 g kosher salt, cover with a plate or fitted lid, and refrigerate at least 1 hour and up to 24 hours.
  2. 2
    Broil the salsa vegetables. Set an oven rack 10 cm / 4 in below the broiler and heat the broiler to high. Spread the 680 g halved tomatillos cut side down, the 300 g whole poblanos, the 250 g Anaheim chiles, the 60 g jalapeños, the 250 g onion wedges, and the 30 g unpeeled garlic on a rimmed sheet pan lined with foil. Broil 6 minutes, turn the chiles and onion, and broil 5 to 6 minutes more, until the chile skins are blistered and blackened in patches and the tomatillos are slumped and charred at the edges. Pull the garlic early if it blackens. Technique: High-heat dry roasting
  3. 3
    Steam and peel the chiles. Move the poblanos and Anaheims to a bowl and cover with a plate for 10 minutes. Rub off the blistered skins with your fingers (do not rinse; water washes away the roasted flavor), then stem and seed them. Stem the 60 g jalapeños, keeping the seeds for heat. Squeeze the garlic from its skins. Leave everything else on the pan with its juices.
  4. 4
    Brown the pork while the chiles steam. Heat the oven to 150 °C / 300 °F. Pat the pork dry. Heat 10 g / 2 tsp of the lard in a large Dutch oven over medium-high heat until it shimmers. Brown a third of the pork in a single layer, about 6 minutes, turning once so two sides are deep brown. Transfer to a bowl and repeat with the remaining 20 g lard and pork in two more batches. Technique: Maillard sear
  5. 5
    Blend the salsa. Put the peeled chiles, jalapeños, garlic, tomatillos, onion, and every bit of pan juice in the blender with the 20 g cilantro stems and the 250 ml / 1 cup blending chicken stock. Pulse 8 to 10 times to a coarse purée with some small pieces still visible. Chile verde has a looser, more rustic sauce than a smooth enchilada salsa.
  6. 6
    Fry the salsa. Lower the heat under the Dutch oven to medium. Add the 2 g / 1 tsp ground cumin and the 2 g / 2 tsp Mexican oregano, rubbing the oregano between your palms, and stir 20 seconds in the fat until fragrant. Pour in the salsa all at once (it will spatter) and cook, scraping up the fond, about 6 minutes, until it darkens from bright to olive green and thickens slightly. This takes the raw edge off the tomatillos and folds the browned pork flavor into the sauce. Technique: Blooming aromatics
  7. 7
    Braise. Return the pork and its juices to the pot with the 375 ml / 1½ cups braising chicken stock and the 2 bay leaves. The pork should be just covered; add water if needed. Bring to a simmer, cover with the lid slightly ajar, and braise in the 150 °C / 300 °F oven for 2 to 2½ hours, stirring once halfway, until a cube splits under light pressure from a spoon. If the sauce is thin at the end, simmer uncovered on the stovetop for 10 minutes.
  8. 8
    Finish and season. Remove the bay leaves. Off the heat, stir in the 30 ml / 2 tbsp lime juice and the 15 g chopped cilantro leaves. Taste a piece of pork with sauce and add up to 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt. Tomatillos lose some of their sourness during the braise; the lime brings back the brightness the dish needs against the pork fat.
  9. 9
    Serve in bowls with the 16 warm corn tortillas on the side, a spoonful of the 120 g Mexican crema, and some of the 60 g crumbled cotija.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Regional chiles→New Mexico green chile stew

    Leave out the tomatillos and cilantro. Use 700 g roasted and peeled Hatch green chiles (fresh or frozen) in place of the poblanos and Anaheims, chop them instead of blending, and add 500 g waxy potatoes in 2 cm cubes for the last 45 minutes of the braise. Increase the chicken stock for the braise to 750 ml / 3 cups.

  • Protein protein→chicken thighs

    Use 1.4 kg boneless, skinless chicken thighs cut into 4 cm pieces. Brown only one side, then braise 45 minutes on the stovetop at a bare simmer.

  • Occasion occasion→burritos or tacos

    Simmer the finished stew uncovered for 20 minutes more until the sauce clings, then shred the pork lightly with two forks.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • Anaheim or Hatch green chiles → more poblanos Same weight. Poblanos are darker and earthier; Anaheims are brighter and more vegetal.
  • lard → neutral oil Same amount. The stew tastes slightly leaner.
  • cotija → queso fresco Milder and less salty; crumbles the same way.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → chicken stock, for blending make it yourself The gelatin in homemade stock gives the green sauce body without any thickener.
  • → chicken stock, for the braise make it yourself Carton stock adds salt and a cooked-vegetable flavor that competes with the tomatillos.
  • → corn tortillas make it yourself Fresh tortillas are soft, pliable, and taste of toasted corn; they are what makes a plate of chile verde.
  • → Mexican crema make it yourselfstub Homemade crema is thinner and tangier than sour cream and does not break on hot stew.

Notes

Why broil the tomatillos. Raw tomatillos are sharply sour and slightly grassy. Charring them under the broiler caramelizes their sugars and softens the acid, so the sauce tastes rounder and the pork does not read as pickled. Their pectin also thickens the sauce as it braises, which is why chile verde needs no flour or masa.

Three green chiles, three jobs. Poblano gives earthy depth and dark color, Anaheim gives a bright, vegetal flavor, and jalapeño gives the heat. Peeling the poblanos and Anaheims matters: their skins turn into tough, papery shreds in a long braise.

Why shoulder. Pork shoulder (Boston butt) has the fat and collagen to stay moist through more than 2 hours of cooking. Loin turns dry and chalky in the same time.

Glutamate and acid. Browned pork, chicken stock, charred tomatillos, and cotija at the table carry the savory base. Tomatillos are the first acid; lime at the end is the second.
