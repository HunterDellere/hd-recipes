Recipe

Birria de res with consomé

Beef chuck and oxtail marinated overnight in a guajillo, ancho, and chile de árbol adobo with vinegar, toasted spices, and charred tomato, then braised for four hours until the meat falls apart and the bones have turned the liquid into a glossy, gelatin-rich consomé. Served as tacos on fat-dipped, griddled corn tortillas with a cup of consomé for dipping.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Marinate the meat in the adobo 12 to 24 hours ahead. The salt and vinegar need the time to season the meat through; a same-day marinade gives chile-coated meat that is bland inside.
  • The braise takes about 4 hours in the oven, mostly unattended. Birria is better made a full day ahead: chill the pot overnight and the fat sets as a red disk on top that lifts off cleanly, ready for frying the tortillas.
  • If you are making your own tortillas, press and cook them during the last hour of the braise and keep them in a towel-lined warmer.

Meat

  2. 1000 g oxtail, cut into segments
  3. 20 g kosher salt, for the meat

Braise and consomé

  1. 1500 ml water, for the braise
  2. 75 g white onion, for the braise — (½ small onion, left in one piece)
  3. 3 bay leaves — (3 dried Mediterranean bay leaves)
  4. 4 g kosher salt, for the consomé

Tacos and garnish

  1. 720 g corn tortillas, 12 to 15 cm / 5 to 6 inch — (24 tortillas)
  2. 120 g white onion, finely diced
  3. 20 g cilantro, chopped
  4. 200 g limes, cut into wedges — (3 limes)

Method

  1. 1
    Salt the meat (the day before). Pat the 1500 g chuck chunks and the 1000 g oxtail dry and season all over with the 20 g / 2 tbsp kosher salt. Put the meat in a Dutch oven or a glass baking dish while you make the adobo.
  2. 2
    Toast the chiles. Heat a dry comal or heavy skillet over medium heat. Open the 80 g guajillo chiles and the 30 g ancho chiles flat and press them onto the surface with a metal spatula for 10 to 15 seconds per side, working in batches, until they blister and smell of dried fruit. Toast the 4 g chiles de árbol for only about 5 seconds per side; they are small and scorch quickly. Any chile that blackens goes in the bin, because burnt chile makes the whole adobo bitter. Cover the toasted chiles with just-boiled water and soak 20 minutes until soft. Technique: Dry-toasting (grains, spices, seeds, dried chilies)
  3. 3
    Toast the spices and char the tomatoes. In the same pan over medium heat, toast the 2 g / 1 tsp cumin seeds, 3 g / 1 tsp black peppercorns, 0.5 g / 4 whole cloves, and the 3 g / 5 cm canela, shaking the pan, for about 1 minute until the cumin darkens a shade and smells nutty. Tip them out. Raise the heat to medium-high and roast the 400 g / 4 Roma tomatoes, turning occasionally, for 10 to 12 minutes until blackened in patches and soft. Technique: Comal charring (vegetables, chilies, tortillas)
  4. 4
    Blend the adobo. Drain the chiles and discard the soaking water. In a blender, combine the chiles, tomatoes, the 75 g chopped white onion, the 40 g / 10 garlic cloves, the 60 ml / ¼ cup apple cider vinegar, the toasted spices, the 2 g / 2 tsp Mexican oregano, the 10 g / 1 tbsp kosher salt, and the 240 ml / 1 cup water for blending. Blend on high for 2 full minutes, until completely smooth. Press it through a fine-mesh sieve with a wooden spoon to remove chile skins and seeds. Taste: it should be aggressively seasoned, sour, and hot, because it is about to be diluted by more than its own volume of water.
  5. 5
    Marinate. Pour the adobo over the salted meat and turn every piece to coat. Cover with the Dutch oven lid (or a plate over the glass dish) and refrigerate 12 to 24 hours. The salt and vinegar season and slightly firm the meat surface during this time.12 h
  6. 6
    Braise. Heat the oven to 160 °C / 325 °F. If the meat marinated in a dish, transfer it and all the adobo to a large Dutch oven (at least 6 L / 6 qt). Add the 1500 ml water for the braise, the 75 g piece of white onion, and the 3 bay leaves; stir with a wooden spoon to loosen the adobo. Bring to a simmer over medium heat, cover tightly, and transfer to the oven. Braise for 3½ to 4 hours, stirring once halfway, until the chuck shreds with no resistance under a fork and the oxtail meat slides off the bone. At 160 °C the liquid holds at a gentle simmer, hot enough to convert the collagen in the oxtail and chuck to gelatin over the hours but gentle enough that the muscle fibers do not tighten and dry.
  7. 7
    Separate meat, fat, and consomé. Lift the meat out with tongs onto a tray. Let the pot sit off the heat for 10 minutes so the fat rises, then ladle the red fat off the surface into a small bowl; you want about 60 g / ¼ cup for the tortillas (if made a day ahead, lift off the chilled fat disk instead). Remove the onion and bay leaves. Pull the oxtail meat from the bones and shred the chuck with two forks, discarding bones and gristle. Moisten the shredded meat with 240 ml / 1 cup of the consomé so it stays juicy.
  8. 8
    Season the consomé. Taste the consomé. It should be rich and sippable, closer to a brothy soup than a sauce. If it tastes heavy or too concentrated, thin it with up to 500 ml / 2 cups water; if thin, simmer it uncovered for 10 to 15 minutes. Season with about 4 g / 1 tsp kosher salt, tasting as you go: a properly salted consomé makes the chiles taste fruity instead of harsh. Keep it hot over low heat.
  9. 9
    Make the tacos. Heat a comal or large skillet over medium-high heat. Dip one side of each of the 720 g / 24 corn tortillas into the reserved red fat (or brush it on), lay it fat-side down on the comal, and pile about 45 g / 3 tbsp shredded meat on one half. Fold and cook 1 to 2 minutes per side, pressing with a metal spatula, until the tortilla is crisp and stained red at the edges. Work in batches and keep finished tacos in a low oven.
  10. 10
    Serve. Ladle the hot consomé into cups or small bowls and top each with some of the 120 g / 1 cup diced white onion and 20 g / 1 cup chopped cilantro. Put the remaining onion, cilantro, and the 200 g / 3 limes cut into wedges on the table. Squeeze lime into the consomé and over the tacos; the lime is the acid that cuts the fat and makes the chile taste bright. Dip each taco in the consomé as you eat.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Occasion quesatacos (quesabirria)→cheese-filled, crisp tacos

    After dipping the tortilla in the fat and laying it on the comal, scatter 15 g shredded queso Oaxaca (about 300 g for 20 tacos) over it, add the meat, and fold once the cheese starts to melt. Cook until the cheese that leaks out fries into a lacy crust against the comal, then flip. The recipe is no longer dairy-free.

  • Regional protein→goat or lamb (the Jalisco original)

    Use 2.5 kg bone-in goat or lamb shoulder and neck in place of the chuck and oxtail. Lamb cooks faster: check at 3 hours. The flavor is gamier and the consomé fattier, so skim more carefully.

  • Occasion serving→birria en plato (as a stew)

    Skip the tacos. Serve the meat in wide bowls covered with ladlefuls of consomé, with the onion, cilantro, lime, and warm soft tortillas on the side, the way it is served in Guadalajara on weekend mornings.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • oxtail → bone-in beef short ribs or cross-cut beef shank Shank gives nearly as much gelatin and more meat; short ribs give a fattier, sweeter consomé with somewhat less body. Use the same weight.
  • Roma tomatoes → canned whole tomatoes Use 400 g drained. Skip the charring; the long braise covers the canned flavor.
  • canela (Ceylon cinnamon stick) → cassia cinnamon stick Use half the amount (a 2.5 cm / 1 inch piece); cassia is harsher and takes over quickly.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → corn tortillas, 12 to 15 cm / 5 to 6 inch make it yourself Fresh masa tortillas hold together better after a dip in the fat and taste distinctly of corn against the chile; make a double batch for 24 tacos.

Notes

Why no sear. Many beef braises start by browning the meat, but chile-coated meat scorches in a hot pan, and burnt chile turns bitter. Birria builds its depth instead from toasted chiles, charred tomato, and toasted spices, plus four hours of reduction. Glutamate comes from the tomato and the long-cooked beef and bones; the vinegar in the adobo and the lime at the table supply the acid.

The chile blend. Guajillo carries the color and a bright, tart fruitiness and makes up most of the adobo. Ancho adds a darker, raisin-like sweetness and body. Árbol adds clean heat and little else, so it is the dial to turn for spiciness without changing the flavor. Buy chiles that are soft and pliable; brittle, faded chiles are old and taste dusty.

Why oxtail. Chuck alone makes good shredded meat but a thin consomé. Oxtail and shank are rich in collagen, which converts to gelatin during the braise and gives the consomé its lip-coating body; chilled, a good consomé sets to a loose jelly.

Make ahead and storage. The meat and consomé keep 4 days refrigerated in separate lidded glass containers and freeze for 3 months. Reheat the meat in a little consomé so it does not dry out.

Sources
  • Jalisco birria tradition, in the beef form popularized in Tijuana — Birria in Jalisco is usually goat or mutton, marinated in a dried-chile adobo and slow-cooked (traditionally in a pit or a sealed pot) so the meat steams in its own juices, with the collected juices served as consomé. Beef birria, now the version most US cooks meet, follows the same logic. The cut choice here does the work the goat's bones and fat do in the original: chuck for meat that shreds, oxtail for gelatin and body in the consomé.
