Gong bao chicken (Sichuan kung pao chicken)

Diced chicken thigh in a light starch marinade, stir-fried hard in oil that has bloomed dried chilies and Sichuan peppercorns, then tossed with scallion, garlic, and ginger in a thin sweet-sour glaze of Chinkiang vinegar and soy. Roasted peanuts go in last so they stay crisp; the sauce clings, it does not pool.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Start jasmine rice first. The stir-fry takes about 5 minutes once the wok is hot, and gong bao is eaten the moment it leaves the wok, before the peanuts soften.
  • Marinate the chicken 15 minutes while you cut the aromatics and mix the sauce. Have everything in bowls next to the stove before the oil goes in; there is no pause once the chilies hit the oil.
  • Turn the range hood on high. Toasting dried chilies in hot oil puts capsaicin in the air, which makes everyone in the room cough.

What to prep, in order 4 phases ▾

Chicken and marinade

  1. 450 g boneless, skinless chicken thighs, trimmed of large fat pockets, cut into 1.5 cm dice
  2. 2 g kosher salt
  3. 10 g light soy sauce (for the marinade)
  4. 10 g Shaoxing wine
  5. 8 g potato starch (for the marinade)
  6. 15 ml cold water (for the marinade)

Sauce

  1. 18 g granulated sugar
  2. 22 g Chinkiang (black) vinegar
  3. 7 g light soy sauce (for the sauce)
  4. 7 g dark soy sauce
  5. 3 g potato starch (for the sauce)
  6. 30 ml chicken stock
  7. 5 g toasted sesame oil

Wok

  1. 40 g neutral oil (peanut, rice bran, or canola)
  2. 6 g dried red chilies (erjingtiao or facing-heaven), snipped with scissors into 2 cm pieces, seeds shaken out and discarded — (about 12 chilies; more for heat, but the seeds scorch and turn bitter, so always discard them)
  3. 3 g Sichuan peppercorns, whole, picked over to remove black seeds and twigs
  4. 15 g garlic, peeled and thinly sliced — (4 cloves)
  5. 15 g fresh ginger, peeled and thinly sliced, about the same size as the garlic — (1-inch piece)
  6. 60 g scallions, white and pale green parts, cut into 1.5 cm lengths, roughly the size of the chicken dice — (about 6 scallions; save the dark greens for another use)
  7. 80 g roasted unsalted peanuts, skins rubbed off

To serve

  1. 450 g cooked jasmine rice

Method

  1. 1
    Marinate. In a bowl, combine the 450 g diced chicken thigh with the 2 g / ½ tsp kosher salt, the 10 g / 2 tsp light soy sauce, and the 10 g / 2 tsp Shaoxing wine; mix by hand for 30 seconds until the meat feels tacky. Add the 8 g / 1 tbsp potato starch and the 15 ml / 1 tbsp cold water and mix until each piece wears a thin, even coat. This is the seasoning stage for the meat: the salt and soy penetrate the small dice in 15 minutes, and the starch film protects the surface in the hot wok and grabs the sauce later. Rest 15 minutes at room temperature.
  2. 2
    Mix the sauce. In a small bowl, stir together the 18 g / 1½ tbsp granulated sugar, the 22 g / 1½ tbsp Chinkiang vinegar, the 7 g / 1¼ tsp light soy sauce, the 7 g / 1¼ tsp dark soy sauce, the 3 g / 1 tsp potato starch, the 30 ml / 2 tbsp chicken stock, and the 5 g / 1 tsp toasted sesame oil until the sugar and starch dissolve. Taste it: it should be sharply sweet-sour and slightly salty. If your vinegar is mild, add up to 5 g / 1 tsp more; the sour should lead.
  3. 3
    Bloom the chilies and peppercorns. Heat a wok over high heat until a drop of water vaporizes on contact, then add the 40 g / 3 tbsp neutral oil and swirl. Lower the heat to medium, add the 6 g / about 12 snipped dried chilies and the 3 g / 1½ tsp Sichuan peppercorns, and stir constantly for 15 to 20 seconds, until the chilies darken to a deep red-brown and the oil smells toasty and numbing. Watch the color, not the clock: black chilies are bitter and must be discarded with the oil. The fat is extracting fat-soluble capsaicin and the peppercorn aromatics; this oil seasons everything that follows. Technique: Blooming aromatics
  4. 4
    Stir-fry the chicken. Immediately turn the heat to high and add the marinated 450 g chicken. Spread it out, let it sit 30 seconds to take some color, then toss constantly until the pieces separate and turn opaque, about 2 minutes.
  5. 5
    Add the aromatics. Add the 15 g / 4 cloves sliced garlic, the 15 g sliced ginger, and the 60 g scallion pieces and stir-fry 1 to 2 minutes, until fragrant and the chicken is cooked through: a cut piece shows no pink, and an instant-read thermometer in the largest piece reads at least 74 °C / 165 °F. Thigh tolerates (and is better at) a few degrees more.
  6. 6
    Glaze. Stir the sauce again (the starch settles) and pour all of it, about 90 g, into the wok. Toss for 20 to 30 seconds until it thickens to a shiny glaze that coats the chicken with almost nothing left pooling in the wok. Starch gelatinizes near boiling, so the wok must be hot enough to bubble the sauce immediately.
  7. 7
    Finish and serve. Off the heat, toss in the 80 g / ½ cup roasted peanuts and serve at once over the 450 g / 3 cups jasmine rice. Taste one bite with rice before serving: it should hit sour, then sweet, then salt, with numbing heat underneath. The chilies are for flavor and are not usually eaten.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Protein protein→chicken breast

    Use the same weight of breast, add a pinch (about 0.5 g) of baking soda to the marinade, and pull the chicken from the wok while the centers are still slightly pink, since it finishes cooking with the aromatics and sauce. Take it to 74 °C / 165 °F and no further: breast dries fast above that.

  • Dietary vegetarian→gong bao with firm tofu and mushrooms

    Replace the chicken with 400 g pressed firm tofu cubes, pan-fried golden in 30 g extra oil before step 3, and 150 g king oyster mushroom dice. Skip the marinade; add the tofu back with the sauce. Use water in place of the chicken stock.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • Chinkiang vinegar → Baoning vinegar The Sichuan original; darker and more malty. Use the same amount.
  • dried red chilies → chiles de árbol Hotter and thinner-skinned than erjingtiao; use about 8 and watch them closely, they scorch faster.
  • potato starch → cornstarch Same quantity. Slightly less glossy, otherwise identical.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → roasted unsalted peanuts make it yourself Freshly roasted peanuts are crisper and more fragrant than jarred ones, which often taste stale by the time they reach the wok.
  • → chicken stock make it yourself Only two tablespoons go in, but homemade stock adds gelatin that helps the glaze cling.
  • → light soy sauce (for the marinade) make it yourselfstub A naturally brewed light soy is the main glutamate source in the dish.
  • → Shaoxing wine make it yourselfstub Shaoxing seasons the marinade and masks the gaminess of thigh meat.

Notes

What makes it gong bao. Three things: chilies and Sichuan peppercorns scorched in oil until fragrant (hu la, "scorched chili" flavor), the sweet-sour "lychee" sauce in which vinegar slightly leads sugar, and peanuts added at the very end for crunch. Bell peppers, celery, zucchini, and a thick sweet sauce belong to the American-Chinese version.

About the marinade. The velveting page describes the Cantonese baking-soda treatment for lean cuts and advises against using it on thigh. This recipe uses the lighter Sichuan starch marinade instead: salt, soy, wine, starch, no baking soda. Thigh does not need tenderizing; the starch coat is there for seasoning, a protected surface in the wok, and sauce cling.

Why thigh. It stays juicy across a wider temperature window than breast, which matters in a stir-fry where you cannot easily take temperatures mid-toss. Cook it past 74 °C / 165 °F; it remains tender well beyond that.

Heat level. The dish is meant to be fragrant and numbing with moderate heat. Discarding the seeds and stopping the chilies at red-brown keeps it from turning harsh. To make it hotter, add more chilies rather than toasting them longer.

  • Sichuan home and restaurant tradition (gong bao ji ding), with sauce proportions close to those Fuchsia Dunlop records in Every Grain of Rice — Gong bao is defined by its "lychee flavor" (li zhi wei): sour and sweet in near balance, with the sour arriving first, over the scorched-chili and numbing-peppercorn base.
