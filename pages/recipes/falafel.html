Falafel

Levantine chickpea falafel made the way the street stalls make it: dried chickpeas soaked overnight and ground raw with a large volume of parsley, cilantro, onion, and garlic, so the interior fries up loose, fluffy, and bright green inside a dark, crackling shell. Served in warm pita with tahini sauce, tomato, cucumber, pickled turnip, and herbs.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Soak the dried chickpeas 18 to 24 hours ahead in plenty of cold water (they triple in volume). They must never be cooked or canned: cooked chickpeas have gelatinized starch and turn the mixture into a paste that dissolves in the oil.
  • The ground mixture rests 30 minutes in the refrigerator before shaping. Use that window to make the tahini sauce, slice the salad, and heat the frying oil.
  • Warm the pita in the oven at 120 °C / 250 °F for the last 10 minutes of frying, wrapped in a clean kitchen towel, so the bread and the falafel land together.

Method

  1. 1
    The day before. Pick over the 250 g / 1¼ cups dried chickpeas, rinse, and cover with cold water by 8 cm / 3 inches in a large bowl. Soak 18 to 24 hours at cool room temperature (or in the refrigerator if the kitchen is warm). They are ready when a chickpea splits cleanly between your fingers and the inside is uniformly pale, with no chalky core.18 h
  2. 2
    Drain the soaked chickpeas (about 550 g / 3½ cups) in a colander, then spread them on a clean kitchen towel and rub dry. Surface water is the main reason falafel fall apart in the oil. Wash and spin the 40 g / 1 cup parsley and 30 g / ¾ cup cilantro, then blot them completely dry in a towel.
  3. 3
    Grind in stages in a food processor. First pulse the chickpeas with the 80 g / ½ onion and 12 g / 4 garlic cloves in 1-second bursts, scraping down twice, until the texture is like coarse couscous. Add the parsley and cilantro, the 4 g / 2 tsp cumin, 3 g / 1½ tsp coriander, 1 g / ½ tsp black pepper, and 8 g / 2½ tsp kosher salt, and pulse again until the mixture is evenly green and fine as wet sand. Stop before it turns into a paste: squeezed in your fist, it should hold together and show distinct grains. A paste fries dense and gummy; grains fry light. Work in two batches if your processor holds less than 11 cups.
  4. 4
    Transfer to a glass or steel bowl, cover with a fitted lid or a plate, and refrigerate 30 minutes. The rest lets the ground chickpea starch absorb free moisture and firms the fat-free mixture enough to shape.
  5. 5
    Make the tahini sauce while the mixture rests. In a bowl, whisk the 120 g / ½ cup tahini with the 45 g / 3 tbsp lemon juice, the 4 g / 1 grated garlic clove, and 2 g / ⅓ tsp fine sea salt. It will seize into a stiff, grainy paste; that is the sesame proteins and starches binding the lemon water. Keep whisking while you add the 100 g / 7 tbsp ice water a tablespoon at a time until it loosens into a pale, pourable sauce the thickness of heavy cream. Taste: it should read nutty, clearly lemony, and well salted. Adjust with a pinch more salt or a squeeze of lemon. Technique: Tahini emulsion
  6. 6
    Set up the salad and the oil. Toss the 200 g tomato and 150 g cucumber with the 1 g / ¼ tsp kosher salt and 10 g / 2 tsp lemon juice. Slice the 120 g pickled turnips and pick the 6 g mint leaves. Pour the 1500 ml / 6 cups frying oil into a heavy saucepan or Dutch oven (at least 5 cm / 2 inches deep, with 8 cm of headroom) and heat to 175 °C / 350 °F, checked with an instant-read or clip-on thermometer.
  7. 7
    Add the leavening. Sprinkle the 2.5 g / ½ tsp baking soda over the cold mixture and fold it through evenly with your hands. It goes in now, not at the grinding stage, so it is fresh when the falafel hit the oil: in the fryer it releases carbon dioxide that opens the crumb, and it raises the surface pH so the crust browns fast. Left in the mixture for hours it reads soapy.
  8. 8
    Test-fry one. Shape 30 g / 2 tbsp of mixture into a ball, flatten it slightly to a thick 4 cm disc, and fry it for 3½ minutes. Break it open: it should hold its shape, the crust should be deep brown, and the inside should be green, steamy, and cooked through with no raw-bean taste. Taste for salt and adjust the bowl with a pinch of kosher salt if needed. If it fell apart, the mixture is too wet; fold in 10 g / 1 tbsp chickpea flour or all-purpose flour and test again. Technique: Deep-frying
  9. 9
    Shape the rest into 30 g / 2 tbsp discs with damp hands or a falafel scoop, about 24 in all, setting them on a parchment-lined tray. Press a pinch of the 10 g / 1 tbsp sesame seeds onto one face of each disc.
  10. 10
    Fry in batches of 6, lowering them in with a slotted spoon so the oil temperature does not fall below 165 °C / 330 °F. Fry 3 to 4 minutes, turning once, until the crust is a deep mahogany brown, darker than you think: a pale falafel is soft and greasy, because the crust has not set enough to keep oil out. Lift onto a rack set over a tray and let the oil come back to 175 °C / 350 °F before the next batch. Technique: Deep-frying
  11. 11
    Assemble. Open each warm pita at the top and spread 1 tbsp of the tahini sauce inside. Add 6 falafel, pressing them lightly so they crack and catch sauce, then layer in about 90 g of the salted tomato and cucumber, 30 g of pickled turnips, and a few mint leaves, and finish with another 2 tbsp tahini sauce. Eat immediately, while the crust still crackles.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Occasion falafel plate instead of sandwich→mezze plate

    Serve the falafel on a platter with the tahini sauce in a bowl for dipping, alongside hummus, the tomato-cucumber salad, pickles, and torn warm pita. Plan 5 falafel per person as a main, 2 to 3 as part of a larger mezze.

  • Regional chickpea only→half chickpea, half split fava (the Lebanese stall blend)

    Replace 125 g of the chickpeas with 125 g dried peeled split fava beans, soaked alongside for the same 18 to 24 hours. Fava grinds smoother and greener and gives a creamier, slightly grassier interior; fry the same way. All fava gives Egyptian ta'ameya, which usually swaps the parsley-cilantro balance toward more cilantro and dill.

  • Occasion fresh frying→make-ahead and frozen

    After the rest, fold in the 2.5 g / ½ tsp baking soda, shape the discs, and freeze them at once in a single layer on a parchment-lined tray (freezing halts the soda), then pack into a glass container. They keep 3 months. Fry from frozen at 175 °C / 350 °F for 4½ to 5 minutes; the crumb is slightly less open than fresh, but still light.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • pickled turnips (kabees lift) → cucumber pickles (sour, not sweet) or pickled hot peppers The sandwich needs a sharp pickle for acid and crunch against the fried crust; any brined sour pickle does the job, though the turnip's mild mustardy bite is the Lebanese standard.
  • cilantro, leaves and tender stems → an equal weight of extra parsley plus 5 g dill For cooks who taste cilantro as soap. The interior stays green; the flavor leans more Lebanese and less Palestinian.
  • tahini sauce (tarator) → tahini-yogurt sauce Softer and less bitter, but no longer vegan. The plain tarator is the street standard.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → pita bread, pocket-style make it yourself Fresh pita still soft from the oven opens cleanly into a pocket and does not crack when stuffed, which supermarket pita rarely manages.
  • → tahini make it yourself Freshly ground tahini is sweeter and less bitter than most supermarket jars, and the sauce is mostly tahini, so the difference carries straight through.
  • → pickled turnips (kabees lift) make it yourselfstub Five minutes of work and a week of waiting give a crisper, cleaner pickle than the jarred kind, which is often soft and dyed rather than beet-tinted.

Notes

Why soaked raw chickpeas. Raw soaked chickpeas contain ungelatinized starch and intact protein. Ground coarsely, the particles stay separate; in the fryer, the starch gelatinizes and the proteins set around steam pockets, which is what makes the interior fluffy. Cooked or canned chickpeas have already gelatinized: they grind to a sticky paste that either falls apart in the oil or fries into a dense, hummus-like puck. No amount of flour fixes this, which is why the soak is the one long lead in the recipe.

The green interior. The herb load (70 g of parsley and cilantro to 250 g dry chickpeas) is what separates a Levantine falafel from a beige one. Dry herbs matter: wet leaves add free water that weakens the mixture. Grinding the herbs after the chickpeas, not with them from the start, keeps them in visible flecks rather than a muddy paste.

Oil temperature. 175 °C / 350 °F is the working point. At 160 °C the crust sets slowly and the falafel absorb oil; at 190 °C the outside darkens before the center steams through. Batches of 6 in 1.5 litres of oil hold the temperature drop to about 10 °C.

Umami and acid. Falafel has no added glutamate source, and the tradition does not use one; the depth comes from the browned crust (Maillard products from the chickpea proteins and the soda-raised surface pH) and from the tomato in the sandwich. Acid is carried by the lemon in the tahini sauce and the salad, and by the pickle. Do not add lemon to the falafel mixture itself: acid neutralizes the baking soda.

Storage. Leftover fried falafel reheat well in a 200 °C / 400 °F oven for 8 minutes on a rack; the microwave makes them soggy. The raw mixture (without soda) keeps 2 days refrigerated in a covered glass container. The tahini sauce keeps 5 days refrigerated and thickens; loosen with a spoon of water.
