Recipe

Texas chili con carne

Salted cubes of beef chuck seared in lard and braised in a purée of toasted ancho, guajillo, pasilla, and árbol chiles, with cumin and Mexican oregano kept in the background. Masa harina thickens the gravy and adds a toasted-corn note; no beans, and only a spoonful of tomato paste. It is better on the second day.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Salt the beef at least 1 hour and up to 24 hours ahead (covered, in the fridge). Longer is better: the salt seasons the cubes all the way through and helps them hold moisture during the long braise.
  • Make the chili a day before you serve it if you can. Overnight in the fridge the chile flavor rounds out, the gravy thickens, and the fat sets on top where it is easy to lift off if you want a leaner bowl.
  • The braise needs about 3 hours in a low oven. Start the chiles soaking before you begin browning the beef; they need 20 minutes and you will be busy at the stove.

Step 1 · Salt the beef

  1. 1800 g beef chuck roast, trimmed, trimmed of thick fat and silverskin, cut into 2.5 cm cubes
  2. 18 g kosher salt

Step 2 · Chile purée

  1. 50 g dried ancho chiles, stemmed, seeded, torn flat — (4 chiles)
  2. 35 g dried guajillo chiles, stemmed, seeded, torn flat — (6 chiles)
  3. 20 g dried pasilla chiles, stemmed, seeded, torn flat — (3 chiles)
  4. 4 g dried árbol chiles, stemmed (seeds left in) — (4 chiles for medium heat; use 2 for mild, 8 for hot)
  5. 4 g cumin seeds
  6. 1000 ml boiling water, for soaking
  7. 30 g garlic cloves, peeled — (8 cloves)
  8. 2 g Mexican oregano
  9. 2 g black pepper, freshly ground
  10. 500 ml beef broth, for blending

Step 3 · Brown and braise

  1. 45 g lard
  2. 300 g yellow onion, cut into 1 cm dice — (1 large)
  3. 1 × 6 oz / 170 g can tomato paste

  5. 6 g dark brown sugar

Step 4 · Thicken and finish

  1. 25 g masa harina
  2. 120 ml cool water, for the masa
  3. 15 ml apple cider vinegar
  4. 3 g kosher salt, to adjust

Step 5 · To serve

  1. 100 g white onion, finely diced, rinsed in cold water and drained — (½ medium)
  2. 2 limes, cut into wedges

Method

  1. 1
    Salt the beef. Toss the 1800 g cubed chuck with the 18 g / 2 tbsp kosher salt in a large bowl, cover with a fitted lid or a plate, and refrigerate for at least 1 hour and up to 24 hours. This is the main seasoning for the meat; the purée and gravy get only small adjustments later.
  2. 2
    Toast the chiles and cumin. Heat a dry cast-iron skillet over medium heat. Working in batches, press the 50 g ancho, 35 g guajillo, 20 g pasilla, and 4 g árbol chiles flat against the pan with a metal spatula for about 15 seconds per side, until they smell toasty, blister slightly, and turn a shade darker. Pull them the moment they smell sharp or show black spots: scorched chiles turn the whole pot bitter, and that cannot be fixed later. Toast the 4 g / 2 tsp cumin seeds in the same pan for about 1 minute, shaking, until fragrant and a shade darker, then tip them into the blender jar. Technique: Dry-toasting (grains, spices, seeds, dried chilies)
  3. 3
    Soak. Put the toasted chiles in a heatproof bowl, pour over the 1000 ml boiling water, and weigh them down with a small plate so they stay submerged. Soak 20 minutes, until fully soft and pliable. Drain and discard the soaking water, which carries most of the bitterness from the skins.
  4. 4
    Brown the beef while the chiles soak. Heat the oven to 150 °C / 300 °F. Pat the salted cubes dry. Heat 15 g / 1 tbsp of the lard in a large Dutch oven over medium-high heat until it shimmers. Add a third of the beef in a single layer with space between the pieces and brown well on two sides, about 6 minutes, without moving them in between. Transfer to a bowl and repeat with the remaining 30 g / 2 tbsp lard and beef in two more batches. Crowding the pot makes the beef steam gray instead of browning, and the brown crust and fond are much of the flavor. Technique: Maillard sear
  5. 5
    Blend the purée. Put the drained chiles in the blender with the toasted cumin, the 30 g / 8 cloves garlic, 2 g / 2 tsp Mexican oregano, 2 g / 1 tsp black pepper, and 500 ml / 2 cups blending beef broth. Blend on high for 2 full minutes until completely smooth. For the silkiest gravy, push the purée through a medium-mesh sieve with a wooden spoon to remove bits of skin; with a high-powered blender you can skip this.
  6. 6
    Cook the onion and the purée. Lower the heat under the empty pot to medium and add the 300 g diced onion with a splash of water to lift the fond. Cook, scraping the bottom with a wooden spoon, about 6 minutes, until soft and golden. Add the 15 g / 1 tbsp tomato paste and stir 1 minute until it darkens. Pour in the chile purée and cook, stirring constantly, for 5 minutes, until it darkens to brick red and thickens to a paste that spatters. Frying the purée in the fat cooks off the raw chile edge and deepens the color, the same move used for Mexican adobos and moles. Technique: Blooming aromatics
  7. 7
    Braise. Return the beef and any juices to the pot with the 500 ml / 2 cups braising beef broth and the 6 g / 1½ tsp dark brown sugar. The liquid should come nearly to the top of the meat; add water if needed. Bring to a simmer, cover with the lid slightly ajar, and move to the 150 °C / 300 °F oven. Braise 2½ to 3 hours, stirring once an hour, until a cube breaks apart easily under a spoon. The oven heats evenly from all sides, so the bottom does not scorch the way it can on a burner.
  8. 8
    Thicken with masa. Move the pot to the stovetop over medium heat. Whisk the 25 g / 3 tbsp masa harina into the 120 ml / ½ cup cool water until smooth, stir it into the chili, and simmer 10 minutes, stirring often. The masa thickens the gravy to a glossy coat and adds a toasted-corn flavor that flour or cornstarch does not.
  9. 9
    Finish and season. Off the heat, stir in the 15 ml / 1 tbsp apple cider vinegar. Taste a piece of beef with some gravy and add up to 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt if it tastes flat. The vinegar should not be detectable as sour; it brightens the chiles and cuts the richness of the beef fat. If serving later, cool, cover, and refrigerate (see the make-ahead note).
  10. 10
    Serve. Reheat gently if made ahead, skimming the fat if you like. Ladle into bowls and top each with some of the 100 g rinsed diced white onion, with the 2 limes in wedges at the table for squeezing. Saltine crackers or warm corn tortillas are the traditional sides.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Heat chiles→smoky, with chipotle

    Replace the 4 árbol chiles with 2 dried chipotle (morita) chiles, toasted and soaked with the rest. The heat drops slightly and a campfire smokiness runs through the gravy.

  • Occasion beef→chili gravy for enchiladas (Tex-Mex)

    Cut the beef into 1 cm dice instead of cubes and add 250 ml / 1 cup more beef broth before braising; braise 2 hours. The result is looser and spoons over cheese enchiladas or a plate of tamales.

  • Regional beans→with pinto beans

    Texas chili traditionally has none, but if you want them, serve 500 g cooked pinto beans warm on the side so everyone can add their own. Simmering them in the pot dilutes the chile gravy and thickens it unevenly.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • dried pasilla chiles → more ancho chiles Use the same weight. Pasilla adds a dark, raisin-like bitterness; without it the chili is a little sweeter and simpler.
  • dried guajillo chiles → dried New Mexico or California chiles Same weight. New Mexico chiles are earthier, California chiles milder; both give the same bright red color.
  • lard → beef tallow or neutral oil Tallow is the most Texan choice and adds beefy depth. Neutral oil works but the chili tastes slightly leaner.
  • Mexican oregano → Mediterranean (Greek) oregano Use 1.5 g. It is more piney and less citrusy, but in a long-cooked chile stew the difference is small.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → beef broth, for blending make it yourself Homemade roasted-bone broth adds gelatin and a deeper beef flavor; carton beef broth is often thin and tastes mostly of yeast extract.
  • → beef broth, for the braise make it yourself The braise reduces by nearly half, so the gelatin in a homemade broth gives the finished gravy noticeably more body.

Notes

What makes it Texas chili. The bowl of red that San Antonio's chili stands sold in the late 1800s was beef stewed with dried chiles, garlic, cumin, and oregano, and the cook-off tradition that grew out of it still bans beans. This version stays inside those lines. The one spoonful of tomato paste is there for savory depth and color, not tomato flavor; leave it out for the strictest version and nothing else changes.

Whole chiles instead of chili powder. Commercial chili powder is ground chile mixed with cumin, garlic powder, and oregano, and it often tastes stale and dusty. Toasting whole pods wakes up their aromatic oils, and blending them gives a gravy that tastes of dried fruit, coffee, and leather rather than one flat note. Each chile has a job: ancho brings sweetness and body, guajillo brings bright red color and a tangy fruitiness, pasilla adds a bitter-chocolate depth, and árbol carries the heat.

Cumin in moderation. Too much cumin is the most common way chili goes wrong; it takes over and makes the pot taste like taco seasoning. Two teaspoons of whole seed for 1.8 kg of beef keeps it in the background.

Glutamate and acid. Browned beef, beef broth, and the small amount of tomato paste supply the savory base, and the dried chiles themselves are rich in glutamate. A tablespoon of cider vinegar at the end is a long-standing Texas move; lime at the table is the second acid for those who want it brighter.

Why chuck, and why cubes. Chuck has enough fat and collagen to stay moist through 3 hours of braising and turns tender as the collagen melts. Cubed meat gives chili a texture ground beef cannot: chunks that hold together on the spoon and fall apart in the mouth. Ground beef also never browns as deeply in a pot this full.

Gluten-free note. Masa harina is corn. If you use carton beef broth, check the label, since a few brands contain wheat.
