Recipe

Japanese chicken curry rice (kare raisu)

Salted chicken thighs seared in a Dutch oven, onions caramelized in the fond to deep amber, then carrot, potato, grated apple, and chicken stock simmered until tender and thickened with a from-scratch butter-flour roux cooked to golden brown with curry powder and garam masala bloomed in it. Thick, glossy, gently sweet, and savory, served beside short-grain rice with fukujinzuke.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Wash and soak the rice first. It needs 30 minutes of soaking, about 15 minutes on the stove, and 10 minutes of rest with the lid on; start it so it finishes while the curry simmers after the roux goes in.
  • The onions take about 30 minutes of mostly hands-off stirring to caramelize. Peel and cut the carrot and potato, grate the apple, garlic, and ginger, and measure the roux ingredients during that window.
  • Curry tastes better the next day, after the spices and onion sugars settle into the sauce. If you make it ahead, cool it quickly in a shallow glass container (see the notes) and refrigerate it within 2 hours.

Method

  1. 1
    Wash the rice. Put the 450 g / 3 rice-cooker cups Japanese short-grain rice in a bowl, cover with cold tap water, swirl with your fingers for 10 seconds, and pour the cloudy water off immediately (dry rice absorbs the first rinse, so it should not sit). Repeat 3 or 4 times until the water is only slightly hazy. Drain in a sieve, transfer to a heavy saucepan with the 600 ml / 2½ cups cold water, and soak 30 minutes so the grains hydrate to the core before they cook.
  2. 2
    Salt the chicken. Toss the 600 g chicken thigh pieces with the 6 g / 2 tsp kosher salt in a bowl and leave them on the counter while you slice the onions, at least 15 minutes. The salt dissolves into the surface proteins, seasons the meat through, and helps it hold its juices during the long simmer.
  3. 3
    Sear the chicken. Heat a 5 to 6 quart Dutch oven over medium-high heat, add the 15 g / 1 tbsp neutral oil, and lay in the chicken in one layer (two batches if it crowds). Sear without moving for 3 minutes until the underside is deep golden, turn, and sear 2 minutes more. It will not be cooked through; that happens in the simmer. Move the chicken to a plate, leaving the fat and browned fond in the pot. Technique: Maillard sear
  4. 4
    Caramelize the onions. Lower the heat to medium, add the 15 g / 1 tbsp butter, the 500 g / 2 large sliced onions, and the 2 g / ½ tsp kosher salt. The salt draws water out of the onions, which dissolves the chicken fond. Cook, stirring every 2 to 3 minutes with a wooden spoon, for about 30 minutes. Each time a brown film builds on the bottom of the pot, add a splash (about 15 ml / 1 tbsp) of the 60 ml / ¼ cup water and scrape it up; the browned sugars go back into the onions. Stop when the onions are a soft, jammy, deep amber and have shrunk to roughly a quarter of their raw volume. These onions are most of the sweetness and depth in the finished sauce, so do not rush them over high heat, which scorches the edges before the centers soften. Technique: Deglazing
  5. 5
    Cook the rice while the onions caramelize. Bring the soaked rice and its 600 ml / 2½ cups water to a boil over high heat with the lid on (about 5 minutes; listen for the rattle and watch for steam). Turn the heat to the lowest setting and cook 13 minutes without lifting the lid, then turn the heat off and let it steam, still covered, 10 minutes more. Fluff with a rice paddle by cutting and folding, and keep covered until serving.
  6. 6
    Add the aromatics. Push the onions aside, add the 10 g / 3 cloves grated garlic, the 10 g grated ginger, and the 15 g / 1 tbsp tomato paste to the cleared spot, and cook 90 seconds, stirring, until the garlic smells sweet rather than sharp and the tomato paste darkens from red to brick. Frying the paste concentrates its glutamates and cooks off its raw, tinny edge.
  7. 7
    Simmer the vegetables and chicken. Drain the potatoes. Add the 150 g carrot, the 350 g potatoes, the 800 ml / 3⅓ cups chicken stock, and the 100 g grated apple to the pot, then return the chicken with any juices from the plate. Bring to a boil, skim off the gray foam that rises in the first few minutes, and simmer gently, lid ajar, for 20 minutes, until a knife slides into the potato and carrot with no resistance. The chicken will be well past 74 °C / 165 °F internal by now; thigh is at its most tender around 80 °C, so the long simmer suits it. Grated apple dissolves into the sauce and adds fruit sugar and malic acid that round the spice.
  8. 8
    Make the roux while the vegetables simmer. Melt the 60 g / 4 tbsp butter in a small heavy saucepan over medium-low heat. Add the 60 g / ½ cup flour and stir constantly with a wooden spoon, scraping the corners. It starts as a pale paste, loosens, and slowly colors. Cook 15 minutes, until it is the color of peanut butter and smells of toasted biscuit. Browning the flour builds a nutty, roasted flavor (at some cost to its thickening power, which the quantity accounts for). If it darkens unevenly or smells sharp, the heat is too high; lower it.
  9. 9
    Bloom the spices. Take the roux off the heat and stir in the 16 g / 2½ tbsp curry powder, the 4 g / 2 tsp garam masala, and the 1 g / ½ tsp cayenne if using. The pan's residual heat is enough: stir 60 seconds until the paste turns a deep golden brown and smells intensely of cumin and toasted coriander. The aromatic compounds in curry spices are fat-soluble, so the butter draws them out and carries them through the sauce. Do not put the pan back on the burner, since ground spices scorch and turn bitter within seconds at frying temperature. Technique: Blooming aromatics
  10. 10
    Thicken the curry. Ladle about 240 ml / 1 cup of the simmering liquid from the pot into the roux and whisk until smooth and lump-free; the roux will seize at first, then loosen into a thick, glossy paste. Turn the heat under the pot to low and stir the loosened roux back in. Simmer 10 minutes, stirring the bottom with the wooden spoon every minute or so, since the thickened sauce sticks and scorches easily. The flour starch needs this time at a simmer to swell fully and lose its raw taste. The curry is ready when it coats the spoon thickly and slides off slowly in a ribbon.
  11. 11
    Season and finish. Stir in the 18 g / 1 tbsp soy sauce, then taste. The sauce should read savory, gently sweet from the onions and apple, and warm rather than hot. Add up to the 2 g / ½ tsp kosher salt if it tastes flat; homemade unsalted stock usually needs all of it. Turn off the heat and stir in the 15 g / 1 tbsp Worcestershire sauce last: its vinegar and tamarind give the finishing acidity that keeps the rich sauce from tasting heavy, and adding it off the heat keeps that acidity bright. Let the curry stand 10 minutes, during which it thickens a little more.
  12. 12
    Serve. Spoon about 250 g / 1¼ cups rice onto one side of each wide, shallow plate and ladle the curry beside it, so each bite can take as much sauce as the eater wants. Put 15 g / 1 tbsp fukujinzuke at the edge of each plate if using; its sweet-salty crunch and acidity cut through the sauce.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Protein protein→beef curry (Kansai style)

    Replace the chicken with 600 g / 1⅓ lb beef chuck in 4 cm cubes, salted the same way. Sear hard, caramelize the onions, then add the stock and simmer the beef alone, lid ajar, for 75 minutes until nearly tender before adding the carrot, potato, and apple and continuing as written. Add 120 ml / ½ cup extra water to make up for evaporation. Beef curry is the default in western Japan (Osaka, Kobe); pork shoulder treated the same way, simmered 45 minutes alone, is the default around Tokyo.

  • Occasion occasion→katsu curry

    Make half the chicken (or leave it out) and serve the curry over sliced tonkatsu on the rice. The crisp panko crust against the thick sauce is the most popular way to eat curry in Japanese curry shops; ladle the sauce beside the cutlet, not over it, so part of the crust stays crisp.

  • Heat heat level→hot (karakuchi)

    Raise the cayenne to 3 g / 1½ tsp and add 2 g / 1 tsp freshly ground black pepper to the roux with the other spices. Keep the apple; sweetness is what lets the heat read as warmth rather than burn.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • Yukon Gold potatoes → russet potatoes Russets break down at the edges and thicken the sauce further, a style many Japanese households prefer (it mirrors the floury Danshaku potato). Expect a slightly thicker, more rustic curry.
  • sweet-tart apple → honey Use 10 g / 1½ tsp honey stirred in with the soy sauce. You lose the fruit acidity, so taste for brightness after the Worcestershire.
  • fukujinzuke → rakkyo (pickled Japanese shallots) The other standard curry pickle: sweet-sour, crunchy, sharper. Serve 2 or 3 bulbs per plate, sliced or whole.
  • Japanese curry powder → Madras curry powder Madras blends carry more chili and less sweet spice. Use the same weight and leave out the cayenne.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → unsalted chicken stock make it yourself Gelatin-rich homemade stock gives the sauce more body and lets you control the salt, which matters in a sauce reduced and seasoned with soy and Worcestershire.
  • → garam masala make it yourselfstub Freshly toasted and ground garam masala is far more aromatic than a jar that has been open for months, and it is the top note of the roux.
  • → fukujinzuke (soy-pickled vegetable relish) make it yourselfstub Commercial fukujinzuke is often dyed bright red and very sweet; a homemade batch keeps the daikon and lotus root crunchy and lets you set the sweetness.

Notes

Why make the roux. Boxed roux blocks (Golden Curry, Vermont Curry, Java Curry) are mostly fat, flour, sugar, salt, and flavor enhancers with a modest dose of spice. A homemade roux takes 15 minutes while the vegetables simmer, uses ingredients you already have, and lets the spices taste of themselves. The sweetness and savoriness the blocks add chemically come here from the caramelized onions, the grated apple, the tomato paste, the soy sauce, and the Worcestershire.

Roux color and thickness. The longer a flour roux browns, the more starch is broken down and the less it thickens. Peanut-butter brown is the balance point: nutty and roasted, with enough thickening power to give the classic spoon-coating curry with 60 g of flour to 800 ml of stock. If you take it darker, add 10 g more flour and butter. If the finished curry is too thick, loosen it with stock or water; if too thin, simmer uncovered a few minutes longer, and the potato starch will help.

Glutamate and acid. Tomato paste, soy sauce, Worcestershire, and the onion fond supply the savory depth. The finishing acid is the Worcestershire sauce, added off the heat, backed by the malic acid in the apple. Japanese cooks often keep a bottle of usutā sauce on the table for people to add more, and the pickle on the side does the same job.

Cooling leftovers safely. A big pot of thick, starchy stew cools slowly, and the spore-forming bacterium Clostridium perfringens multiplies in exactly that slow cool; leftover curry left in its pot overnight is a known cause of food poisoning in Japan. Transfer leftovers to a shallow glass container so the layer is no deeper than 5 cm, cool it uncovered on the counter for no more than an hour, then refrigerate. Reheat until the curry bubbles all the way through, stirring the bottom. It keeps 3 days in the refrigerator. It freezes well without the potato, which turns grainy when frozen; for a freezer batch, leave the potato out and add freshly boiled potato when reheating.

Rice. Japanese short-grain rice is stickier and more tender than long-grain rice, and holds the thick sauce on the spoon. Washing removes surface starch so the grains stay distinct, and the 10-minute rest lets the moisture even out through each grain. A rice cooker works the same way: use the same rice and the white-rice line for 3 cups.

Sources
  • Japanese home curry (kare raisu), a yōshoku dish that arrived in Meiji-era Japan through British-style curry powder and became a national staple through the navy, school lunches, and boxed roux blocks. — Most Japanese households now use a commercial roux block. This version builds the roux from butter, flour, curry powder, and garam masala, which is how the dish was made before the blocks existed and still how many curry specialists make it. It trades the blocks' added sugar, palm fat, and flavor enhancers for a cleaner, more aromatic sauce you season yourself.
