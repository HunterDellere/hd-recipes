Chocolate Texas sheet cake

A thin, fudgy half-sheet chocolate cake built from a boiled butter-coffee-cocoa base and buttermilk, then flooded while still warm with a poured brown-butter cocoa icing and toasted pecans. The warm-on-warm pour is the defining move: the icing sinks a few millimeters into the crumb and sets into a soft fudge layer rather than sitting on top as frosting.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • The icing must go onto the cake within about 5 minutes of it leaving the oven. Toast the pecans and measure every icing ingredient while the cake bakes, then start browning the butter when the timer has about 6 minutes left.
  • Sift the powdered sugar before you start. Lumps will not dissolve in a pourable icing, and there is no time to sieve once the butter is brown.

Method

  1. 1
    Heat the oven to 350°F (177°C) with a rack centered. Butter an 18 x 13-inch rimmed half-sheet pan with 1 tsp / 5 g of the butter. Spread the pecan halves (1 cup / 115 g) on a small tray and toast alongside the preheating oven for 7 to 9 minutes, until they smell roasted and the cut faces darken a shade. Chop coarse and set aside for the icing.
  2. 2
    In a large bowl, whisk the all-purpose flour (2 cups / 250 g), granulated sugar (2 cups / 400 g), baking soda (½ tsp / 3 g), baking powder (1 tsp / 4 g), fine sea salt (½ tsp / 3 g), and ground cinnamon (½ tsp / 1 g). The cinnamon should not be identifiable; at this dose it reads as warmth behind the chocolate.
  3. 3
    In a medium saucepan, bring the remaining butter (1 cup / 225 g), brewed coffee (1 cup / 240 g), and Dutch-process cocoa (⅓ cup / 30 g) to a full boil over medium heat, whisking. Boiling is the bloom: the near-100°C liquid swells the cocoa's starch and frees the fat-bound aromatics that stay dusty in a cold batter, and the coffee's roasted pyrazines overlap with the cocoa's so the chocolate reads deeper, not coffee-flavored. Technique: Blooming cocoa
  4. 4
    Pour the boiling cocoa mixture over the dry ingredients and whisk until smooth. The heat partially gelatinizes the flour starch, which is why this cake bakes fudgy and dense-crumbed rather than fluffy. In a small bowl, whisk the buttermilk (½ cup / 120 g), the eggs (2 large / 100 g), and vanilla bean paste (1 tsp / 5 g), then whisk into the warm batter in one addition until no streaks remain. The batter will be thin and pourable.
  5. 5
    Pour into the prepared pan, tilt to level, and bake 18 to 22 minutes, until the center springs back and a tester comes out with a few moist crumbs, or the center reads 205°F (96°C). A sheet this thin overshoots fast; pull it at the first clean spring-back.
  6. 6
    While the cake bakes, brown the butter for the icing: melt the unsalted butter (½ cup / 115 g) in a light-colored saucepan over medium heat and cook, swirling, until the foam subsides and the milk solids turn hazelnut brown, 4 to 6 minutes. Time it so the butter finishes as the cake comes out. Technique: Brown butter (beurre noisette)
  7. 7
    Off heat, whisk the Dutch-process cocoa (¼ cup / 22 g) into the brown butter, then the whole milk (7 tbsp / 105 g); it will sputter. Return to medium heat just until it bubbles at the edges. Off heat, whisk in the sifted powdered sugar (about 3⅓ cups / 400 g), vanilla bean paste (1 tsp / 5 g), and fine sea salt (¼ tsp / 1.5 g) until glossy, then stir in the chopped pecans. The extra tablespoon of milk replaces the water lost while browning.
  8. 8
    Pour the hot icing over the hot cake immediately and spread it to the edges with an offset spatula in a few strokes. Warm crumb plus hot icing lets the surface fudge into the top of the cake; icing a cool cake leaves a separate, crackly shell. Sprinkle with flaky sea salt (2 pinches / 1 g) while it is still wet.
  9. 9
    Cool in the pan at least 45 minutes before cutting, until the icing sets to a soft matte skin. Cut into 24 squares.
  10. 10
    The Adjustment: if the icing seizes thick before you can spread it, whisk in 1 tbsp / 15 g warm milk and work faster next time. If it runs off the edges, it was too thin or the cake was not level; pull back to 6 tbsp milk. If the cake is dry at the edges, it baked past 205°F (96°C) or the pan is dark; drop to 325°F (163°C) in a dark pan. If the whole thing reads too sweet, add the second pinch of flaky salt and cut smaller squares rather than cutting sugar, which tenderizes the crumb.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Texture pan size→10 x 15-inch jelly-roll pan (thicker, cakier)

    Same batter and icing. The layer is about twice as deep, so bake 25 to 30 minutes at 350°F (177°C) and pull at 205°F (96°C) in the center. Expect more cake than fudge per bite.

  • Dietary nuts→nut-free

    Omit the pecans and finish with the flaky salt only. The toasted-nut crunch is the main texture contrast, so a scatter of toasted pepitas or cacao nibs is the closest swap.

  • Dietary caffeine→water bloom

    Replace the coffee with 1 cup / 240 g boiling water. Traditional and still good; the chocolate reads a step lighter, so raise the cake cocoa to 7 tbsp / 35 g.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • Dutch-process cocoa → natural cocoa (same weights) The original Texas sheet cake uses it: lighter, redder, tangier. In the cake, drop the baking powder and raise the baking soda to 1 tsp / 6 g, since natural cocoa supplies its own acid. No change to the icing.
  • buttermilk → ½ cup / 120 g whole milk with 1½ tsp lemon juice or white vinegar Rest 5 minutes to clabber. Same acid for the soda, slightly thinner body.
  • vanilla bean paste → vanilla extract (same amount in each phase) 1:1 by volume. Fine in a pinch, but you lose the seeds and some aromatic depth, most noticeably in the icing.
  • pecan halves → walnuts More tannic and bitter. Toast the same way; the brown-butter icing softens the edge.

Notes

Why boil the base. Every Texas sheet cake starts by boiling butter, liquid, and cocoa, and the step is doing more than melting. Hot liquid blooms the cocoa, and pouring it over the flour pre-gelatinizes some of the starch, so the batter bakes into a dense, moist, almost brownie-adjacent crumb in a 20-minute bake. Hot coffee in place of water is the cheapest depth upgrade in chocolate baking: its roast compounds overlap with cocoa''s and amplify it.

Dutch cocoa and the leavening. The classic uses natural cocoa with only baking soda, because the cocoa''s acid is what the soda reacts against. Dutch-process cocoa has been alkalized, so that acid is gone; keeping a full teaspoon of soda would leave the cake soapy and over-browned. The fix is half the soda (enough for the buttermilk and coffee acid) plus baking powder, which carries its own acid. The payoff is a darker, rounder chocolate flavor.

Brown butter in the icing only. The cake butter is boiled with coffee and cocoa, where browning would be lost. The icing butter is the one you taste directly, and its hazelnut Maillard notes land on the toasted pecans. Browning drives off roughly 15% of the butter''s weight as water, which is why the icing carries 7 tbsp milk instead of the usual 6.

Warm on warm. The defining texture is the fudge seam where icing meets cake. Both must be hot: the icing sugar is still dissolved, and the cake surface is open and steaming, so the two fuse as they cool. Let it set fully before cutting or the seam smears.

Storage. Covered at room temperature 3 days; the crumb stays moist because the icing seals the top. Freezes well in squares, wrapped, for 2 months.
