Recipe

Roasted butternut squash salad with radicchio, pecans, and balsamic

Hard-roasted butternut cubes, dressed hot so they drink the vinaigrette, laid over radicchio and arugula with salted toasted pecans, crisped sage, and shaved parmigiano. Sweetness comes only from the caramelized squash and the balsamic; bitter leaves, a red wine vinegar spine, and a salty umami finish keep it a savory side rather than a dessert on a plate.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Put two half-sheet pans in the oven as it preheats to 230 °C / 450 °F. A hot pan is what browns the squash instead of steaming it, and 1.2 kg of cubes needs two pans to stay in a single layer.
  • Thanksgiving oven math: the squash can roast up to a day ahead and be refrigerated, then rewarmed on a sheet pan for 8 minutes at whatever temperature the turkey or sides are using. The vinaigrette keeps a week. Toast the pecans and crisp the sage the morning of. Only the leaves and the final toss happen at the table.
  • Taste your balsamic straight from a spoon before you build the dressing. A thin supermarket IGP, a thick aged one, and a flavored bottle can differ by a factor of three in sugar. The vinaigrette step tells you how to adjust the red wine vinegar to match.

What to prep, in order 4 phases ▾

Step 1 · Roasted squash

  1. 1600 g butternut squash, peeled with a Y-peeler until the flesh is uniformly deep orange (no pale green streaks), seeded, cut into 2 cm cubes
  2. 40 g extra-virgin olive oil
  3. 10 g kosher salt
  4. 2 g black pepper, freshly cracked
  5. 1 g red pepper flakes
  6. 6 g fresh sage leaves — (about 20 leaves, picked off the stem)

Step 2 · Balsamic-shallot vinaigrette

  1. 30 g shallot, finely minced
  2. 50 g balsamic vinegar
  3. 15 g red wine vinegar
  4. 10 g dijon mustard
  5. 3 g kosher salt
  6. 120 g extra-virgin olive oil

Step 3 · Salted pecans

  1. 90 g pecan halves
  2. 5 g extra-virgin olive oil
  3. 1 g flaky salt — (a generous pinch)

Step 4 · Leaves and finish

  1. 250 g radicchio, cored, leaves torn into 4 cm pieces — (1 large head of Chioggia, or 2 small)
  2. 120 g baby arugula
  3. 50 g parmigiano reggiano, shaved into wide curls with a vegetable peeler
  4. 1 g flaky salt — (a pinch, to finish)

Method

  1. 1
    Set two racks in the upper and lower thirds of the oven, slide a half-sheet pan onto each, and heat to 230 °C / 450 °F for at least 15 minutes. Meanwhile peel, seed, and cube the 1600 g / 2 medium butternut squash. Peel past the first pale layer until the flesh is solid orange; the greenish band under the skin stays fibrous no matter how long it roasts. Technique: High-heat dry roasting
  2. 2
    Make the vinaigrette base first so the shallot has time to mellow. In a jar, combine the 30 g / 1 medium minced shallot, the 50 g / 3 tbsp balsamic vinegar, the 15 g / 1 tbsp red wine vinegar, and the 3 g / ¾ tsp kosher salt. Let it sit at least 10 minutes while you prep. The acid and salt draw out and partially neutralize the sharp thiosulfinates, so the shallot reads sweet-allium instead of raw and hot, and the salt fully dissolves before any oil arrives to block it.
  3. 3
    In a large bowl, toss the cubed squash with the 40 g / 3 tbsp olive oil, the 10 g / 2½ tsp kosher salt, the 2 g / ½ tsp black pepper, and the 1 g / ½ tsp red pepper flakes until every face is glossed. Salting now, before heat, pulls a thin film of moisture to the surface that dissolves the salt into the flesh and evaporates within the first minutes of roasting, so the cubes season through rather than just on the crust.
  4. 4
    Pull the hot pans and divide the squash between them in a single layer, cut faces down, with a finger-width gap between cubes. It should sizzle on contact. Roast 15 minutes undisturbed, then flip with a thin metal spatula and swap the pans top to bottom. Scatter the 6 g / about 20 sage leaves over the squash, pressing them flat into the oil on the pan. Technique: High-heat dry roasting
  5. 5
    Roast another 10 to 14 minutes, until the cubes are fork-tender with deep mahogany edges on at least two faces and the 6 g / 20 sage leaves are crisp and dark green (pull any leaves early if they start to brown). Do not stop at pale orange and soft: the browned surface is where the savory pyrazines and the bitter edge of caramelized sugar live, and that bitterness is half of what keeps this salad from tasting sweet. Technique: High-heat dry roasting
  6. 6
    While the squash finishes, toast the 90 g / ¾ cup pecans in a dry skillet over medium-low, shaking often, 5 to 7 minutes until they smell buttery and the cut faces darken one shade. Off heat, toss with the 5 g / 1 tsp olive oil and the 1 g pinch of flaky salt. The oil gives the salt something to stick to; salted rather than candied pecans are a deliberate choice here. Technique: Dry-toasting (grains, spices, seeds, dried chilies)
  7. 7
    Finish the vinaigrette. Add the 10 g / 2 tsp dijon mustard to the macerated shallot and stir. Add the 120 g / ½ cup + 1 tbsp olive oil, cap the jar, and shake hard for 20 seconds until thick and opaque; the mustard mucilage holds the emulsion. Taste it on a radicchio leaf, not a spoon. It should land sharp first, then fruity, then mellow. If it reads sweet or syrupy (common with flavored or thick aged balsamic), add red wine vinegar 5 g / 1 tsp at a time until it bites; if it reads harsh, a few more drops of olive oil. Technique: Vinaigrette emulsion
  8. 8
    As soon as the squash comes out, crumble most of the crisp sage over it, then spoon 45 g / 3 tbsp of the vinaigrette over the hot squash on the pans and toss gently. Hot, porous squash absorbs the dressing into the outer layer the way warm potatoes do in a potato salad, so the acid seasons the flesh rather than sliding off the surface. Let it cool 10 minutes; hot squash wilts arugula on contact.
  9. 9
    In your widest bowl, toss the 250 g / 1 large head torn radicchio and the 120 g / 5 cups arugula with about 60 g / 4 tbsp of the remaining vinaigrette until lightly but completely coated. Leaves should glisten, not pool. Radicchio takes dressing well and stays crisp for an hour; arugula goes limp within 20 minutes of dressing, so do this right before it goes to the table.
  10. 10
    Spread the dressed leaves on a platter. Lay the warm squash over them (do not toss it through, the cubes break and smear), then scatter the salted pecans, the 50 g / ½ cup parmigiano curls, and the reserved sage. Finish with the 1 g pinch of flaky salt and a last 15 g / 1 tbsp drizzle of vinaigrette over the squash. Put the leftover vinaigrette in a small pitcher on the table.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Occasion make-ahead for a big table→buffet-stable version

    Drop the arugula and double the radicchio (500 g), or swap in 250 g of shaved Brussels sprouts or ribboned lacinato kale massaged with the 3 g salt. Sturdy bitter leaves hold under dressing for 2 hours on a buffet, where arugula is limp in 20 minutes. Dress the leaves 30 minutes ahead; add the squash, nuts, and cheese at service.

  • Texture cheese→tangy-creamy with goat cheese or gorgonzola

    Replace the parmigiano with 100 g crumbled fresh goat cheese or 80 g gorgonzola dolce, dotted over the finished platter in small pieces. Both trade the salty-glutamate crunch for lactic tang and cream, which plays well against the squash. Keep the salt in the pecans; the softer cheeses are less salty than parmigiano.

  • Occasion grain→main-course grain salad

    Fold 200 g dry farro or wild rice, cooked and dressed warm with 45 g / 3 tbsp of the vinaigrette, under the leaves. The dish becomes a full vegetarian main for 4 to 6 and holds better at room temperature.

  • Texture finish→balsamic glaze drizzle

    If you have a sharp, plain balsamic and want a visual finish, reduce 120 g to about 40 g (see the balsamic glaze recipe) and draw a thin line over the squash only. Skip this with flavored or thick aged balsamics; the glaze would push the plate past the sweetness target.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • butternut squash → kabocha (peeled) or delicata (skin on, 1 cm half-moons) Kabocha is drier and nuttier and browns faster; check at 20 minutes total. Delicata needs no peeling and roasts in about 22 minutes. Both are slightly sweeter than butternut, so lean on the red wine vinegar.
  • radicchio → endive, frisée, or escarole Any bitter chicory does the same job. Belgian endive (sliced into 1 cm rings) is milder and crunchier; frisée is the most bitter and holds dressing longest.
  • pecan halves → walnuts, hazelnuts (skinned), or pepitas Walnuts bring tannic bitterness that suits the radicchio. Toasted pepitas make the salad nut-free and add a savory crunch; toast 4 minutes, they pop and color fast.
  • parmigiano reggiano → pecorino romano or aged gouda Pecorino is saltier and sharper; drop the finishing flaky salt. Aged gouda adds butterscotch notes, which works but nudges the salad sweeter, so add the extra red wine vinegar.
  • red wine vinegar → sherry vinegar or lemon juice Sherry vinegar adds a nutty depth that pairs well with the pecans. Lemon is brighter and less integrated but works if it is what you have.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → red wine vinegar make it yourself Leftover wine plus a mother turns into a live vinegar with far more fruit than most supermarket bottles, and it is the acid holding this whole salad in balance.
  • → dijon mustard make it yourself Fresh-made dijon is hotter and more aromatic than a jar that has been open in the fridge for months, and it keeps for weeks.

Notes

Where the sweetness is, and how it stays in check. Roasted butternut is already about 4 to 5 percent sugar, and high heat concentrates and caramelizes it. That is the sweet side of the dish, and it is enough. There is no maple, honey, dried fruit, or candied nut anywhere. Every other element pushes the other way: radicchio and the browned squash edges for bitter, red wine vinegar for acid, parmigiano and the salted pecans for salt and glutamate, and the chili flakes for a faint warmth on the finish. The parmigiano is the umami anchor; without it the plate reads as sweet and sour with nothing savory underneath.

Using your flavored balsamics. Flavored balsamics range widely in sugar, so treat the 15 g of red wine vinegar as the adjustment knob, not a fixed amount. Good fits for this salad: fig, maple, apple or cider, pear, pomegranate, blood orange, and plain aged dark balsamic. They all echo the squash and work in autumn. Use with care: thick "condimento" style or very sweet dessert flavors (chocolate, raspberry, vanilla). If you use one, double the red wine vinegar to 30 g / 2 tbsp and taste on a leaf. Avoid savory-herb flavors that clash with sage (lemon-thyme is fine, garlic is redundant with the shallot). White balsamic works too and keeps the platter brighter in color, but it is usually sweeter than dark, so add the extra red wine vinegar.

Why dress the squash hot. Starch-rich vegetables absorb liquid best while hot, because steam is still escaping from the surface and pulling liquid back in as it cools. Three tablespoons of vinaigrette on hot squash seasons it all the way through; the same amount on cold squash beads up and runs to the bottom of the bowl.

Thanksgiving logistics. Every component except the dressed leaves holds well: roast the squash the day before, make the vinaigrette up to a week ahead (shake it again before using, the emulsion loosens in the fridge), and toast the nuts and crisp the sage that morning. Rewarm the squash for 8 minutes in any oven between 175 and 230 °C while the turkey rests. Leftover undressed squash and dressing make a good next-day lunch over the same leaves.
