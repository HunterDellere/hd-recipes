Chicken pot pie

Bone-in thighs poached gently in chicken stock, and that enriched poaching liquid becomes the velouté that binds browned mushrooms, carrot, parsnip, celery, and peas. Dry sherry and a squeeze of lemon keep the cream sauce bright, and an all-butter puff pastry lid bakes up crisp over the hot filling.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Frozen puff pastry needs to thaw in the refrigerator overnight (or about 4 hours). Counter-thawed pastry goes soft at the edges while the center is still frozen, and soft butter layers do not puff.
  • The poaching (about 35 minutes) is mostly hands-off. Cut every vegetable while the chicken poaches so the sauce can start the moment the liquid is strained.
  • The filling can be made up to 2 days ahead and refrigerated in the baking dish. Bake cold filling with the pastry on at 200 °C / 400 °F for about 45 minutes, until the center reads 74 °C / 165 °F; the lower temperature keeps the pastry from scorching before the filling heats through.

Method

  1. 1
    Poach. Put the 1400 g skinless thighs in a Dutch oven in one snug layer and add the 1000 ml chicken stock, 6 g / 2 tsp kosher salt, 2 bay leaves, 4 g thyme sprigs, and 2 g / ½ tsp peppercorns. The stock should just cover the meat; top up with water if it does not. Bring to a bare simmer over medium heat, skimming any gray foam, then lower the heat until only a few bubbles break the surface, cover, and cook about 30 minutes, until the thickest thigh reads 82 °C / 180 °F. A hard boil tightens the meat and clouds the liquid, so keep it gentle. The salt here seasons the meat from the inside; the rest of the seasoning goes on later.
  2. 2
    Strain and pull. Lift the thighs onto a board to cool for 10 minutes. Strain the liquid through a fine-mesh sieve into a large measuring jug and discard the aromatics. Let it settle, spoon off the fat from the top, and check the volume: you need 950 ml. Top up with water if short. Pull the meat from the bones in large bite-size pieces (about 3 cm), discarding bones and gristle. Keep the 950 ml poaching liquid hot in a covered pan; hot liquid whisks into the roux without lumps. Heat the oven to 220 °C / 425 °F with a rack in the middle.
  3. 3
    Brown the mushrooms. Wipe out the Dutch oven, set it over medium-high heat, and add the 15 g / 1 tbsp neutral oil. Add the 225 g quartered mushrooms in one layer and leave them alone for 3 minutes, until the undersides are deep brown, then stir and cook 3 more minutes. Season with 2 g / ½ tsp of the vegetable kosher salt at the end; salting earlier draws water and stews them. The browned mushrooms are the main glutamate source in the filling. Technique: Maillard sear
  4. 4
    Sweat the vegetables. Lower the heat to medium, add the 30 g / 2 tbsp butter, then the 250 g onion, 200 g carrots, 200 g parsnip, and 100 g celery with the remaining 3 g / 1 tsp vegetable kosher salt. Cook, stirring with a wooden spoon, about 8 minutes, until the onion is translucent and the carrot edges just give to a knife. They finish cooking in the oven, so stop short of soft. Add the 10 g / 3 cloves minced garlic and cook 30 seconds.
  5. 5
    Deglaze. Pour in the 60 ml / ¼ cup dry sherry and scrape the brown film from the bottom of the pot. Simmer until the pot is almost dry, about 1 minute, which cooks off the raw alcohol and leaves the nutty, oxidized acidity of the sherry behind. Technique: Deglazing
  6. 6
    Make the roux. Add the 70 g / 5 tbsp roux butter to the vegetables and let it melt. Sprinkle the 70 g / ½ cup plus 1 tbsp flour over everything and stir constantly for 2 minutes, until the flour coats the vegetables and smells lightly toasted. Cooking the flour removes its raw cereal taste; it should stay pale.
  7. 7
    Build the velouté. Add the 950 ml hot poaching liquid about 240 ml / 1 cup at a time, stirring well after each addition until smooth before adding more. Bring to a simmer and cook, stirring often and scraping the bottom, for 5 minutes; the starch fully swells only once the sauce simmers, and it should end up thick enough to coat a spoon heavily. Stir in the 120 ml / ½ cup heavy cream, 3 g / 1 tbsp thyme leaves, and 1 g / ½ tsp ground black pepper.
  8. 8
    Finish and season. Take the pot off the heat. Fold in the pulled chicken, the 150 g / 1 cup frozen peas, the 15 ml / 1 tbsp lemon juice, and the 10 g / ¼ cup chopped parsley. Taste and add up to 2 g / ½ tsp kosher salt. The filling should taste slightly over-seasoned and noticeably bright on its own, because the pastry lid mutes it at the table. If it tastes flat, a little more lemon juice usually fixes it before more salt does.
  9. 9
    Fill and rest. Spread the filling into a 23 x 33 cm (9 x 13 inch) or 3-quart baking dish; it should come to within 1 cm of the rim. Set the dish on a sheet pan to catch drips and let it stand 10 minutes. Steam rising off just-boiled filling melts the butter in the pastry before the oven can set it, and melted layers bake flat.
  10. 10
    Top. Unfold the cold 400 g puff pastry sheet on a lightly floured counter and roll it just enough to remove the folds and reach 2 cm larger than the dish on all sides. Lay it over the filling, press the overhang onto the rim, and trim to 1 cm past the edge. Cut four 3 cm vents in the center so steam can escape. Brush the top with the 50 g beaten egg (mixed with 15 ml / 1 tbsp water), keeping it off the cut edges so they can rise, and scatter the 1 g / ¼ tsp flaky salt over it.
  11. 11
    Bake. Bake at 220 °C / 425 °F for 25 minutes, rotating the pan halfway, until the pastry is deep golden brown all over and the filling bubbles through the vents. If the edges brown too fast, lay a strip of foil over them. Pale pastry is underbaked and gummy underneath, so take it to a full brown.
  12. 12
    Rest and serve. Let the pot pie stand 10 minutes so the sauce thickens slightly as it cools from boiling. Break the lid with a large spoon and serve each portion with a piece of pastry on top.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Texture pastry→all-butter pie crust lid

    Replace the puff pastry with one disc of all-butter pie dough (about 350 g), rolled 3 mm thick. Bake at 200 °C / 400 °F for about 35 minutes. The lid is denser and more crumbly, closer to a New England chicken pie, and it holds up better to reheating.

  • Occasion format→individual pot pies

    Divide the filling among six 350 ml ovenproof bowls, cut pastry rounds 2 cm wider than each rim, and press them on. Bake on a sheet pan at 220 °C / 425 °F for about 20 minutes. Each serving gets a full pastry top and the lids stay crisp longer at the table.

  • Protein chicken→leftover roast chicken or turkey

    Skip the poach. Use 900 g pulled cooked meat and 950 ml good chicken stock heated with the thyme sprigs and bay for 15 minutes, then strained. The sauce will be thinner in body, so cook the velouté 3 minutes longer.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • bone-in chicken thighs → boneless, skinless chicken thighs Use 1000 g and poach about 20 minutes to the same 82 °C / 180 °F. The liquid picks up less gelatin, so the sauce is slightly less rich.
  • parsnip → celery root, Yukon Gold potato, or rutabaga Same weight and dice. Celery root is the closest in sweetness; potato gives a starchier, softer filling.
  • dry sherry → dry white wine Use 90 ml / ⅓ cup and reduce the same way. The filling loses the nutty note and leans more on the lemon for acidity.
  • heavy cream → whole milk Use 120 ml. The sauce is a little lighter and less glossy; nothing else changes.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → chicken stock, unsalted or low-sodium make it yourself Homemade stock carries far more gelatin than carton broth, and that gelatin is what gives the velouté its body and gloss.
  • → all-butter puff pastry, thawed make it yourselfstub Rough puff made at home rises nearly as high as classic puff and tastes of real butter, which most supermarket sheets (made with shortening) do not.

Notes

Why poach in stock instead of water. Poaching bone-in thighs in stock for half an hour pulls collagen from the bones and joints into the liquid, which then becomes the sauce. The result tastes of chicken first and flour second, and it sets to a soft gel in the fridge, the sign of a sauce with real body. Plain carton broth thickened with a roux tastes noticeably thinner by comparison.

Why thighs, and why so hot. Breast meat pulled at 74 °C / 165 °F and then baked again goes dry and stringy in a pot pie. Thighs have more fat and connective tissue, so they stay moist through a second cooking and get more tender, not less, when taken to 82 °C / 180 °F.

Glutamate and acid. Browned cremini mushrooms and the reduced chicken stock supply the savory depth. The acid comes in two layers: sherry cooked into the vegetables for a rounded, nutty acidity, and raw lemon juice at the end for lift. Cream sauces read heavy without acid; the lemon should be just detectable, never sour.

Why puff pastry here. An all-butter puff pastry lid is the least work for the most contrast against a soft filling. Check the label: many supermarket sheets are made with vegetable shortening, which bakes up flaky but tastes of nothing. All-butter brands cost more and are worth it.

Leftovers reheat best in a 180 °C / 350 °F oven, covered loosely with foil, for about 20 minutes; the microwave softens the pastry.
