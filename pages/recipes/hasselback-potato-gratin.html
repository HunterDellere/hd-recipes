Recipe

Hasselback potato gratin

Unrinsed Yukon Gold slices tossed in garlic-and-thyme-infused cream with gruyère and parmigiano, then stood upright in tight rows so the exposed edges roast into a crisp, frilled top while the buried half braises creamy. The format splits the gratin into two textures in one dish: a crackling ridge of browned potato and cheese above a soft, starch-thickened base.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Make-ahead path for a one-oven holiday: bake the gratin fully earlier in the day (it needs about 85 minutes at 190 °C / 375 °F), let it rest uncovered at room temperature for no more than 2 hours (the food-safety limit for a cream and cheese dish), then re-crisp it on the top rack for 15 to 20 minutes at 220 °C / 425 °F while the mains roast. If it must wait longer, cool it and refrigerate it, then reheat it covered at 190 °C / 375 °F until the center reads 74 °C / 165 °F, 35 to 45 minutes, and uncover for the last 10 minutes. The re-crisp restores the edges and reheats the center through; the dish tastes better this way than straight from the first bake, because the cream has fully set.
  • Do not rinse the potato slices. The surface starch released by slicing is what thickens the cream into a sauce that clings; rinsed slices give a thin, weeping gratin.
  • Plan at least 15 minutes of rest after any bake before cutting. Straight from the oven the cream is too loose to portion.

  4. 20 g kosher salt
  5. 2 g black pepper, freshly ground
  6. 1 g whole nutmeg, freshly grated
  7. 8 g sherry vinegar

Step 2 · Potatoes and cheese

  1. 15 g unsalted butter, softened
  2. 2000 g Yukon Gold potatoes, peeled and sliced 2 to 3 mm thick on a mandoline, not rinsed
  3. 170 g gruyère, finely grated
  4. 50 g parmigiano reggiano, finely grated

Step 3 · Finish

  1. 6 g chives, finely sliced
  2. 1 g flaky salt

Method

  1. 1
    Infuse the cream. Combine 600 g / 2½ cups heavy cream, 20 g / 5 smashed garlic cloves, 6 g / 8 thyme sprigs, 20 g / 7 tsp kosher salt, 2 g / 1 tsp black pepper, and 1 g / ¼ tsp grated nutmeg in a small saucepan. Bring to a bare simmer over medium heat, then pull off the heat, cover with a lid, and steep 15 minutes. Heat speeds the extraction of the fat-soluble aromatics in garlic and thyme into the butterfat; steeping off the boil keeps the garlic sweet rather than sharp. Strain into a large bowl, pressing on the garlic, and stir in 8 g / 1½ tsp sherry vinegar. Taste it: the cream should taste distinctly salty, almost too salty, because it seasons 2 kg of bland potato.
  2. 2
    Preheat the oven to 190 °C / 375 °F with a rack in the center. Rub a 23 x 33 cm / 9 x 13 inch baking dish (ceramic or glass) with 15 g / 1 tbsp softened butter.
  3. 3
    Slice the potatoes. Peel the 2000 g / 4½ lb Yukon Golds and slice them 2 to 3 mm thick on a mandoline, using the hand guard. Drop the slices straight into the bowl of warm cream as you go and do not rinse them; the starch on the cut surfaces thickens the cream during the bake. Yukon Golds are the right potato because their medium starch holds slice shape while still releasing enough starch to bind.
  4. 4
    Toss. Add 120 g / about 1½ cups of the gruyère and 25 g / ¼ cup of the parmigiano to the bowl and toss with your hands until every slice is coated and the slices separate. Reserve the remaining 50 g / ½ cup gruyère and 25 g / ¼ cup parmigiano for the top.
  5. 5
    Stand the slices upright. Gather the slices into stacks of about 15 to 20, like a deck of cards, and stand each stack on its long edge in the buttered dish, working in rows along the 33 cm length. Pack them tight so the rows hold each other up; the dish should be fully filled with no gaps. Tuck leftover slices into any loose spots. Pour the remaining cream and cheese from the bowl evenly over the top. The cream should come about two-thirds of the way up the slices; the top third stays exposed and is what crisps.
  6. 6
    Bake covered. Cover the dish tightly with foil and bake 40 minutes at 190 °C / 375 °F. The covered phase steams and braises the buried halves of the slices in the cream until a paring knife meets only slight resistance.
  7. 7
    Bake uncovered. Remove the foil and scatter the reserved 50 g / ½ cup gruyère and 25 g / ¼ cup parmigiano over the top. Bake another 35 to 45 minutes, until the exposed edges are deep golden-brown and frilled, the cream bubbles thickly at the sides, and a knife slides through the center with no resistance. Pale edges mean more time; the Maillard browning on the slice edges and the cheese is where most of the flavor lives.
  8. 8
    Rest. Let the gratin stand 15 minutes before cutting. The starch-thickened cream sets as it cools a few degrees; cut too early and the portions slump into a puddle. If following the make-ahead path, rest it uncovered no more than 2 hours at room temperature, then re-crisp 15 to 20 minutes on the top rack at 220 °C / 425 °F.
  9. 9
    Finish. Scatter 6 g / 3 tbsp sliced chives and 1 g / ¼ tsp flaky salt over the top just before serving. Taste an edge and a center piece: if the center reads heavy, a few extra drops of sherry vinegar on the table are the correction, not more salt.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Dietary make it lighter→half cream, half whole milk

    Replace 300 g of the cream with 300 g whole milk. The lower fat makes the sauce more prone to curdling with the vinegar, so stir the vinegar into the finished gratin as a few drops at the table instead of into the cream.

  • Occasion individual portions→muffin-tin Hasselback stacks

    Stand short stacks of the coated slices upright in a buttered 12-cup muffin tin, spoon 2 tbsp cream over each, and bake uncovered at 200 °C / 400 °F for 45 to 55 minutes. Every portion gets crisp edges all around; unmold with a butter knife after a 10-minute rest.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • gruyère → Comté, Emmental, or aged white cheddar Comté is the closest match; Emmental melts more but browns less; aged cheddar reads sharper and more American.
  • sherry vinegar → white wine vinegar or lemon juice Same quantity. Lemon reads brighter and less rounded.
  • Yukon Gold potatoes → russet potatoes Russets crisp harder at the edges but break down more in the cream; slice at the thicker 3 mm end.

Notes

Why upright. A flat-layered gratin exposes only its top layer to dry oven heat, so it has one thin browned surface over a uniformly soft interior. Standing the slices on edge exposes the top third of every slice; each edge browns and frills, and the dish gets far more crisp surface area per portion while the submerged base braises in the cream the same way a classic gratin does.

Why no flour. The unrinsed potato starch thickens the cream on its own. Flour or a béchamel base makes the sauce pasty and dulls the cheese.

The vinegar. Cream plus cheese plus potato is all fat and starch, which is why many gratins eat heavy after two bites. A small dose of sherry vinegar in high-fat cream does not curdle it (the fat coats the casein and protects it) and makes the whole dish taste brighter without tasting sour.

Holding. Covered in the fridge, leftovers keep 3 days. Reheat covered at 190 °C / 375 °F until the center reads 74 °C / 165 °F, then uncover for 10 minutes to re-crisp.
