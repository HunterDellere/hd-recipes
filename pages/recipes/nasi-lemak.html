Nasi lemak

Malaysian coconut rice steamed with pandan, ginger, and lemongrass, served with sambal ikan bilis (a dried-chili and shallot paste fried slowly until the oil splits, then sharpened with tamarind and sweetened with palm sugar), crisp fried anchovies and peanuts, cucumber, and a boiled egg. The sambal is the work and the reason the plate tastes like itself.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Soak the dried chilies first. They need 20 minutes in boiling water before they can be blended, so start them before any other prep.
  • The sambal is the long track (about 40 minutes, most of it slow frying with frequent stirring). Start the rice as soon as the sambal paste goes into the oil; both finish around the same time, and the eggs boil in parallel.
  • Ventilate before toasting the belacan. Toasted shrimp paste smells strong for an hour; open a window and run the hood.
  • The sambal can be made 1 to 3 days ahead and tastes better for it. Fry the peanuts and anchovies the same day you serve them, or store them airtight once fully cool; they lose crunch in the fridge.

What to prep, in order 4 phases ▾

Step 1 · Fried anchovies and peanuts

  1. 300 g neutral oil, for frying
  2. 120 g raw peanuts, skin-on
  3. 100 g dried anchovies (ikan bilis), split, heads and dark gut removed
  4. 2 g fine sea salt, for the peanuts

Step 2 · Sambal ikan bilis

  1. 30 g dried red chilies (long, medium-heat), stems snipped off, about half the seeds shaken out — (about 20 long dried chilies; Thai or Malaysian cili kering; Kashmiri chilies for a milder sambal)
  2. 150 g shallots, peeled and roughly chopped — (about 6 medium)
  3. 15 g garlic — (4 cloves)
  4. 10 g belacan (block shrimp paste) — (a 2 cm cube)
  5. 60 ml water, for blending
  6. 25 g tamarind pulp (seedless block) — (a heaped tablespoon)
  7. 100 ml warm water, for the tamarind
  8. 150 g red onion, halved and sliced into 5 mm half-rings — (1 medium)
  9. 30 g palm sugar (gula melaka), shaved or chopped fine
  10. 3 g fine sea salt, for the sambal

Step 3 · Coconut rice

  1. 400 g jasmine rice
  2. 1 × 13.5 oz / 400 ml can full-fat coconut milk
  3. 300 ml water, for the rice
  4. 15 g pandan leaves, tied into a knot — (3 to 4 leaves; frozen leaves work)
  5. 10 g ginger, sliced into 3 coins — (a 3 cm piece)
  6. 30 g lemongrass, bottom 12 cm only, bruised — (1 stalk)
  7. 5 g fine sea salt, for the rice

Step 4 · Plate

  1. 4 eggs — (large)
  2. 200 g cucumber, sliced into 5 mm rounds or half-moons — (1 medium or 2 Persian cucumbers)

Method

  1. 1
    Soak and toast. Put the 30 g dried chilies in a heatproof bowl, cover with boiling water, and weigh them down with a small plate; soak 20 minutes until fully soft. Meanwhile, wrap the 10 g belacan in a small square of foil, flatten it, and toast it in a dry skillet over medium heat 2 minutes per side, until it is dry, crumbly, and smells roasted rather than raw and fishy. Raw belacan tastes harsh in a sambal; toasting rounds it out.
  2. 2
    Prep the anchovies and tamarind. Rinse the 100 g split dried anchovies in a sieve under cold water for 10 seconds to remove grit and excess salt, then spread on a towel and pat completely dry; wet anchovies spatter and never crisp. Soak the 25 g tamarind pulp in the 100 ml / scant ½ cup warm water for 10 minutes, squeeze and mash it with your fingers, and strain, pressing the pulp. Keep the liquid and discard the seeds and fiber. Technique: Tamarind souring (sour-broth extraction and balance)
  3. 3
    Fry the peanuts. Heat the 300 g / 1⅓ cups oil in a wok to 150 °C / 300 °F. Add the 120 g raw peanuts and fry, stirring, 5 to 6 minutes, until the nuts under the skins are light golden. Lift them out with a spider onto a tray lined with paper towels and toss with the 2 g / ⅓ tsp fine sea salt. Pull them a shade lighter than you want; they keep darkening for a minute out of the oil, and peanuts that go brown taste bitter. Technique: Deep-frying
  4. 4
    Fry the anchovies. Raise the oil to 165 °C / 330 °F. Fry the 100 g anchovies in two batches, stirring, about 2 to 3 minutes each, until golden and crisp; they go from gold to burnt in under 30 seconds, so pull them at the first even golden color. Drain on paper towels. Set aside 40 g for the sambal and keep 60 g for the plate. Let the oil cool slightly, then measure out 120 g / ½ cup for the sambal; the anchovy oil adds savory depth. Technique: Deep-frying
  5. 5
    Blend the sambal paste. Drain the soaked chilies. Blend them with the 150 g shallots, 15 g / 4 cloves garlic, the toasted 10 g belacan, and the 60 ml / ¼ cup water until very smooth, scraping down the sides. The paste should be a uniform bright red purée with no chili skin flakes visible.
  6. 6
    Fry the paste until the oil splits. Heat the 120 g / ½ cup reserved oil in a wide skillet or clean wok over medium heat, add the paste (it will spit), and cook, stirring every minute or so with a wooden spoon and scraping the bottom, for 25 to 30 minutes. Lower the heat if it sticks. The paste first bubbles and spatters as the water cooks off, then darkens from bright red to deep brick red, thickens, and smells sweet instead of raw. It is ready when the oil separates and pools around the paste (pecah minyak in Malay). Stopping early leaves the sambal tasting raw and sharp of shallot; this slow fry is where its depth comes from.
  7. 7
    Start the rice while the paste fries. Rinse the 400 g / 2 cups jasmine rice in several changes of water until the water runs nearly clear, and drain well. In a heavy 3-liter saucepan, combine the rice with the 250 g / 1 cup coconut milk, the 300 ml / 1¼ cups water, and the 5 g / ¾ tsp fine sea salt; stir so the salt dissolves. Add the 15 g knotted pandan leaves, the 10 g ginger coins, and the 30 g / 1 stalk bruised lemongrass, pushing them into the rice. Bring to a boil over medium-high heat, stir once from the bottom, cover with a tight lid, and turn the heat to its lowest setting for 15 minutes. Then take it off the heat and leave it covered 10 minutes. The coconut fat and sugar scorch easily, so keep the heat truly low and do not lift the lid during the cook.
  8. 8
    Boil the eggs in parallel. Bring a small pot of water to a boil, lower in the 4 eggs, and cook 9 minutes for a fully set but still moist yolk. Move them to a bowl of ice water for 5 minutes, then peel and halve. Technique: Ice bath shock
  9. 9
    Finish the sambal. When the oil has split, add the tamarind liquid (from the 25 g pulp), the 30 g / 2½ tbsp palm sugar, and the 3 g / ½ tsp fine sea salt, and stir until the sugar dissolves. Add the 150 g sliced red onion and the 40 g reserved fried anchovies and simmer gently, stirring, 5 to 8 minutes, until the onion is soft but still has a little bite and the sambal is thick and glossy with oil on its surface. Taste: it should be sweet and spicy up front, then sour and savory. Add salt in pinches if it tastes flat; more palm sugar if the chili is harsh. The anchovies in the sambal soften as they absorb the sauce, which is intended.
  10. 10
    Fluff the rice. Remove the 15 g pandan knot, the 10 g ginger, and the 30 g lemongrass. Fluff the rice with a rice paddle or fork, folding up from the bottom so the coconut fat that settled there is redistributed.
  11. 11
    Plate. Mound about 200 g / 1 heaped cup rice in the center of each plate. Arrange around it a generous 2 tbsp (about 60 g) of sambal, 15 g / 2 tbsp fried anchovies, 15 g / 2 tbsp fried peanuts, a fan of 50 g cucumber slices, and 2 halves of 1 egg. Eat by mixing a little of everything into each bite; the cucumber and egg cool the sambal, the anchovies and peanuts add crunch and salt.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Texture occasion→fried egg instead of boiled

    Fry each egg in 15 g / 1 tbsp of the leftover frying oil over medium-high heat until the edges are lacy and crisp and the yolk is still runny, about 2 minutes. Many home cooks prefer it; the boiled egg is the version that travels in a banana-leaf packet.

  • Heat heat→milder sambal

    Use 20 g dried chilies, shake out all the seeds, and add 1 small red bell pepper (about 120 g), chopped, to the blender for body and color. Increase the frying time slightly to cook off its extra water.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • dried anchovies (ikan bilis) → no direct substitute Buy them split and cleaned (often labeled "ikan bilis, split" or "dried anchovy, no head") at Malaysian, Chinese, Korean, or Filipino grocers to skip the cleaning. Korean myeolchi of medium size works the same way.
  • belacan → Thai shrimp paste (kapi) Softer and sold in jars; use the same weight and toast it in foil the same way. It is a little milder.
  • pandan leaves → frozen pandan leaves Pandan is what makes the rice smell like nasi lemak; frozen leaves are equivalent to fresh and stocked by most Southeast Asian grocers. Bottled pandan extract is a poor stand-in, adding color and a flatter, artificial aroma.
  • tamarind pulp → concentrated tamarind paste Use about 15 g whisked into 60 ml / ¼ cup water.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → full-fat coconut milk make it yourselfstub In a rice where coconut is the main flavor, fresh-pressed coconut milk gives a cleaner, sweeter coconut aroma than canned.

Notes

The sambal decides the dish. Sambal tumis is cooked, not raw: the blended chili and shallot paste has to fry slowly until its water is gone and the oil separates out. During that fry the shallot sugars caramelize and the chili and belacan aromas move into the oil. Underfried sambal is the most common failure; it tastes of raw shallot and bitter chili. Keep the heat at medium or below and stir often; scorched spots taste burnt throughout.

Balance. Malaysian nasi lemak sambal is sweeter than most outsiders expect. Palm sugar balances the chili, tamarind keeps it from tasting cloying, and the belacan and anchovies give glutamate and salt. Tamarind is the acid here: the dish does not get a squeeze of lime, and the cucumber supplies the freshness.

Rice. Rinsing removes surface starch so the grains stay separate despite the fat in the coconut milk. Salt goes in the cooking liquid so every grain is seasoned. Pandan carries most of the aroma; ginger and lemongrass are common in Malay home kitchens but not universal.

Cleaning ikan bilis. Pull off the heads and the small dark gut along the belly, then split the fish in half. The gut tastes bitter after frying. Pre-split anchovies save 15 minutes.

Storage and food safety. Leftover sambal keeps a week in a sealed glass jar in the fridge, with its oil on top, and freezes well. Nasi lemak is traditionally sold at room temperature, but cooked rice should not sit out more than 2 hours (Bacillus cereus spores survive cooking); refrigerate leftovers promptly and reheat until steaming.
