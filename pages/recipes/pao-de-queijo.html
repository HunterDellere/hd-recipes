Pão de queijo

Brazilian cheese bread from Minas Gerais: sour and sweet tapioca starch scalded with boiling milk and oil, beaten with eggs and grated cheese, rolled into small balls, and baked until they puff into crisp shells around a chewy, stretchy center. The dough freezes unbaked and goes straight from freezer to oven.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • The scalded dough must cool until it is only warm to the touch (about 15 minutes) before the eggs go in, or the eggs start to cook in streaks. Grate the cheese and preheat the oven during the wait.
  • If you plan to freeze most of the batch, clear a sheet pan's worth of flat freezer space before you start shaping.

Scalding liquid

  1. 250 ml whole milk
  2. 100 ml water
  3. 120 ml neutral oil
  4. 8 g fine sea salt

Enrichment

  1. 3 eggs
  2. 250 g queijo Minas meia-cura, finely grated
  3. 5 ml neutral oil, for shaping

Method

  1. 1
    Prepare. Heat the oven to 200 °C / 400 °F (180 °C / 350 °F with convection) with racks in the upper and lower thirds, and line two sheet pans with parchment. Put the 250 g polvilho azedo and the 250 g polvilho doce in a large heatproof bowl (or a stand mixer bowl) and whisk to combine and break up lumps.
  2. 2
    Scald the starch. In a saucepan, bring the 250 ml whole milk, the 100 ml water, the 120 ml / ½ cup neutral oil, and the 8 g / 1⅓ tsp fine sea salt to a full rolling boil. Pour it over the starch all at once and stir hard with a wooden spoon (or the mixer paddle on low) until every bit of starch is moistened. It will look lumpy, glossy, and stiff. The boiling liquid gelatinizes part of the starch on contact, and that cooked gel is what binds a dough with no gluten in it; liquid that is merely hot leaves a crumbly dough that bakes flat.
  3. 3
    Cool. Keep stirring for a minute to break up the larger lumps, then leave the dough to cool for about 15 minutes, until it feels warm but no longer hot when you press a finger into the center (below about 45 °C / 113 °F). Grate the 250 g queijo Minas meia-cura finely while you wait.
  4. 4
    Add the eggs. Beat in 2 of the 3 eggs (150 g total) one at a time, mixing (by hand, kneading in the bowl, or with the paddle on medium) until each is fully absorbed before adding the next. The dough goes from shaggy to smooth and sticky. Beat the third egg in a small cup and add it a little at a time, stopping when the dough is soft and sticky but still holds a shape when scooped, like a thick, slack cookie dough. Polvilho brands absorb differently, so you may not need the whole egg.
  5. 5
    Add the cheese. Work in the 250 g grated queijo Minas until evenly distributed, about 2 minutes. The dough will be sticky and slightly elastic.
  6. 6
    Shape. Rub your palms with a little of the 5 ml / 1 tsp neutral oil. Scoop 25 g (about a heaping tablespoon) of dough and roll it into a smooth ball between your palms. Set the balls on the lined pans about 4 cm / 1½ inches apart, re-oiling your hands as the dough starts to stick. At this point, set aside the balls you want to freeze (see the last step).
  7. 7
    Bake. Bake for 20 to 25 minutes, swapping and rotating the pans halfway, until the balls have puffed, the bases are golden brown, the tops are pale gold, and they sound hollow when tapped. Pulled too early, they collapse into dense, gummy centers as they cool; a few extra minutes only makes the shell crisper. Let them rest 5 minutes and eat warm, while the center is still stretchy.
  8. 8
    Freeze the rest unbaked. Freeze the shaped balls on a parchment-lined tray until hard, about 2 hours, then transfer to a lidded glass or metal container and keep frozen up to 3 months. Bake straight from frozen (do not thaw, or they slump and spread) at 200 °C / 400 °F for 28 to 32 minutes. Baked and cooled pão de queijo also freezes; reheat at 180 °C / 350 °F for 8 minutes to restore the crisp shell.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Occasion size→larger sandwich rolls

    Shape 50 g balls for sandwich-sized rolls and bake 28 to 32 minutes at the same temperature. Split and fill with ham and requeijão or a fried egg.

  • Regional cheese profile→sharper, more aged flavor

    Replace 50 g of the Minas cheese with finely grated aged Gouda or Pecorino Romano. The sharper cheese reads stronger but browns faster; check at 18 minutes.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • queijo Minas meia-cura → parmigiano-reggiano, finely grated Use the same 250 g and cut the fine sea salt to 5 g. Parmesan is drier, saltier, and more savory than Minas cheese, so the bread tastes nuttier and browns a little faster, but the texture holds. A 50/50 mix of parmesan and a mild semi-firm cheese (Monterey Jack, young Gouda) lands closer to the Minas flavor.
  • polvilho azedo → more polvilho doce (all sweet starch) Use 500 g tapioca starch in total. The bread puffs less, the interior is denser and chewier with no hollow, and the faint sour tang disappears. Still good; closer to the American "Brazilian cheese bread" mixes.

Notes

How the dough works. Tapioca starch has almost no protein, so there is no gluten to trap gas. Scalding gelatinizes a fraction of the starch into a sticky gel that holds the dough together, and the eggs add protein that sets in the oven. In the heat, water in the dough turns to steam and inflates the elastic, starch-and-egg network like a choux pastry; the remaining raw starch gelatinizes around those bubbles, giving the crisp shell and the chewy center.

Why sour starch. Polvilho azedo is cassava starch fermented for weeks and then sun-dried. The lactic fermentation and the ultraviolet drying change the starch so it expands much more in the oven, which is where the hollow, airy interior comes from, and it brings a mild sourness. That tang, plus the salt and glutamate in the aged cheese, is the whole seasoning; as a bread, pão de queijo carries no separate finishing acid.

Texture troubleshooting. Flat, spreading balls mean the dough was too wet (too much egg) or was not scalded with liquid at a full boil. Dense, crumbly balls mean too little liquid: add the rest of the beaten egg or a spoonful of milk. Gummy centers mean underbaking.

Sources
  • Minas Gerais home tradition — Mineiro cooks disagree about the ratio of sour to sweet polvilho; recipes run from all sour to mostly sweet. Equal parts is a middle path: the sour starch gives the puff, the hollow interior, and a faint tang, while the sweet starch keeps the crumb tender and chewy. The scald with boiling liquid is the step every version shares.
