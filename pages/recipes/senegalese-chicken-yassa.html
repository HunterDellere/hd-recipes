Chicken yassa (yassa poulet)

The Senegalese Casamance classic: chicken marinated overnight with a kilo of sliced onions, lemon juice, mustard, and garlic, browned hard, then braised in those same onions slowly cooked down to a soft, sweet-sour, golden mass with green olives and a whole scotch bonnet. Served over white rice.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Marinate the chicken and onions together 12 to 24 hours ahead, in a covered glass or ceramic dish in the refrigerator. Under 4 hours the onions stay sharp and the chicken tastes of lemon only on the surface. Past 24 hours the acid starts to turn the outer layer of the meat soft and chalky.
  • Start the rice when the chicken goes back into the pot to braise; both finish within a few minutes of each other.

What to prep, in order 3 phases ▾

Step 1 · The marinade (the night before)

  1. 1200 g bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs, trimmed of overhanging skin and fat
  2. 1000 g yellow onions, halved and sliced 3 mm thick, pole to pole
  3. 120 g lemon juice (for the marinade)
  4. 45 g dijon mustard (for the marinade)
  5. 15 g garlic cloves, grated on a microplane — (4 large cloves)
  6. 10 g kosher salt (for the marinade)
  7. 2 g black pepper, freshly ground
  8. 30 g peanut oil, refined, or another neutral oil (for the marinade)

Step 2 · Browning and the onion braise

  1. 30 g peanut oil, refined, or another neutral oil (for browning)
  2. 2 g kosher salt (for the onions)
  3. 15 g dijon mustard (for the sauce)
  4. 10 g chicken bouillon cube (Maggi or Jumbo), crumbled — (1 cube; the Senegalese glutamate source)
  5. 240 g water (for the braise)
  6. 0.5 g bay leaves — (2 dried leaves)
  7. 10 g scotch bonnet pepper, left whole, pierced twice with a paring knife — (1 pepper)
  8. 120 g green olives, pitted, drained and rinsed
  9. 15 g lemon juice (to finish)

Step 3 · Rice

  1. 300 g jasmine rice, or broken jasmine rice
  2. 450 g water (for the rice)
  3. 3 g kosher salt (for the rice)

Method

  1. 1
    The night before, marinate. In a large glass or ceramic dish, whisk the 120 g / ½ cup lemon juice, 45 g / 3 tbsp dijon mustard, 15 g / 4 grated garlic cloves, 10 g / 1 tbsp kosher salt, 2 g / 1 tsp black pepper, and 30 g / 2 tbsp oil. Add the 1000 g sliced onions and squeeze them through the marinade with your hands for a minute, until they start to wilt; the salt and acid begin softening them now so they cook down faster tomorrow. Bury the 1200 g chicken thighs in the onions, turning to coat. Cover with a fitted lid or a plate and refrigerate 12 to 24 hours, turning the chicken once if you think of it.12 h
  2. 2
    Separate. Lift the chicken out of the marinade, scraping the onions back into the dish, and pat the skin very dry with paper towels; wet skin steams instead of browning. Tip the onions into a sieve set over a bowl and press lightly. Keep both the onions and the drained liquid (about 200 g / ¾ cup).
  3. 3
    Brown the chicken. Heat the 30 g / 2 tbsp oil in a 5 to 6 litre Dutch oven over medium heat until it shimmers. Lay the thighs in skin side down, in two batches if they crowd, and cook without moving them for 8 to 10 minutes, until the skin is deep brown and much of the fat has rendered. Flip and brown the second side for 2 minutes. Transfer to a plate. Pour off all but about 45 g / 3 tbsp of the fat. Technique: Maillard sear
  4. 4
    Cook down the onions. Add the drained onions and the 2 g / ½ tsp kosher salt to the pot, scraping up the brown chicken fond as they release their water. Cover and cook over medium heat for 10 minutes, stirring twice, until collapsed. Uncover and cook another 20 to 25 minutes, stirring every few minutes with a wooden spoon, until the onions are soft, jammy, and golden with browned edges. This is slower than browning plain onions: the lemon acid lowers their pH, which slows the Maillard reactions, so give them the time rather than more heat, which scorches the edges while the centers stay raw.
  5. 5
    Build the sauce. Pour in the reserved marinade liquid (about 200 g / ¾ cup), the 15 g / 1 tbsp dijon mustard, the 10 g crumbled bouillon cube, the 240 g / 1 cup water, and the 0.5 g / 2 bay leaves. Stir, scraping the bottom, and bring to a boil for 1 minute; the liquid held raw chicken, and the boil makes it safe. Taste: it should be sharp, savory, and onion-sweet. Technique: Deglazing
  6. 6
    Braise. Nestle the browned chicken back into the onions skin side up, with the skin above the liquid so it stays somewhat crisp, and tuck in the 10 g pierced whole scotch bonnet. Cover, reduce to a gentle simmer, and cook 30 minutes, until the thighs read at least 80 °C / 175 °F next to the bone on an instant-read thermometer. Thighs are safe at 74 °C / 165 °F, but their connective tissue needs the higher temperature to turn tender. Start the rice now.
  7. 7
    Cook the rice alongside. Rinse the 300 g / 1½ cups rice in a sieve until the water runs mostly clear. Bring it to a boil in a heavy saucepan with the 450 g water and 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt, stir once, cover with a fitted lid, and cook on the lowest heat for 15 minutes (12 for broken rice). Take off the heat and rest, covered, 10 minutes, then fluff with a fork.
  8. 8
    Finish. Uncover the pot, scatter in the 120 g / ¾ cup green olives, and simmer uncovered 5 to 10 minutes until the sauce has thickened to a loose onion jam that still pools a little around the chicken. Fish out the scotch bonnet and bay leaves; for more heat, split the pepper and stir some of its seeds back into the sauce. Stir in the 15 g / 1 tbsp lemon juice and taste for salt: the sauce should be clearly tart against the sweetness of the onions, with a slow fruity heat. Add a pinch of kosher salt if it tastes flat.
  9. 9
    Serve each portion of rice topped with 1 or 2 thighs and a generous ladle of the onions, olives, and sauce, so the rice drinks up the juices.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Regional browning in the pot→charcoal-grilled yassa

    After marinating, grill the chicken over a medium-hot charcoal fire, skin side down first, for 20 to 25 minutes, turning often, until charred in spots and 70 °C / 158 °F inside. Meanwhile cook the onions in 45 g / 3 tbsp oil in the Dutch oven as in step 4. Add the grilled chicken to the onion sauce and braise 15 minutes to bring it to 80 °C / 175 °F. The smoke is the street-food version of the dish.

  • Protein chicken→yassa poisson (fish yassa)

    Use 1 kg whole snapper, sea bream, or 4 thick fillets of firm white fish. Marinate only 2 to 4 hours, since the acid cooks fish quickly. Grill or broil the fish until charred, cook the onions as written, then lay the fish on the onion sauce for the last 5 minutes only.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • chicken bouillon cube (Maggi or Jumbo) → 5 g / 1 tsp fish sauce plus 1 g / ¼ tsp MSG For cooks who avoid bouillon cubes. Bouillon is a normal, long-standing part of the Senegalese pantry, not a shortcut, so the cube is the default.
  • green olives, pitted → leave them out Olives are common in Dakar yassa but not universal; without them the sauce is a touch sweeter, so add another 5 g / 1 tsp lemon juice.
  • scotch bonnet pepper → habanero Same heat and fruitiness. Do not use generic chili flakes; the fruity aroma is part of the sauce.

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → dijon mustard (for the marinade) make it yourself Mustard is half the sauce's sharpness, and a fresh-ground batch is hotter and more aromatic than a jar that has sat open in the refrigerator.

Notes

The onions are the dish. A kilo of onions for four people looks wrong until you see how far they cook down: by the end they are a soft, sweet, sour, golden sauce that coats the rice. Slice them pole to pole (root to tip) rather than across; the slices hold their shape as ribbons instead of dissolving into mush.

Why marinate the onions with the chicken. The overnight acid and salt do three things. They season the chicken through, they tame the raw onion sulfur compounds, and they pull water out of the onions so they collapse quickly in the pot. The cost is slower browning, because Maillard reactions run faster at higher pH and the onions are now acidic. That is why the onions cook covered first (to soften) and then uncovered for a long stretch (to color); turning up the heat burns the edges before the middles soften.

Why reserve and boil the marinade. The lemon-mustard liquid is too good to waste, but it held raw chicken overnight. Bringing it to a full boil before the chicken returns, then simmering 30 minutes, makes it safe.

Umami and acid. The bouillon cube is the glutamate source, as it is in most Senegalese kitchens; the browned chicken fond and mustard add more depth. Lemon is the acid at two stages: the marinade, which mellows as it cooks, and a fresh squeeze at the end that brings back the brightness the braise cooked off.

Mustard. Senegalese cooks most often use a smooth Dijon-style mustard, sold everywhere in Dakar. Grainy mustard works but makes the sauce look speckled.

Storage. Yassa is better the next day. Refrigerate in a covered glass container up to 4 days; reheat gently with a splash of water and a fresh squeeze of lemon. It freezes for 2 months, though the olives soften.

Sources
  • Senegalese home cooking, from the Casamance region in the south, where yassa originated, to Dakar, where it is an everyday family dish. — Yassa is made with chicken, fish, or lamb; chicken is the most common. Families split on whether the chicken is grilled over charcoal before it meets the onions or browned in the pot; this version browns in the pot so the fond goes into the sauce, and the charcoal version is under modifications.
