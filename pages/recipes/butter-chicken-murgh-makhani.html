Butter chicken (murgh makhani)

Boneless chicken thighs marinated in spiced yogurt, charred hard under the broiler in place of a tandoor, then simmered briefly in a silky tomato, cashew, and butter gravy finished with crushed kasuri methi and cream. The gravy is blended and reduced until it clings, so it reads rich and tangy rather than sweet.

Before you start

Read this first — the long-lead and coordination steps that need to be moving before you start prepping.

  • Marinate the chicken at least 4 hours and up to 24 hours ahead, covered in a glass or ceramic dish in the refrigerator. Under 4 hours the salt and yogurt only reach the surface; past 24 hours the yogurt acid starts to make the meat mealy.
  • Pull the chicken from the fridge 20 minutes before broiling, and make the gravy while the broiler heats. The gravy takes about 35 minutes and holds happily; the chicken goes into it at the end.
  • Canned whole peeled tomatoes are the right choice from autumn through spring, when supermarket fresh tomatoes are pale and watery. One 28-oz can is exactly the amount needed.

Method

  1. 1
    Marinate. In a glass or ceramic bowl, stir together the 120 g / ½ cup Greek yogurt, 20 g / 4 tsp ginger-garlic paste, 15 ml / 1 tbsp lemon juice, 5 g / 2 tsp Kashmiri chili powder, 2 g / 1 tsp garam masala, 2 g / 1 tsp ground cumin, 1 g / ¼ tsp turmeric, 7 g / 1¾ tsp kosher salt, and 15 ml / 1 tbsp neutral oil into a thick red paste. Add the 800 g chicken pieces and turn until every surface is coated. The salt seasons the meat through and helps it hold moisture under the broiler; the yogurt and lemon tenderize the surface and give the char something to cling to. Cover with a fitted lid or a plate and refrigerate 4 to 24 hours.
  2. 2
    Start the gravy. Melt the 30 g / 2 tbsp butter for the gravy base in a heavy saucepan over medium heat. Add the 4 cracked cardamom pods, 4 cloves, the 3 g / 1-inch cinnamon stick, and the 1 bay leaf; fry 30 seconds until they smell fragrant and the butter foams around them. Add the 25 g / 5 tsp ginger-garlic paste and fry, stirring with a wooden spoon, 1 minute until the raw smell is gone and it just begins to color. Technique: Blooming aromatics
  3. 3
    Simmer the tomatoes and cashews. Add the 800 g / 28-oz can hand-crushed tomatoes, then swirl the 120 ml / ½ cup water in the can and add it. Stir in the 50 g / ⅓ cup raw cashews, 5 g / 2 tsp Kashmiri chili powder, and 5 g / 1¼ tsp kosher salt. Bring to a simmer, cover partway, and cook 20 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the tomatoes have collapsed and a cashew crushes easily against the side of the pan. Salting now lets the tomatoes season while they cook, rather than tasting salted on top.
  4. 4
    Heat the broiler. While the gravy simmers, set an oven rack so the top of the chicken will sit about 10 cm / 4 inches below the broiler element and heat the broiler on high for 10 minutes. Line a rimmed sheet pan with foil and set a wire rack on it.
  5. 5
    Char the chicken. Lift the 800 g chicken out of the marinade, letting the excess drip off, and spread the pieces on the rack with space between them; crowded pieces steam rather than char. Brush with half of the 15 g / 1 tbsp melted ghee. Broil 8 minutes, until blackened at the edges and in spots. Flip, brush with the remaining ghee, and broil 5 more minutes. The chicken is done when the thickest piece reads at least 74 °C / 165 °F on an instant-read thermometer; thighs stay juicy well past that, up to about 85 °C / 185 °F, so err toward more char. Rest the chicken on the rack while you finish the gravy.
  6. 6
    Blend the gravy. Fish out the 3 g cinnamon stick and the 1 bay leaf with tongs. Transfer the gravy to a blender, vent the lid (remove the center cap and cover the opening with a folded kitchen towel so steam can escape), and blend on high 1 minute until completely smooth and pale orange-red. For the most velvety result, press it through a fine sieve back into the saucepan with the back of a ladle; with a high-speed blender this step can be skipped.
  7. 7
    Enrich and reduce. Return the gravy to medium-low heat and stir in the 30 g / 2 tbsp butter for enriching and the 8 g / 1 tsp honey. Simmer, stirring often (it spits), 8 minutes, until it thickens enough to coat the back of a spoon and small beads of orange butterfat appear on the surface. Taste for salt; it should taste fully seasoned and bright, with the honey only taking the raw edge off the tomato.
  8. 8
    Combine and simmer. Add the 800 g charred chicken and any juices from the sheet pan to the gravy. Simmer gently 5 minutes so the chicken absorbs some of the gravy and the char flavors it. Crush the 3 g / 1 tbsp kasuri methi between your palms directly over the pot (rubbing breaks the dried leaves and releases their aroma), then add the 2 g / 1 tsp garam masala for finishing. Both go in late so their volatile aromatics survive.
  9. 9
    Finish. Stir in the 120 ml / ½ cup heavy cream and heat through without boiling hard. Off the heat, stir in the 15 g / 1 tbsp cold butter until it melts into a glossy surface. Taste and add the 5 ml / 1 tsp lemon juice if the gravy reads heavy; it should be rich, tangy, and gently warm rather than sweet. Serve with a swirl of cream if you like.

Modifications

Pivots that change the dish's character — protein swaps, regional variants, dietary recastings.

  • Regional smoky restaurant finish→butter chicken with dhungar (charcoal smoke)

    Heat a 3 cm piece of lump charcoal over a gas burner until glowing all over. Set a small steel bowl on top of the finished curry, put the coal in it, pour 5 g / 1 tsp ghee over the coal, and cover the pot with a lid for 2 minutes. This adds the tandoor smoke the broiler only approximates. Run the hood, and use lump charcoal, never briquettes.

  • Protein vegetarian→paneer makhani

    Replace the chicken with 400 g paneer cut into 2.5 cm cubes. Marinate 30 minutes in half the marinade, broil 4 minutes per side until spotted brown, and simmer in the finished gravy only 2 minutes so it stays soft.

  • Occasion no broiler→cast-iron char

    Heat a cast-iron skillet over high heat until it smokes. Sear the marinated chicken in two batches, 3 minutes per side without moving it, until blackened in spots and cooked to 74 °C / 165 °F. The char is patchier than under a broiler but the flavor is close.

Substitutions

1:1 ingredient swaps where the dish stays the same dish.

  • boneless, skinless chicken thighs → boneless, skinless chicken breast Breast dries out quickly under the broiler. Pull it the moment the thickest piece reads 74 °C / 165 °F, and simmer it in the gravy only 2 minutes.
  • raw cashews → blanched almonds, or 60 ml / ¼ cup more heavy cream Almonds thicken the same way with a slightly grainier finish. Leaving the nuts out keeps the dish nut-free; the gravy is thinner, so reduce it a few minutes longer.
  • kasuri methi (dried fenugreek leaves) → no close substitute Kasuri methi gives butter chicken its signature savory, faintly maple-like aroma. It keeps for a year and is sold at any Indian grocer and online. Without it the dish is still good but reads generic.
  • whole-milk Greek yogurt → whole-milk plain yogurt, strained 30 minutes in a cheesecloth-lined sieve Straining thickens it to the same clinging consistency (hung curd).

Make it from scratch

Store-bought is fine, but each of these has a homemade version that earns the time.

  • → ginger-garlic paste (for the marinade) make it yourselfstub Fresh-blended paste is brighter and sharper than jarred, which is usually preserved with citric acid and tastes faintly sour.
  • → garam masala (for the marinade) make it yourselfstub Freshly ground garam masala carries far more aroma into the finish than a jar that has been open for months.
  • → ghee (for basting) make it yourselfstub Homemade ghee with properly toasted milk solids adds a nutty note to the char that jarred ghee rarely matches.

Notes

Why the char matters. Butter chicken began as a way to use leftover tandoori chicken, so the chicken should arrive in the gravy already blackened in spots. That char adds bitter, smoky Maillard and pyrolysis notes that the sweet-sour gravy needs; chicken poached directly in the gravy produces a flatter, sweeter dish. A broiler on high with the chicken close to the element is the closest home stand-in for a tandoor, and the rack lets heat reach the underside so the pieces do not stew in their own juices.

Why thighs. Thigh meat has more fat and connective tissue than breast, so it stays juicy well above the 74 °C / 165 °F safe temperature and through the final simmer. Breast works (see substitutions) but leaves almost no margin for error.

Building the gravy. The tomatoes and cashews simmer together, then blend into one smooth emulsion: the cashews thicken it and add a mild, creamy body, while the butter and cream are added after blending so they stay glossy rather than cooking into the solids. The gravy should taste tangy first and rich second. Many restaurant versions read sugary; the honey here is only enough to soften canned-tomato acidity, so add it gradually if your tomatoes are already sweet.

Umami and acid. Cooked-down tomato is the glutamate source and also the main acid, backed by the yogurt and lemon in the marinade. The final squeeze of lemon is a correction, not a feature: add it only if the cream and butter have dulled the tomato.

Kasuri methi and garam masala go in last. Both carry volatile aromatics that fade with long cooking, so they are added in the final minutes, as the South Asian tradition does with garam masala generally.

Make ahead. The gravy keeps 4 days refrigerated or 3 months frozen (without the cream). Broil the chicken fresh on the day for the best char, or refrigerate cooked chicken in the gravy for up to 3 days; it reheats well over low heat with a splash of water.

Sources
  • Punjabi-Delhi restaurant tradition — Butter chicken is a 20th-century dish credited to the Moti Mahal restaurant in Delhi, founded by cooks from Peshawar after Partition: leftover tandoori chicken simmered in a buttery tomato gravy so it would not dry out. The broiler stands in for the tandoor here; the char it builds is what gives the gravy its faint smokiness.
