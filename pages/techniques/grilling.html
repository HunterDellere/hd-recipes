Technique

Grilling

Cook over or under intense radiant heat from fire, coals, or a broiler element, so the surface browns and chars quickly while fat drips away and smoke adds flavor.

About

A grill cooks mostly by radiant heat: the glowing coals, flames, or element emit infrared energy that browns any surface facing them, much faster than hot air in an oven. Fat and juices that drip onto the coals vaporize into smoke that carries back onto the food, which is a large part of grilled flavor. Contact with hot grates adds the dark sear marks.

Direct and indirect heat. Directly over the fire, surfaces brown in minutes: right for thin cuts, skewers, flatbreads, vegetables, and fish. Thick cuts and bone-in pieces burn outside before they cook inside, so they go on the cooler side of a two-zone fire, with coals or burners on one side only and the lid closed, to cook through with convected heat, then move over the fire at the end to brown. Building a two-zone fire is the single most useful habit on a grill because it gives you somewhere to move food that is browning too fast or flaring up.

Flare-ups. Dripping fat ignites and coats food in soot. Trim excess fat, keep marinades with oil and sugar to a thin coat, and move food to the cool zone rather than spraying water on the fire. Sugary glazes go on only in the last few minutes, since sugar burns well below the temperature of a direct fire.

Broiling is grilling upside down. An oven broiler is an overhead radiant source; the same rules apply, with distance from the element standing in for distance from the coals. It suits fish, yakitori-style skewers, and charring peppers and tomatoes indoors.

Grilling runs through Turkish and Persian kebabs, Thai and Vietnamese grilled meats, Japanese yakitori and salt-grilled fish, Argentine asado, Korean barbecue, and American backyard cooking.
