Technique

Poaching

Cook delicate food submerged in liquid held well below a simmer, about 70 to 85 °C, so proteins set gently and stay moist and tender instead of tightening.

About

Poaching is cooking in liquid that barely moves: small wisps of steam, maybe a rare bubble on the bottom, around 70 to 85 °C. At that temperature proteins set slowly and evenly from the outside in. Chicken breast, fish fillets, shrimp, eggs, and pork loin all contain little collagen, so they gain nothing from long or hot cooking and lose moisture the moment their fibers contract hard. Poaching keeps them below the point where that happens for as long as possible.

The liquid. Water seasoned with salt and aromatics is enough for most poaching; stock, wine, milk, coconut milk, or a court bouillon with vinegar and herbs add flavor. Salt the liquid so the food is seasoned through. Large pieces such as whole chickens or pork shoulders can start in cold liquid so they heat evenly; small pieces and fish go into liquid already at temperature.

Carryover and resting. Food keeps cooking after it leaves the liquid. Pull chicken breast at about 63 to 65 °C and shrimp the moment they turn opaque; letting poached meat cool in its liquid, or shocking it in ice water as Chinese white-cut chicken does, firms the texture and keeps it juicy.

The same idea at the low end is low-temperature poaching, where fish or eggs are held at 50 to 65 °C for a silky, barely set texture. At the high end it shades into simmering. Applications include Chinese white-cut and Hainanese chicken, poached shrimp for salads and cocktails, French poached fish and eggs, and Vietnamese and Korean poached pork belly.
