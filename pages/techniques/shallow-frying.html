Technique

Shallow frying

Fry food in a layer of oil that comes partway up its sides, turning it once, to get a deep, even crust on each face without the volume of oil that deep-frying needs.

About

Shallow frying sits between a sear and a deep-fry. The oil is 0.5 to 2 cm deep, enough to surround the bottom half of the food so the crust forms evenly across the whole face, including around irregular edges that a thin film of oil in a sear would miss. Breaded cutlets, fritters, fish fillets, falafel, latkes, dumpling bottoms, and fried eggs are all shallow-fried.

Temperature. Most shallow frying happens at 160 to 190 °C. Below that the coating absorbs oil before it sets and the food turns greasy; above it the outside browns before the inside cooks. A crumb of bread or a drop of batter should sizzle steadily on contact and brown in about 40 to 60 seconds. Add food in batches with space between pieces: each piece drops the oil temperature, and a crowded pan cannot recover.

The oil level matters more than people expect. Too little oil and the crust forms only where the food touches the pan, giving blotchy color. For thin cutlets and fillets, the oil should come about halfway up; for thicker items such as fritters or croquettes, turn them more than once so every side spends time in the oil. Use a neutral oil with a high smoke point, or lard, ghee, or clarified butter for flavor.

Turn once, when the crust releases. Food stuck to the pan has not finished forming a crust; forcing it tears the coating. Once it releases on its own and the edges are deep golden, turn it.

Keep it crisp. Drain on a rack, not on paper, so steam can escape from the bottom; food sitting on paper steams its own crust soft. Season with salt the moment it comes out of the oil, while the surface is still wet with fat.
