Technique

Braising

Cook collagen-rich meat or dense vegetables partly submerged in liquid, covered, below a boil for hours, until connective tissue converts to gelatin and the liquid becomes the sauce.

About

Braising works on collagen, the connective protein that makes shoulder, shank, chuck, short rib, and thigh tough when cooked quickly. Held moist at about 70 to 95 °C, collagen slowly unwinds and dissolves into gelatin. The muscle fibers squeeze out moisture as they heat, but the dissolved gelatin coats them and the meat reads as tender and juicy. That conversion takes time, which is why braises run 1½ to 4 hours depending on the cut and the size of the pieces.

Below a boil, not at one. Liquid at a full boil churns the meat and drives the internal temperature high enough that the fibers contract hard and dry out before the collagen finishes melting; the result is meat that is falling apart but stringy. A covered pot in a 140 to 160 °C oven holds the liquid at a lazy simmer from all sides without scorching the bottom, which is why most braises move to the oven after the liquid comes to a simmer. On a stovetop, look for an occasional bubble, not a rolling surface.

How much liquid. A braise is partly submerged: the liquid comes one half to three quarters of the way up the meat. The exposed tops brown and concentrate in the oven, and less liquid means a more concentrated sauce at the end. A fully submerged pot is a stew; the line between the two is loose, and the same rules about temperature apply.

Flavor is built before the liquid goes in. Searing the meat, sweating the aromatics, frying tomato paste or chile purée, and deglazing the fond all happen first, because a wet pot never gets hot enough to brown anything. Wine, stock, vinegar, soy, coconut milk, and chile purées are all braising liquids; acid in the liquid slows the softening of vegetables but does little to meat.

When it is done. Test the meat, not the clock: a piece should yield and split under light pressure from a spoon. If it resists, it needs more time, not more heat. Most braises improve overnight in the fridge as the flavors settle and the fat sets on top, where it can be lifted off. Reduce the liquid at the end, uncovered, if it is thin.

The same method runs through Texas chili and pot roast, French beef bourguignon and coq au vin, Chinese red-cooked pork, North African tagines, Mexican adobos, and Filipino vinegar braises.
