about: |
  Bulk fermentation is the period between mixing a dough and dividing or shaping it. Yeast (and, in sourdough, lactic acid bacteria) eat sugars released from the flour and produce carbon dioxide, alcohol, and organic acids. The gas inflates the dough, and the acids and alcohols are most of what bread tastes like. At the same time, gluten, the network formed when flour proteins are hydrated, keeps organizing itself into the stretchy structure that traps that gas.

  Gluten develops with time as well as kneading. An autolyse, resting flour and water for 20 to 60 minutes before adding salt and leavening, lets enzymes and hydration start building gluten with no work. During bulk, sets of stretch and folds (lifting one side of the dough, stretching it up, and folding it over, repeated around the bowl every 30 minutes or so) strengthen the network gently. This replaces most kneading in wet, high-hydration doughs, which are too slack to knead conventionally.

  Preferments and cold. A preferment, such as a poolish, biga, or sourdough levain, is a portion of the flour fermented hours ahead; it adds flavor and acidity that a short bulk cannot. Cold slows yeast far more than it slows flavor development, so retarding dough in the fridge (during bulk or after shaping) stretches fermentation over 12 to 48 hours for a more complex, slightly tangy loaf and a schedule that fits around the day.

  Reading the dough. Time is only a guide, because temperature changes everything: a dough at 27 °C ferments about twice as fast as one at 21 °C. Bulk is done when the dough has grown by roughly 50 to 100% depending on the recipe, feels airy and jiggly, domes at the edges, and shows bubbles on the sides and surface. Underfermented dough bakes dense and tight; overfermented dough slumps, smells boozy, and spreads in the oven.

  The same principles apply across lean country loaves and sourdough, pizza and focaccia, rye breads, and enriched doughs such as brioche and milk bread, which ferment more slowly because fat and sugar slow the yeast.
