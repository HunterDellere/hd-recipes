about: |
  A simmer is defined by what the surface looks like. A gentle simmer shows a few small bubbles breaking now and then; a steady simmer has small bubbles rising across much of the surface; a full boil churns and rolls. Under that churn the liquid is at 100 °C at sea level, and the agitation itself does damage that the extra few degrees alone would not.

  Why stay below the boil. Boiling tumbles food against itself and the pot, which splits bean skins and breaks up fish and dumplings. It emulsifies fat into stocks and broths, which turns them cloudy and greasy. It overheats meat, squeezing the fibers dry before collagen has time to soften. And it reduces liquids fast and unevenly, scorching thick sauces at the bottom. A simmer cooks almost as fast without any of that.

  Holding it steady. Bring the liquid to a boil to get it hot quickly, then turn the heat down until the surface settles to the simmer you want. A partly covered pot holds temperature with less heat and loses less liquid; a fully covered pot can creep back up to a boil. A heavy pot evens out hot spots. Watch the surface rather than the dial, since the same setting behaves differently with different volumes.

  Simmering is the working temperature of stocks and broths, dried beans and lentils after their first boil, soups, ragù and other meat sauces, curries, and Japanese simmered dishes cooked under a drop lid (otoshibuta), which keeps the surface covered so the liquid circulates over the food without a hard boil.
