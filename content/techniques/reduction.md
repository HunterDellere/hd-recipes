about: |
  Reducing removes water and leaves everything else behind. Flavor compounds, salt, sugar, acids, and dissolved gelatin and starch all become more concentrated, which is why a reduced stock tastes deeper and coats a spoon, and why a tomato sauce cooked down turns sweet and jammy. Gelatin from bones and collagen-rich cuts is the main thickener in meat reductions; pectin and sugars do the work in fruit and tomato reductions.

  Salt last. Salt does not evaporate, so a liquid seasoned correctly at the start becomes too salty once it has reduced by half. Season reductions at the end, after they have reached the right consistency.

  Speed and pan shape. Evaporation happens at the surface, so a wide pan reduces much faster than a tall pot. A steady simmer reduces cleanly; a hard boil is faster but emulsifies fat into the liquid, turning it cloudy, and scorches sugary or thick liquids at the bottom and edges. Stir and scrape thick reductions such as tomato sauces as they cook down.

  How far to go. Most sauce reductions are judged by the nappe test: the sauce coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through it leaves a clean line. Glazes go further, until bubbles turn large and glossy and the sauce coats the food in a shiny layer. Taste as you go; past a point, reduction concentrates bitterness and acidity along with the good flavors.

  Reduction finishes pan sauces after deglazing, braising liquids at the end of a braise, wine and stock sauces, tomato sauces and ragù, Japanese teriyaki and simmered-dish glazes, and caramel-and-fish-sauce glazes in Vietnamese cooking.
