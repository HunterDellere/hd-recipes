about: |
  Sweating softens onions, leeks, celery, carrots, fennel, garlic, and peppers by cooking them in fat over medium-low heat until their cell walls break down and they release water. The water keeps the pan at or near 100 °C, too cool for browning, so the vegetables turn sweet, soft, and translucent and keep a clean, pale flavor.

  Salt does part of the work. A pinch of salt added with the vegetables draws water out of the cells by osmosis, which softens them faster and keeps the pan moist enough to prevent browning. Covering the pan for part of the time does the same by trapping steam.

  Sweating, sautéing, and caramelizing are the same vegetables at different points. Sweated onions (8 to 12 minutes, no color) disappear into a soup, risotto, or pale sauce. Sautéed onions (higher heat, 6 to 10 minutes, golden edges) add some roasted sweetness to stews and chili. Caramelized onions (low heat, 40 minutes or more, deep brown throughout) are a flavor of their own. Choose by the color and flavor the finished dish should have.

  Failure modes. Too high a heat browns the edges before the centers soften, adding bitterness to dishes meant to be clean. Garlic added at the start with onions often scorches before the onions are done; add it in the last minute or two. Undersweated onions stay crunchy and sharp, and no amount of later simmering fully fixes that.

  Sweating is the first step of French soups and sauces, Italian soffritto, Spanish sofrito, risotto and pilaf, Persian and Indian onion bases cooked to translucence, and most vegetable soups.
