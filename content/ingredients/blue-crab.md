about: |
  Blue crab is a swimming crab of the Portunidae family, native to the western Atlantic from Nova Scotia to Argentina and fished most heavily in the Chesapeake Bay, the Carolinas, and the Gulf of Mexico. The last pair of legs is flattened into paddles, the shell is olive to blue-green raw and turns orange-red when cooked, and a legal adult is 12 to 18 cm across the points of the carapace. A 150 to 200 g crab yields only 25 to 30 g of picked meat (about 14% of live weight), which is why picked meat is expensive and why whole crabs are eaten as an event rather than an ingredient.

  The meat is high in free glycine, alanine, arginine, and glutamate, plus nucleotides (AMP), which together read as sweet and savory at once. It is more mineral and briny than Dungeness and finer in grain. Crab muscle is low in fat and connective tissue, so it is fully cooked and tender at 63 °C / 145 °F and turns stringy and dry with long or repeated heating. Since nearly all picked crab is sold already cooked, cooking it again is really a matter of reheating the meat while setting whatever surrounds it (an egg binder, a custard, a soup).

  Picked meat is graded by where it comes from. Jumbo lump is the two large muscles that drive the swimming legs: white, whole, and the priciest. Lump is broken jumbo pieces plus large body flakes. Backfin (or special) is smaller white body flakes. Claw meat is brownish-pink, stronger in flavor, and the cheapest. The grade should match the job: jumbo lump where the meat is seen and bitten whole (crab cakes, salads, cocktails), lump or backfin where it is mixed (dips, stuffings, imperial), and claw where it flavors a liquid (soups, gumbos, bisques, pasta sauces).

  Applications by category. In Chesapeake cooking it is the crab cake, crab imperial, Maryland crab soup, and the bushel of steamed crabs crusted with seafood seasoning. In the Lowcountry it is she-crab soup, enriched with roe. In Louisiana and the Gulf it goes into gumbo, stuffed crabs, and boils with corn and potatoes. The molted, soft-shell stage (late spring) is eaten whole, dredged and fried. Outside the US it works anywhere a sweet, small-flaked crab is wanted: Thai and Vietnamese crab fried rice, Italian crab pasta, and crab omelets.

  Buying notes. Live crabs should be heavy for their size and actively moving. For picked meat, buy refrigerated fresh (sold in tubs at the seafood counter, best within days of picking) rather than pasteurized; pasteurization firms the meat into shreds and adds a metallic note. Shelf-stable cans in the tuna aisle are a different, lesser product. Much "blue crab" meat sold in the US is actually imported swimming crab (Portunus species) from Asia; it is acceptable, slightly less sweet, and is labeled by country of origin.

storage: 'Live crabs: cook the same day; hold up to 24 hours in a cooler or bushel basket over ice, covered with a damp towel, never submerged in fresh water (they drown). Dead raw crabs spoil within hours and should be discarded. Fresh picked meat: keep at 0 to 2 °C in the coldest part of the fridge and use within 3 to 5 days of the pick date; spoiled meat smells of ammonia and feels slimy. Pasteurized sealed tubs: unopened up to 6 months refrigerated (check the date), then 3 days after opening. Frozen picked meat keeps 3 months but loses its lumps and weeps on thawing; use it for soups, not crab cakes.'
