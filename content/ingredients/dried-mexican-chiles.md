about: |
  Most dried Mexican chiles are fully ripened chiles of Capsicum annuum, dried until they hold about 10 to 15% water, and many change name when they dry. Ancho is a dried ripe poblano. Mulato is a dried poblano of a darker strain. Guajillo is a dried mirasol. Pasilla (chile negro) is a dried chilaca. Chipotle is a ripe jalapeño dried over wood smoke; the small, red, pliable morita is the form most sold in the United States, and the tan, leathery meco is smokier. Árbol is dried under its own name. New Mexico and California chiles are dried ripe New Mexico chiles and Anaheims. Drying concentrates the sugars and the red carotenoid pigments, and the slow browning that happens as the pods dry gives the dried-fruit, coffee, and leather notes that fresh chiles do not have.

  The varieties to know, with approximate heat in Scoville units (individual pods vary widely):
  - **Ancho** (about 1,000 to 2,000): wide, wrinkled, mahogany. Sweet, raisin and prune notes, mild. Supplies body and sweetness.
  - **Mulato** (about 2,500 to 3,000): like ancho but nearly black, with a bittersweet chocolate and licorice note. Central to mole poblano.
  - **Guajillo** (about 2,500 to 5,000): long, smooth, shiny, bright red. Tangy, berry-like, with green tea notes. Supplies color and acidity.
  - **Pasilla** (about 1,000 to 2,500): long, thin, black, wrinkled. Bitter, herbal, raisin-like. Supplies depth and dark color.
  - **Chipotle morita** (about 5,000 to 10,000): small, plum-colored, leathery. Smoky and sweet with real heat. Also sold canned in adobo sauce.
  - **Árbol** (about 15,000 to 30,000): small, thin, bright red. Sharp, clean heat with little flavor of its own. Used to set the heat level.
  - **New Mexico** (mild to hot; from about 800 to over 5,000): long, smooth, deep red, sold by heat grade and by region (Hatch, Chimayó). Earthy and sweet with a slight tartness. Carries a sauce on its own.
  - **California** (about 500 to 2,500): like a milder, sweeter New Mexico chile. Mostly color.

  How they work in a dish. Mexican cooking treats dried chiles as the body of a sauce rather than as a spice: a mole, adobo, or red chile sauce is mostly rehydrated chile flesh blended with liquid. The flesh contributes sugars, pectin and fiber that thicken the sauce, carotenoid pigments that color it, and the browned, fruity flavors of drying. Heat comes from capsaicin, which is concentrated in the pale ribs (the placenta) that hold the seeds rather than in the seeds themselves; removing seeds and ribs lowers heat and some bitterness. Cooks build a sauce from complementary varieties: ancho or mulato for body and sweetness, guajillo or New Mexico for color and tang, pasilla for depth, chipotle for smoke, árbol for heat.

  Toasting, soaking, frying. Three steps transform the pods. A brief dry toast in a hot pan wakes up the aromatics and deepens the flavor, but the window is short: thin-skinned guajillo, New Mexico and árbol scorch in 5 to 15 seconds per side, thicker ancho and mulato in about 20, and a blackened pod turns the whole sauce bitter. Soaking in hot water for 15 to 30 minutes softens them for blending; the soaking water takes up bitterness from the skins and is usually discarded, though some cooks keep a little of it for milder pods. Finally, the blended purée is fried in hot fat for several minutes until it darkens and thickens, which cooks off the raw, grassy edge, concentrates the sauce, and develops sweetness. Skins of guajillo and New Mexico chiles stay gritty after blending, so sauces made from them are usually strained.

  Where they appear. Moles (ancho, mulato, pasilla, chipotle), adobos and marinades for grilled and roasted meats such as al pastor, red pozole and other chile broths, New Mexico red chile sauce and carne adovada, Texas and Tex-Mex chili, table salsas and chile oils such as salsa macha, and enchilada sauces. The same handful of varieties covers nearly all of them, which is why a small stock of ancho, guajillo, and one hot chile is enough for most of this cooking.

  Buying. Good pods are pliable, like a fruit leather, with a glossy surface and a strong fruity smell when the bag is opened. Brittle pods that crack when bent, faded or dusty color, and little smell are old and taste flat or bitter. Mexican groceries turn their stock faster than supermarket spice aisles. Names are not standardized in the United States: fresh poblanos are sometimes labeled pasilla, and dried anchos are sometimes labeled pasilla too, so go by appearance. The common Western shortcut is chili powder blend, which mixes ground chile with cumin, garlic, and oregano and loses the separate jobs that each whole chile does.

seasonality: 'Year-round as dried pods. The new crop of New Mexico red chile is dried in autumn after the green harvest, and freshly dried pods appear in late autumn and winter.'
storage: |
  In a sealed glass jar in a cool, dark cupboard: about 6 months at full quality, up to a year usable. In the freezer: a year or more with the aroma largely intact; pods thaw pliable in minutes. Failure looks like brittleness, fading from deep red or mahogany to brick or brown, and a dusty or hay-like smell in place of a fruity one; such pods make flat, bitter sauces. Check for pantry moths, which lay eggs in dried chiles: fine webbing or small holes mean the batch should be discarded. Freezing new pods for 3 days kills any eggs.

substitutions:
  - for: 'ancho (body and sweetness)'
    use: 'mulato, or ancho plus a pasilla'
    note: 'Mulato is darker and more bitter; a pasilla alongside ancho gets close to mulato. Weight for weight.'
  - for: 'guajillo (red color and tang)'
    use: 'New Mexico or California chiles'
    note: 'Same weight. New Mexico is earthier, California milder; both give similar color but less tang, so a little more vinegar or lime in the dish helps.'
  - for: 'pasilla (dark, bitter depth)'
    use: 'mulato, or more ancho with a small piece of unsweetened chocolate'
    note: 'Closer in color than in flavor; the dish will be sweeter.'
  - for: 'chipotle morita (smoke and heat)'
    use: 'chipotle in adobo, or smoked paprika plus árbol'
    note: 'One canned chipotle in adobo per dried morita. Smoked paprika supplies smoke without heat, so add an árbol for the heat.'
  - for: 'árbol (heat only)'
    use: 'cayenne or other small hot dried chiles'
    note: 'About 1 g cayenne per árbol. Japones or Thai bird chiles also work.'
  - for: 'whole dried chiles (any sauce)'
    use: 'pure ground chile of the same variety'
    note: 'About 10 g ground chile per 12 g whole pod. Fry it briefly in fat before adding liquid. Avoid chili powder blends, which include cumin, garlic, and oregano.'
