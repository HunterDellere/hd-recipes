about: |
  Water is the solvent every other ingredient on this site dissolves into, and it is never pure. Even good tap water carries tens to hundreds of milligrams per litre (mg/L) of dissolved minerals. A bottled mineral water can carry several thousand. Those ions decide whether beans soften, how fast tea and coffee extract, how tight a dough feels, and how the water tastes on its own. The one number that summarises them is **total dissolved solids (TDS)**: the weight of everything dissolved in a litre. Labels often print it as "dry residue at 180 °C", which is close enough to read the same way.

  The second idea is **hardness**: the calcium and magnesium content, usually expressed as mg/L of calcium carbonate. The third is **alkalinity**: mostly bicarbonate, which buffers acid. Hardness gives body and drives most of the kitchen effects below. Alkalinity mutes acidity, which matters for coffee, tea and wine.

  All mineral figures on this page are in mg/L. Most come from the Fine Water Society database, brand analyses and retailer copies of labels, and sources often disagree by 10 to 20 percent. Springs drift over time and brands change sources, so the label on the bottle in your hand is the authority. Where sources conflict, this page gives a range.

  ## Water in the kitchen

  **Beans and other legumes.** Calcium and magnesium cross-link the pectin in bean cell walls and slow softening. In very hard water, dried beans can stay firm after hours of simmering. A pinch of baking soda raises the pH, loosens the pectin and speeds softening, at some cost to flavour and texture if overdone. Soft or filtered water avoids the problem.

  **Tea.** Low-mineral water extracts tea cleanly. Hard water, especially water high in calcium bicarbonate, dulls the flavour and leaves a film on the surface of black tea. Green and white tea want water under about 100 mg/L TDS. Black tea and pu-erh tolerate more. See [steeping tea](../techniques/steeping-tea.html).

  **Coffee.** Some minerals are needed to extract flavour, and too much bicarbonate flattens acidity. The 2009 standard of the Specialty Coffee Association of America targets:
  - TDS 150, acceptable range 75 to 250.
  - Calcium hardness 68 as calcium carbonate, acceptable range 17 to 85.
  - Alkalinity about 40.
  - pH 7.0, acceptable range 6.5 to 7.5.
  - Sodium about 10, and no chlorine.

  Castle Rock (TDS about 100) is close on its own. A 50/50 blend of Mountain Valley and Saratoga lands near the target; that blend is a calculation, not a tested result.

  **Bread.** Moderately hard water strengthens gluten slightly. Very soft water gives a slacker, stickier dough, and very hard water can tighten it and slow fermentation. Chlorine in tap water can slow a sourdough starter, and letting the water stand uncovered for some hours lets much of it escape (chloramine does not).

  **Batter.** Cold sparkling water in a tempura or fried-fish batter adds bubbles that expand in the oil, and the cold limits gluten development. The result is a lighter, lacier crust. Plain soda water works; the mineral content matters less than the gas and the temperature.

  **Stock, ice and cocktails.** Hard water clouds ice and can leave scale in kettles and stockpots. For clear ice and clean cocktails, a low-mineral water helps. Topo Chico and similar bold sparkling waters are the standard highball and ranch-water mixers.

  ## What each mineral does

  - **Calcium.** It tastes soft and faintly chalky or milky-sweet. It supports bones and teeth, and calcium from mineral water absorbs as well as calcium from milk. EU label term: "contains calcium" means more than 150 mg/L. The adult daily need is about 1,000 to 1,200 mg.
  - **Magnesium.** It tastes bitter and drying above about 50 mg/L. It supports muscles, nerves and sleep, and most Western diets fall short. With sulfate, it acts as a laxative. EU: "contains magnesium" means more than 50. The adult daily need is about 310 to 420 mg.
  - **Bicarbonate.** It gives the smooth, slightly sweet, soft finish. It buffers stomach acid, which is the basis of the heartburn evidence below, and it dulls an acidic wine. EU: "contains bicarbonate" means more than 600.
  - **Sodium.** It rounds and salts the water, and you taste it clearly above about 200. It replaces sweat losses, but it counts toward a daily limit of about 2,300 mg. EU: "suitable for a low-sodium diet" means under 20; "contains sodium" means more than 200.
  - **Sulfate.** It tastes bitter and dry. With magnesium it is laxative. EU: "contains sulfate" means more than 200.
  - **Chloride.** It tastes salty and usually comes paired with sodium. EU: "contains chloride" means more than 200.
  - **Silica.** It gives a silky, smooth mouthfeel. It explains the smoothness of Fiji and Socosani. Some small studies suggest silica-rich water increases aluminium excretion; clinical benefit is not established.
  - **Fluoride.** It protects teeth at low doses and causes dental fluorosis in children at high doses. EU: "contains fluoride" means more than 1 mg/L. Above 1.5 mg/L the label must warn that the water is not suitable for regular consumption by infants and children under 7, and must state the fluoride content. The legal maximum for natural mineral water is 5 mg/L.
  - **Nitrate.** It has no taste and signals farming or sewage influence near the source. Under 10 is good and under 2 is excellent. The legal maximum is 50.
  - **Free carbon dioxide.** Above 250 mg/L, a water may be labelled "acidic".

  Other EU label terms: "low mineral content" means a dry residue of 500 or less; "very low mineral content" means 50 or less; "rich in mineral salts" means more than 1,500.

  ## How to taste water

  A water sommelier judges five things:
  1. **TDS.** The Fine Water Society bands are super low (under about 50), low (50 to 250), medium (250 to 800), high (800 to 1,500) and very high (over about 1,500).
  2. **Carbonation.** Still, light, classic or bold, and whether the gas is natural or added at bottling. Natural carbonation tends to give finer bubbles.
  3. **pH.** Below 7 tastes slightly tart. Above 8 can feel soft. Carbon dioxide always lowers it.
  4. **Hardness.** It gives body and chalky dryness.
  5. **Virginality.** Freedom from human influence, usually read from nitrate.

  In judging a water, mouthfeel counts most, mineral density next, and pH least.

  **Method**
  1. Serve every sample at the same temperature. Some sommeliers taste at room temperature; others use 12 to 17 °C. Never serve ice-cold, because cold numbs the tongue.
  2. Use identical stemmed glasses, about 60 ml each.
  3. Taste from the lowest TDS to the highest, and still before sparkling.
  4. Look at clarity and bubbles. Smell: a good water smells of nothing, and chlorine, plastic or must is a fault.
  5. Hold a small sip for three seconds. Notice weight, then salt, bitterness or sweetness, then the finish.
  6. Reset between waters with a super-low water or a plain unsalted cracker. Taste blind where you can.

  **Vocabulary.** Weight: light, round, full, thick. Texture: soft, silky, crisp, chalky, dry. Taste: sweet (calcium, alkaline), bitter (magnesium, sulfate), salty (sodium, chloride), tart (carbon dioxide), metallic, mineral. Bubbles: fine, lively, aggressive, fading. Finish: short, long, clean, drying.

  ## Notable waters

  **Everyday waters**
  - **Aqua Carpatica, still (Romania).** Low mineral, square glass bottle. TDS about 170 to 200; calcium 46 to 52; magnesium 13 to 17; sodium under 1; nitrate near zero; pH about 8. Clean and softly sweet. An everyday base water for a low-sodium diet. Its naturally sparkling sibling is very different: calcium 260 to 296, magnesium 77 to 89, sodium about 5, dry residue about 1,000. That makes it one of the few very mineral-rich waters that is almost sodium-free.
  - **Gerolsteiner Sparkling (Germany).** Volcanic Eifel, naturally carbonated. TDS about 2,490 to 2,530; calcium 348; magnesium 108; sodium 118; bicarbonate 1,816; silica about 40; pH 5.9. Salty-chalky, bold bubbles. One litre gives about a third of daily calcium and a quarter to a third of daily magnesium.
  - **Galvanina (Italy).** Rimini, embossed glass, carbon dioxide added. TDS about 475 to 580; calcium 113 to 135; sodium about 35. Rounded and calcium-led; an Italian table water.
  - **Agua de Piedra (Mexico).** Huasteca, near Monterrey, green glass with a paper label. A 2025 lab report gives TDS 330; calcium 93; magnesium 14; sodium 13; sulfate 77; pH 8.1. A retailer page claims calcium 440, so check the label.
  - **Icelandic Glacial (Iceland).** Ölfus Spring, lava-filtered. TDS 62 to 69; calcium about 6; pH 8.4 to 8.7. Very light and soft; good for tea and whisky.
  - **Hallstein (Austria).** Artesian, 214 m below Obertraun. TDS 84 to 98; calcium about 20 and magnesium about 10; sodium about 0.15; pH 8.3 to 8.6. It scores at the top of independent contaminant testing.

  **Mineral-rich and specialist waters**
  - **Gerolsteiner Ursprung (Germany).** The most mineralised Gerolsteiner. Calcium about 590; magnesium about 167; bicarbonate about 3,144; sodium about 223. Small glasses only.
  - **St. Gero Heilwasser (Germany).** A licensed medicinal water. Total minerals about 2,470; calcium about 331; magnesium about 109; bicarbonate about 1,775.
  - **ROI (Slovenia).** Rogaška Slatina, bottled since 1647. TDS about 7,500; magnesium about 1,300; sodium about 1,600; sulfate about 2,100. Intensely salty and bitter; laxative. Sip 100 to 250 ml.
  - **Three Bays (Australia).** Mornington Peninsula, 900 m aquifer. TDS about 1,300; pH about 8.3. A rare high-mineral water that stays alkaline and still.
  - **Antipodes (New Zealand).** Volcanic ignimbrite, 327 m. TDS about 130; silica about 73; sodium about 12. Silky; a reference water for fine dining and wine.
  - **San Mateo (Peru).** Andean spring above 3,300 m. Calcium about 90; magnesium about 11; sodium about 32. Hard to find outside Peru.
  - **Ararat (Armenia).** Sparkling bicarbonate water. TDS about 2,478; pH about 6.6. A morning water; it pairs with roast chicken.
  - **Vichy Catalan (Spain).** A 60 °C thermal spring, with the spring's own gas re-added at bottling. Dry residue 2,850 to 3,052; sodium 1,080 to 1,110; bicarbonate 1,990 to 2,135; chloride about 590; pH about 6.2. Fluoride measured at 0.82 mg/L in a 2012 Spanish survey. A sommelier favourite and a classic hangover water. One litre carries about half the daily sodium limit.
  - **Mountain Valley (USA).** Hot Springs, Arkansas, green glass since 1871. TDS 220 to 230; calcium 67 to 74; magnesium about 7; sodium about 3; pH 7.3 to 7.8. The best widely available American everyday water.
  - **Saratoga (USA).** Cobalt bottle, now sourced in Maine and Pennsylvania. TDS about 30 to 75; calcium 7.5 to 11; sodium about 7. A sushi water and a good palate cleanser.

  ## Classic waters

  - **Topo Chico (Mexico).** TDS 490 to 630; calcium 120 to 140; sodium 33 to 41; pH about 5.5. Bold bubbles; spicy food and cocktails.
  - **San Pellegrino (Italy).** Carbon dioxide added. TDS about 854 to 880; calcium about 160; magnesium about 50; sulfate about 400 to 420. Fine bubbles and a dry finish; tomato, cheese, cured meat.
  - **Acqua Panna (Italy).** Still. Dry residue 142 to 188; calcium about 31; sodium about 6.5. Soft; wine service.
  - **Evian (France).** Still. Dry residue about 345; calcium about 80; magnesium about 26; bicarbonate about 360. Dependable daily water.
  - **Vittel (France).** Still and high mineral: dry residue 841 to 1,084; calcium 202 to 240; sulfate 306 to 400.
  - **Hépar (France).** Dry residue about 2,513; calcium about 549; magnesium about 119; sulfate about 1,530; sodium about 14. A researched constipation water, now scarce.
  - **Contrex (France).** Dry residue about 2,078; calcium about 468; sulfate about 1,121; sodium about 9.
  - **Badoit (France).** Naturally lightly sparkling. Dry residue 1,100 to 1,200; bicarbonate 1,250 to 1,300; sodium 150 to 180; fluoride about 1.2. Very fine bubbles; a digestif.
  - **Staatl. Fachingen (Germany).** Licensed medicinal water. Bicarbonate about 1,812 to 1,854; sodium about 560 to 600. The water from the heartburn trial; note the sodium.
  - **Donat Mg (Slovenia).** TDS about 13,000; magnesium about 1,000 to 1,040; sulfate about 2,100. The most-studied constipation water.
  - **Svalbarði (Norway).** Iceberg water, TDS about 21. A luxury curiosity.
  - **Volvic (France).** Volcanic. Dry residue 108 to 130; silica about 32. Cheap and soft.
  - **Fiji (Fiji).** TDS 220 to 240; silica 85 to 99. Silky, sold in plastic. Its silica suits red wine, but it has been criticised over arsenic, which was not verified here.
  - **Lauretana (Italy).** Dry residue 14 to 15. Among the lightest waters in Europe.

  ## Pairing water with food and drink

  The rules:
  1. Match intensity. Delicate food takes a low-TDS still water. Rich, fatty or salty food takes a high-mineral or sparkling water.
  2. Carbonation cuts fat.
  3. Bicarbonate softens acidity. That helps next to spicy or acidic food and hurts next to a fine wine.
  4. Sodium amplifies savoury, and clashes with dessert.
  5. Magnesium and sulfate add bitterness, so keep them away from dark chocolate, radicchio and coffee.
  6. Silica smooths. Silica-rich water softens red-wine tannin.

  By dish:
  - Sushi, sashimi, oysters: Saratoga, Waiākea, Antipodes, Lurisia.
  - Light fish and salads: Proud Source, Icelandic Glacial, Iskilde.
  - Poached fish and fresh fruit: Mountain Valley.
  - Roast chicken: Ararat.
  - Pasta and tomato: Socosani, Galvanina, San Pellegrino.
  - Steak, burgers, barbecue: Vichy Catalan, Crazy Water No. 3, Gerolsteiner.
  - Fried food and tempura: Gerolsteiner, Topo Chico, Agua de Piedra.
  - Mexican and spicy food: Agua de Piedra, Topo Chico.
  - Cheese and charcuterie: Ferrarelle, Gerolsteiner, San Pellegrino.
  - Curry: a medium sparkling water such as Galvanina or Perrier.
  - Dessert and chocolate: a soft still water such as Acqua Panna or Hallstein.

  By drink:
  - White wine: Antipodes, Acqua Panna, Hallstein. Chlorine in tap water overpowers wine.
  - Red wine: a silica-rich water such as Fiji, Antipodes or Waiākea.
  - Whisky: a super-low still water such as Icelandic Glacial, Lauretana or Saratoga.
  - Coffee and tea: see "Water in the kitchen" above.

  ## Water and health

  Every water hydrates, and volume matters before brand. A high-mineral water is also a real mineral source. Sodium is the main trade-off: Vichy Catalan, Borjomi, ROI, Fachingen, Apollinaris and Ursprung are all high in it. Aqua Carpatica Sparkling, Hépar and Contrex give high minerals with little sodium.

  What the evidence supports, with its strength:
  - **Calcium from water is well absorbed** (strong, small trials). Robert Heaney's 2006 review found mineral-water calcium absorbs as well as or slightly better than milk calcium.
  - **Magnesium-sulfate waters relieve constipation** (moderate, placebo-controlled trials). Donat Mg, 500 ml a day for 6 weeks, improved stool frequency and consistency in 106 adults (Bothe et al., 2017, published online 2015; a co-author works for the producer). Hépar, 1 L a day, gave a response within 7 days in 226 patients (Dupont et al., 2019).
  - **Bicarbonate-rich water reduces heartburn** (moderate, one double-blind trial). In STOMACH STILL, 1.5 L a day of Staatl. Fachingen for 6 weeks gave about 85 percent responders against 64 percent for a low-mineral placebo water (2023; some authors had ties to the producer). At about 560 to 600 mg/L sodium, that dose carries about 850 to 900 mg of sodium.
  - **Magnesium in drinking water and heart disease** (weak and mixed). Case-control meta-analyses find about 25 percent lower heart-disease mortality where tap water is rich in magnesium. A 25-year British cohort found no protection.
  - **Hydration and cognition, headache and kidney stones** (moderate). Mild dehydration impairs attention and mood, and higher water intake reduces kidney-stone recurrence.

  Situations:
  - **Training.** Under an hour, any still water. For long or hot sessions, sweat loses about 0.5 to 1.5 g of sodium per litre, so add electrolytes or small amounts of Vichy Catalan or Borjomi. For recovery, a calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate water such as Gerolsteiner, Ararat or St. Gero.
  - **Heartburn.** A high-bicarbonate still water such as Fachingen or St. Gero. Sparkling water can make reflux worse.
  - **Constipation.** ROI, Donat Mg or Hépar, starting with 250 ml in the morning.
  - **After a heavy meal or a late night.** A small glass of Vichy Catalan, Badoit or Gerolsteiner, then a soft still water.
  - **Low-sodium diet.** Hallstein, Valser Still, Aqua Carpatica, Lurisia, Mountain Valley, Icelandic Glacial.

  People with kidney disease, high blood pressure or heart failure should check with a doctor before drinking high-mineral waters daily. Infant formula needs a water labelled suitable for infant food. Calcium and magnesium can block absorption of tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics and of thyroid hormone, so separate them by a few hours.

  ## Picks by occasion

  A quick list by occasion, not a quality ranking: Ararat or a soft still water for the morning, Borjomi for the gym, Icelandic Glacial for a light lunch, Mountain Valley for daily drinking, Gerolsteiner for calcium, Fiji or Antipodes for red wine, Saratoga for sushi, Socosani for pasta, and Vichy Catalan for steak and hangovers. Iskilde and Vichy Catalan are sommelier favourites. Across all of them: TDS matters far more than pH, natural spring water beats purified water, water is best cool rather than iced, and glass beats plastic.

  ## Tasting flights

  Each flight teaches one contrast.
  1. **TDS ladder, still:** Lauretana, Saratoga, Icelandic Glacial, Mountain Valley, Evian, Vittel, Hépar or Contrex.
  2. **Calcium, magnesium, sodium:** Contrex, then Hépar or Donat, then Vichy Catalan. Sweet-chalky, bitter-dry, salty.
  3. **Bicarbonate against sulfate:** Fachingen, Vichy Catalan and Borjomi against San Pellegrino, Hépar and Valser Calcium + Magnesium.
  4. **Natural against added carbonation:** Gerolsteiner, Badoit and Perrier against San Pellegrino, Galvanina and Mountain Valley Sparkling.
  5. **Silica:** Volvic, Waiākea, Antipodes, Fiji.
  6. **Low against high sodium:** Hallstein, Aqua Carpatica Sparkling, Gerolsteiner, Gerolsteiner Ursprung, Vichy Catalan.
  7. **Source curiosities:** Svalbarði or Berg, Kona Deep, Waiākea.

  For deeper study, the Fine Water Academy runs an online certification of about $2,200, and the Doemens Academy near Munich runs a 10-day water sommelier course.

  ## Myths and marketing

  - **Alkaline water does not change body pH.** Stomach acid sits at pH 1.5 to 3.5, and the lungs and kidneys hold blood pH tightly. Bicarbonate does buffer stomach acid locally, which is the heartburn effect.
  - **High TDS does not mean dirty.** TDS measures minerals, not contaminants.
  - **Low TDS is not purer, only lighter.** Very low-mineral water supplies no calcium or magnesium.
  - **Check "electrolyte" claims against the numbers.** Kona Deep has about 3 mg/L magnesium.
  - **Mineral water, spring water and purified water differ.** An EU natural mineral water comes from a protected underground source, is bottled at source with a stable composition and is barely treated. Spring water has looser rules. Purified or drinking water (Dasani, Aquafina, Smartwater, Essentia) is often treated municipal water.
  - **Price is not quality.** Mountain Valley, Gerolsteiner, Topo Chico and Ararat are excellent and inexpensive.

  ## Contaminants and packaging

  - **PFAS** ("forever chemicals") turn up in some bottled and tap waters. The US EPA set tap-water limits in 2024.
  - **Micro- and nanoplastics.** A 2024 *PNAS* study estimated about 240,000 plastic particles per litre of plastic-bottled water, about 90 percent of them nanoplastics. Glass and keeping water away from heat reduce exposure.
  - **Natural contaminants** such as arsenic and uranium occur in some springs. Every US bottler must provide a water quality report on request; read TDS, sodium, nitrate, PFAS and arsenic, and the year of the analysis.
  - **Fluoride** appears on only a minority of labels; one Spanish survey found 28 of 109 brands stated it.

storage: 'Store bottled water cool, dark and away from solvents or strong odours, which can migrate through plastic. Heat and sunlight increase the release of plastic particles and compounds from plastic bottles, so never leave them in a hot car. Glass keeps longest and holds carbonation best. Once opened, sparkling water loses its gas within a day even when recapped; still water is best within a few days, refrigerated. Tap water left standing for a few hours loses much of its chlorine, but not chloramine. Kettles and stockpots used with hard water build white scale, which a soak in dilute citric acid or vinegar dissolves.'
