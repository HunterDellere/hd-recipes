steps:
  - text: 'Thin the paste. In a bowl, whisk the 120 g / ½ cup huangjiang and the 60 g / ¼ cup tianmianjiang with the 180 ml / ¾ cup water until completely smooth, with no lumps of dry paste left. It should look like thick chocolate milk. Do not add salt at any point in the sauce: huangjiang is heavily salted and supplies all of it.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Prepare the raw toppings. Cut the 200 g cucumber and the 150 g watermelon radish into fine julienne about 2 mm thick, and slice the 20 g scallion greens. Peel the 32 g / 8 garlic cloves. Keep each topping in its own pile; the bowl is assembled at the table.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Blanch the cooked toppings. Bring the 4000 ml / 4 quarts water to a rolling boil. Drop in the 150 g mung bean sprouts for 30 seconds, lift them out with a spider, and rinse under cold water so they stay crisp. Add the 120 g frozen edamame to the same water for 2 minutes, lift out, and drain. Keep the pot at a simmer for the noodles.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Render the pork. Set a wok over medium heat with the 30 g / 2 tbsp neutral oil and add the 450 g diced pork belly. Stir every 30 seconds for about 8 minutes, until the fat has turned translucent, a clear layer of rendered fat pools in the wok, and the lean edges are golden. Pour the 15 g / 1 tbsp Shaoxing wine around the edge of the wok; it boils off in seconds and takes the raw-pork smell with it.'
    technique: 'lardon-rendering'
    time_min: 9
  - text: 'Add the aromatics. Stir in the 40 g scallion whites and the 10 g minced ginger and fry for 30 seconds, until fragrant but not browned.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Fry the sauce. Pour in all of the thinned paste (about 360 g), turn the heat down to low, and stir with a wooden spoon, scraping the bottom of the wok every minute or so. The sauce first bubbles in large, loose bursts; as water cooks off, the bubbles shrink and pop slowly. After 15 to 20 minutes three things happen together: the color goes from tan to a deep chestnut brown, a glossy layer of red-brown oil separates out and pools around the edges, and the smell changes from raw bean to roasted soy. That separated oil is the sign the sauce is done; stop when you see it, because past that point the paste dries and turns bitter. The pork is far past 63 °C / 145 °F after this long in the wok. Taste: it should be too salty to eat on its own, because it seasons a whole bowl of plain noodles.'
    time_min: 18
  - text: 'Cook the noodles. Bring the reserved pot back to a rolling boil and cook the 600 g fresh noodles until just tender with a firm center, usually 3 to 4 minutes for fresh noodles (check the package). From autumn into spring, serve them the way Beijing does in cold weather: lifted straight from the pot into warmed bowls, unrinsed, so the hot starch holds the sauce. In summer, rinse them under cold water instead.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Assemble. Share the 600 g noodles evenly among the bowls. Spoon about 130 g of the fried sauce, with plenty of its oil, onto the center of each bowl. Arrange the 200 g cucumber, 150 g radish, 150 g sprouts, 120 g edamame, and 20 g scallion greens in small piles around the sauce, a quarter of each per bowl.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'At the table. Each person tosses their bowl hard with chopsticks until every strand is coated brown and the vegetables are mixed through. Set out the 32 g / 8 raw garlic cloves to bite between mouthfuls, and the 30 g / 2 tbsp Chinkiang black vinegar to splash on at about 7 g / 1½ tsp per bowl; the acid and the raw garlic cut through the pork fat and the salt of the paste.'
    time_min: 2

notes: |
  Why two pastes. Huangjiang is a dry, salty soybean paste with a deep fermented flavor; tianmianjiang is a wheat-flour paste, sweeter and glossier. Beijing sauce is built mostly on the yellow paste, which keeps it savory rather than sweet, with the sweet paste rounding the edges. A sauce made only from tianmianjiang (common outside China) reads like hoisin noodles. The fermented soybean supplies the glutamate, so no soy sauce or MSG is needed.

  Why fry slowly. The paste needs to fry in fat, not simmer in water. As the water cooks off, the paste starts to fry in the rendered pork fat, and sugars and amino acids in the paste brown (the same Maillard reactions as a sear, at lower heat over a longer time). When the oil separates, the water is gone and the paste is frying. High heat gets there faster but scorches the bottom before the top has cooked, so keep it low and keep scraping.

  On vinegar and garlic. The sauce itself carries no acid. Black vinegar and raw garlic at the table are how Beijing eaters cut the fat, and many add a splash of vinegar without thinking about it. Use both; they make the bowl.

  Seasons. The cai ma toppings change through the year. In spring, Beijing uses fresh toon shoots and young garlic; in summer, cucumber and fresh soybeans dominate; in winter, radish and napa cabbage take over. Watermelon radish and daikon are at their best in US markets from autumn into spring.

  Storage. The fried sauce keeps a week in a covered glass container in the refrigerator; the fat sets on top and protects it. Warm it gently in a wok or saucepan before serving.
