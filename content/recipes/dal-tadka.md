steps:
  - text: 'Pressure-cook the dal. Put the 200 g soaked, drained toor dal in a pressure cooker with the 750 ml / 3 cups water, 1 g / ½ tsp turmeric, 4 g / 1 tsp kosher salt, and 5 g / 1 tsp ghee. Seasoning the cooking water salts the lentils through; toor dal does not toughen from salt the way whole beans can. Stovetop cooker: cook over high heat to the first whistle, lower to medium, and cook through 4 more whistles (about 12 minutes). Electric cooker: 12 minutes at high pressure. Let the pressure release naturally for 10 minutes; the dal keeps softening as it depressurizes.'
    time_min: 25
  - text: 'Whisk the dal smooth. Open the cooker and whisk the dal hard for 30 seconds (a wire whisk or a wooden dal ghotni both work). The grains should collapse into a creamy, pale-yellow body with a few soft flecks; if hard centers remain, the dal was undercooked, so close the lid and give it 5 more minutes at pressure.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'First tadka. While the dal releases pressure, heat the 20 g / 1½ tbsp ghee in a heavy saucepan or wide skillet over medium heat. Add the 3 g / 1 tsp cumin seeds and let them sizzle and darken one shade, about 30 seconds; they should smell toasty, not acrid. Blooming the seeds in fat moves their aromatic oils into the ghee, which then carries them through the whole pot.'
    technique: 'spice-tempering'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Build the masala. Add the 120 g / 1 medium chopped onion and cook, stirring with a wooden spoon, until soft and golden at the edges, about 6 minutes. Add the 10 g grated ginger, 10 g / 3 cloves grated garlic, and the 5 g / 1 slit green chili; cook 1 minute until the raw garlic smell is gone. Add the 150 g / 2 chopped Roma tomatoes, 2 g / 1 tsp ground coriander, 2 g / ¾ tsp Kashmiri chili powder, and 3 g / ¾ tsp kosher salt. The salt draws water out of the tomatoes so they break down faster. Cook, stirring and mashing, until the tomatoes collapse into a jammy paste and the ghee separates and pools at the edges, about 6 minutes. That separation is the bhuna stage: the spices are cooked through and no longer taste raw.'
    time_min: 13
  - text: 'Simmer together. Scrape the whisked dal into the masala and add the 250 ml / 1 cup hot water. Bring to a simmer and cook, stirring occasionally, 8 minutes, so the masala flavors the whole pot. Adjust with more hot water as needed: dhaba dal should pour from a ladle in a thick ribbon, thinner than porridge, thicker than soup. It thickens as it stands. Taste and add kosher salt in pinches; the dal should taste fully seasoned before the tadka goes on, since the tadka adds aroma, not salt.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Finishing tadka. Transfer the dal to a serving bowl. Wipe out a small pan, set it over medium heat, and add the 25 g / 2 tbsp ghee. When it shimmers, add the 2 g / ½ tsp cumin seeds; when they sizzle, add the 8 g / 2 cloves sliced garlic and the 2 dried red chilies. Fry, swirling, until the garlic turns light gold, about 1 minute. Pull the pan off the heat, then add the 0.3 g / ⅛ tsp asafoetida and the 2 g / ¾ tsp Kashmiri chili powder; the residual heat blooms them in a few seconds and turns the ghee brick red. Off the heat matters here: both powders scorch and turn bitter within seconds over a live flame, and the garlic keeps coloring after the pan comes off.'
    technique: 'spice-tempering'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Pour and finish. Pour the sizzling tadka over the dal immediately, so it hits the surface while it still crackles. Sprinkle over the 1 g / ½ tsp garam masala, squeeze over the 15 ml / 1 tbsp lime juice, and scatter the 10 g / ¼ cup chopped cilantro. Stir the tadka in at the table, or leave it pooled on top and let each spoonful catch some. Taste once more: if it reads flat, the fix is more lime, not more salt.'
    time_min: 1

notes: |
  Why two tadkas. The first tadka is cooked into the dal: cumin bloomed in ghee, then onion, ginger, garlic, and tomato fried down until the fat separates. It seasons the body of the dish. The second tadka goes on at the last moment and is never simmered, so its aromatics (the toasted garlic, the hing, the brick-red chili ghee) reach the table at full strength. A dal with only the first tadka tastes rounded but quiet; the finishing tadka is what makes dhaba dal smell like dhaba dal.

  Asafoetida. Hing is a dried resin with a strong sulfurous smell in the jar that turns savory and leek-like once it hits hot fat. A pinch adds an allium depth under the garlic and is traditionally credited with making lentils easier to digest. Most hing sold in the US is compounded with wheat flour (and sometimes rice flour) to tame its strength, so check the label if you need the dish gluten-free; brands cut with rice flour only are available. Without it the dal is still good but noticeably flatter.

  Umami and acid. The glutamate here comes from the tomato cooked down to a paste and from the lentils themselves, deepened by the ghee-fried onion. The acid comes in two forms: the tomato in the masala, and lime at the end, which brightens the earthy dal more than any amount of extra salt could.

  Consistency and reheating. Toor dal keeps absorbing water as it stands, and it sets almost solid in the fridge. Reheat with a splash of hot water, whisking until it pours again, and adjust salt after thinning. For leftovers, make a fresh small finishing tadka rather than reheating the old one; it takes 3 minutes and restores the aroma. Keeps 4 days refrigerated and freezes well for 2 months without the tadka.
