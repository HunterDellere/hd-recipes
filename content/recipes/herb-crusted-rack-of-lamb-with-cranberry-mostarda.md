safety_notes:
  - for: 'Pulling rack of lamb at 52 °C / 126 °F and resting to 55 to 57 °C / 131 to 135 °F (below the USDA published-safe 145 °F for whole-muscle lamb)'
    ref: 'safety/meat-doneness#beef-and-lamb-whole-muscle'
    why: 'The USDA published-safe target for whole-muscle lamb is 145 °F (63 °C) followed by a 3-minute rest. Rack of lamb is an intact whole muscle whose surface (where contamination sits) is seared well above pasteurization temperature in the cast iron and again in a 220 °C oven; the interior of an intact muscle is not where pathogens live. The French and American restaurant canon serves rack medium-rare at 55 to 57 °C after rest, and the loin eye turns livery and gray past 60 °C. Cooks serving pregnant, elderly, or immunocompromised guests should roast to 63 °C (pull at 58 °C) and accept a firmer eye.'

before_you_start:
  - 'Make the cranberry mostarda 1 to 7 days ahead. It needs at least 12 hours in the fridge for the mustard seeds to hydrate and soften and for the pungency to settle; made the same day it tastes raw and the seeds pop like grit. Serve it at room temperature, so take it out of the fridge when the lamb goes in to temper.'
  - 'Dry-brine the racks the night before (12 to 24 hours), uncovered on a wire rack in the fridge. A dry surface is what lets the fat cap brown in 3 minutes instead of steaming for 6.'
  - 'Make the breadcrumb crust up to 1 day ahead and keep it covered in the fridge in a lidded container. Grate the parmigiano and zest the lemon before the herbs go in so the zest oils go straight into the crumb.'
  - 'Pull the racks out of the fridge 45 minutes before searing. If the oven is shared with a second roast at the same 220 °C / 425 °F, put the lamb on the upper-middle rack and expect 2 to 3 extra minutes; trust the thermometer, not the clock.'

steps:
  - text: 'The night before, dry-brine. Pat the 1600 g / 2 racks of lamb dry with paper towels. If the fat cap is thicker than 3 mm, trim it down; a thick cap will not render in a 20-minute roast and eats as a waxy layer. Score the fat in a 1 cm crosshatch, cutting through the fat but not into the meat. Season all sides, including between the frenched bones, with the 12 g / 4¼ tsp kosher salt. Set the racks fat-side up on a wire rack over a sheet pan and refrigerate uncovered 12 to 24 hours. The salt first draws moisture out, then the brine diffuses back into the muscle and dissolves part of the myosin, so the eye seasons through and holds more juice under heat; the uncovered rest dries the fat cap so it browns instead of steaming.'
    technique: 'dry-brining'
    time_min: 720
  - text: 'Make the mostarda (1 to 7 days ahead). In a small saucepan, combine the 10 g / 1 tbsp yellow mustard seeds, the 10 g / 1 tbsp brown mustard seeds, the 120 g / ½ cup red wine vinegar, and the 60 g / ¼ cup water. Let the seeds soak 10 minutes off heat while you mince the 50 g / 1 large shallot; the head start lets the seed coats begin to hydrate. Add the shallot, the 150 g / ⅔ cup packed light brown sugar, and the 2 g / ¾ tsp kosher salt, and bring to a simmer over medium heat, stirring with a wooden spoon until the sugar dissolves.'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Add the 340 g / 12 oz cranberries and the orange zest (about 3 g / 1 tbsp). Simmer over medium heat, stirring every minute, until roughly half the berries have burst and the liquid thickens to a loose syrup that coats the spoon, 8 to 10 minutes. Leave half the berries whole; the mix of intact fruit and jammy pulp is the texture of a mostarda, not a smooth sauce. Cranberries are rich in pectin, and the syrup will set noticeably as it cools, so stop while it still looks a little loose.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Bloom the mustard powder cold. While the berries cook, whisk the 6 g / 2½ tsp dry mustard powder into the orange juice (about 60 g / ¼ cup) in a small bowl until smooth, and let it stand 10 minutes. Dry mustard has no heat until water wakes up its enzyme (myrosinase), which converts the seed glucosinolates into the volatile isothiocyanate that gives mustard its bite. Heat above about 60 °C kills that enzyme, so the powder goes in cold and is added to the pot off the heat. Take the saucepan off the burner and let the mostarda cool until it reads below 50 °C / 120 °F, 15 to 20 minutes (spread it on a plate to speed this up), then stir in the bloomed mustard. Stirred into hotter fruit, the mustard loses most of its heat. Taste: it should be sweet first, then sharply sour, then a nasal heat. If it tastes only sweet, add red wine vinegar 5 g / 1 tsp at a time. Cool, transfer to a glass jar, press a round of parchment onto the surface, lid it, and refrigerate at least 12 hours (up to 7 days).'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Make the crust (up to 1 day ahead). In a bowl, rub the 2 g / 2 tsp lemon zest and the 12 g / 3 grated garlic cloves into the 60 g / 1 cup breadcrumbs with your fingertips, so the zest oils and garlic juice are absorbed by the crumb rather than clumping. Add the 30 g / ½ cup grated parmigiano, the 15 g / ½ cup chopped parsley, the 3 g / 1 tbsp minced rosemary, the 2 g / 2 tsp thyme leaves, and the 2 g / 1 tsp black pepper. Drizzle in the 30 g / 2 tbsp olive oil and toss until the crumb looks like damp sand and holds a loose clump when squeezed. Do not salt it: the brined lamb and the parmigiano carry enough.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Temper and preheat. Take the racks out of the fridge 45 minutes before searing and the mostarda out at the same time. Heat the oven to 220 °C / 425 °F with a rack in the upper-middle position. Set a clean wire rack over a foil-lined sheet pan for roasting.'
    time_min: 45
  - text: 'Sear the fat cap. Heat a 30 cm / 12-inch cast-iron skillet over medium-high heat for 4 minutes, add the 15 g / 1 tbsp neutral oil, and when it shimmers lay one rack in fat-side down, bones pointing up and out of the pan. Sear 3 to 4 minutes without moving it, until the fat is deep golden and has rendered a few tablespoons into the pan. Tip the rack up to sear the two ends for 30 seconds each. Do not sear the bone side or the underside of the eye; that surface is what touches the hot sheet pan and only needs the oven. Repeat with the second rack, pouring off excess fat between racks if more than 2 tbsp collects. Set both racks fat-side up on the wire rack and let them cool 5 minutes so the mustard does not slide off a sizzling surface.'
    technique: 'maillard-sear'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Coat. Brush the 45 g / 3 tbsp dijon mustard over the seared fat cap and the exposed ends of both racks in an even, thin layer. Pack the herb crust onto the mustard with a cupped palm, pressing firmly so it adheres in a layer about 5 mm thick; use all of it across the two racks. Wrap the exposed bone tips in small pieces of foil if you want them to stay pale for presentation.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Roast. Set the racks crust-side up on the wire rack, bones interlocked or pointing in the same direction, and roast at 220 °C / 425 °F until an instant-read thermometer in the center of the thickest part of the eye reads 52 °C / 126 °F, 15 to 20 minutes (start checking at 12). The crust should be deep golden; if it is browning faster than the meat cooks, lay a loose sheet of foil over the top for the last few minutes. If the oven also holds a second roast, add 2 to 3 minutes and rely on the thermometer.'
    time_min: 18
  - text: 'Rest. Transfer the racks to a cutting board, crust-side up, and rest uncovered 10 minutes. The interior will climb 3 to 5 °C to 55 to 57 °C, medium-rare, and the juices thicken enough that the cut faces stay pink instead of flooding the board. Do not tent tightly; trapped steam softens the crust.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Carve and finish. Stand each rack on its side and slice between the bones with a long sharp knife, cutting straight down along the bone so each chop keeps an even portion of eye. Carve into double chops (2 ribs) for 6 guests, or single chops when the lamb shares the table with a second main. Sprinkle the cut faces with the 2 g / ½ tsp flaky salt. Serve with the room-temperature cranberry mostarda in a bowl alongside, about 1 tbsp per chop: its vinegar and mustard heat are the finishing acid that resets the palate between bites of lamb fat.'
    time_min: 5

notes: |
  Why a dry-brine on a 20-minute roast. Rack of lamb cooks so fast that surface salt has no time to move inward during cooking. A night in the fridge seasons the loin eye through, and the dried fat cap browns in the 3-minute sear instead of releasing water into the pan. Salted 30 minutes before cooking, the rack tastes salted on the outside and flat in the middle.

  Why sear before the crust instead of after. The fat cap needs direct contact with hot iron to render and brown; a crust in the way insulates it, and the herbs and garlic in the crumb burn long before the fat renders. Searing first, cooling 5 minutes, then coating means the oven only has to do two jobs: bring the eye to temperature and toast the crumb. At 220 °C those two finish in about the same 15 to 20 minutes, which is the reason for that oven temperature.

  The doneness window is narrow. The loin eye of a rack is small (about 5 cm across), so it moves from 50 °C to 60 °C in roughly 4 minutes at 220 °C. Pull at 52 °C. The 10-minute rest carries it to 55 to 57 °C. Past 60 °C the meat turns gray and the lamb flavor, which lives in the fat and in branched-chain fatty acids, starts to read as muttony rather than sweet.

  Why the mostarda works here. Lamb fat is saturated and coats the palate; the classic answers are mint sauce (acid plus sugar plus aromatic) and mustard. Mostarda combines both and adds the cranberry, which carries the Thanksgiving signal. The heat comes from allyl isothiocyanate formed when the dry mustard meets cold liquid. Because that compound is volatile, the jar loses some bite over a week; a 7-day-old batch can take an extra 2 g / 1 tsp of dry mustard bloomed in a spoonful of water and stirred in.

  Cost and buying. Two frenched racks cost roughly 60 to 90 USD in the US; New Zealand and Australian racks at warehouse clubs sit at the low end and are smaller and milder. American racks are larger and more strongly flavored; if the racks are over 1 kg each, add 3 to 5 minutes to the roast. The boneless leg modification is the budget route and feeds the same table for about half the price.

  Serving for a crowd with a second main. With a second main on the table, most guests take 2 or 3 single chops. Carve singles, arrange them overlapping on a warm platter with the bones pointing the same way, and put the mostarda bowl in the middle.
