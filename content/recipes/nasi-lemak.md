before_you_start:
  - 'Soak the dried chilies first. They need 20 minutes in boiling water before they can be blended, so start them before any other prep.'
  - 'The sambal is the long track (about 40 minutes, most of it slow frying with frequent stirring). Start the rice as soon as the sambal paste goes into the oil; both finish around the same time, and the eggs boil in parallel.'
  - 'Ventilate before toasting the belacan. Toasted shrimp paste smells strong for an hour; open a window and run the hood.'
  - 'The sambal can be made 1 to 3 days ahead and tastes better for it. Fry the peanuts and anchovies the same day you serve them, or store them airtight once fully cool; they lose crunch in the fridge.'

steps:
  - text: 'Soak and toast. Put the 30 g dried chilies in a heatproof bowl, cover with boiling water, and weigh them down with a small plate; soak 20 minutes until fully soft. Meanwhile, wrap the 10 g belacan in a small square of foil, flatten it, and toast it in a dry skillet over medium heat 2 minutes per side, until it is dry, crumbly, and smells roasted rather than raw and fishy. Raw belacan tastes harsh in a sambal; toasting rounds it out.'
    time_min: 20
  - text: 'Prep the anchovies and tamarind. Rinse the 100 g split dried anchovies in a sieve under cold water for 10 seconds to remove grit and excess salt, then spread on a towel and pat completely dry; wet anchovies spatter and never crisp. Soak the 25 g tamarind pulp in the 100 ml / scant ½ cup warm water for 10 minutes, squeeze and mash it with your fingers, and strain, pressing the pulp. Keep the liquid and discard the seeds and fiber.'
    technique: 'tamarind-souring'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Fry the peanuts. Heat the 300 g / 1⅓ cups oil in a wok to 150 °C / 300 °F. Add the 120 g raw peanuts and fry, stirring, 5 to 6 minutes, until the nuts under the skins are light golden. Lift them out with a spider onto a tray lined with paper towels and toss with the 2 g / ⅓ tsp fine sea salt. Pull them a shade lighter than you want; they keep darkening for a minute out of the oil, and peanuts that go brown taste bitter.'
    technique: 'deep-fry'
    time_min: 7
  - text: 'Fry the anchovies. Raise the oil to 165 °C / 330 °F. Fry the 100 g anchovies in two batches, stirring, about 2 to 3 minutes each, until golden and crisp; they go from gold to burnt in under 30 seconds, so pull them at the first even golden color. Drain on paper towels. Set aside 40 g for the sambal and keep 60 g for the plate. Let the oil cool slightly, then measure out 120 g / ½ cup for the sambal; the anchovy oil adds savory depth.'
    technique: 'deep-fry'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Blend the sambal paste. Drain the soaked chilies. Blend them with the 150 g shallots, 15 g / 4 cloves garlic, the toasted 10 g belacan, and the 60 ml / ¼ cup water until very smooth, scraping down the sides. The paste should be a uniform bright red purée with no chili skin flakes visible.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Fry the paste until the oil splits. Heat the 120 g / ½ cup reserved oil in a wide skillet or clean wok over medium heat, add the paste (it will spit), and cook, stirring every minute or so with a wooden spoon and scraping the bottom, for 25 to 30 minutes. Lower the heat if it sticks. The paste first bubbles and spatters as the water cooks off, then darkens from bright red to deep brick red, thickens, and smells sweet instead of raw. It is ready when the oil separates and pools around the paste (pecah minyak in Malay). Stopping early leaves the sambal tasting raw and sharp of shallot; this slow fry is where its depth comes from.'
    time_min: 30
  - text: 'Start the rice while the paste fries. Rinse the 400 g / 2 cups jasmine rice in several changes of water until the water runs nearly clear, and drain well. In a heavy 3-liter saucepan, combine the rice with the 250 g / 1 cup coconut milk, the 300 ml / 1¼ cups water, and the 5 g / ¾ tsp fine sea salt; stir so the salt dissolves. Add the 15 g knotted pandan leaves, the 10 g ginger coins, and the 30 g / 1 stalk bruised lemongrass, pushing them into the rice. Bring to a boil over medium-high heat, stir once from the bottom, cover with a tight lid, and turn the heat to its lowest setting for 15 minutes. Then take it off the heat and leave it covered 10 minutes. The coconut fat and sugar scorch easily, so keep the heat truly low and do not lift the lid during the cook.'
    time_min: 27
  - text: 'Boil the eggs in parallel. Bring a small pot of water to a boil, lower in the 4 eggs, and cook 9 minutes for a fully set but still moist yolk. Move them to a bowl of ice water for 5 minutes, then peel and halve.'
    technique: 'ice-bath-shock'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Finish the sambal. When the oil has split, add the tamarind liquid (from the 25 g pulp), the 30 g / 2½ tbsp palm sugar, and the 3 g / ½ tsp fine sea salt, and stir until the sugar dissolves. Add the 150 g sliced red onion and the 40 g reserved fried anchovies and simmer gently, stirring, 5 to 8 minutes, until the onion is soft but still has a little bite and the sambal is thick and glossy with oil on its surface. Taste: it should be sweet and spicy up front, then sour and savory. Add salt in pinches if it tastes flat; more palm sugar if the chili is harsh. The anchovies in the sambal soften as they absorb the sauce, which is intended.'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Fluff the rice. Remove the 15 g pandan knot, the 10 g ginger, and the 30 g lemongrass. Fluff the rice with a rice paddle or fork, folding up from the bottom so the coconut fat that settled there is redistributed.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Plate. Mound about 200 g / 1 heaped cup rice in the center of each plate. Arrange around it a generous 2 tbsp (about 60 g) of sambal, 15 g / 2 tbsp fried anchovies, 15 g / 2 tbsp fried peanuts, a fan of 50 g cucumber slices, and 2 halves of 1 egg. Eat by mixing a little of everything into each bite; the cucumber and egg cool the sambal, the anchovies and peanuts add crunch and salt.'
    time_min: 5

notes: |
  The sambal decides the dish. Sambal tumis is cooked, not raw: the blended chili and shallot paste has to fry slowly until its water is gone and the oil separates out. During that fry the shallot sugars caramelize and the chili and belacan aromas move into the oil. Underfried sambal is the most common failure; it tastes of raw shallot and bitter chili. Keep the heat at medium or below and stir often; scorched spots taste burnt throughout.

  Balance. Malaysian nasi lemak sambal is sweeter than most outsiders expect. Palm sugar balances the chili, tamarind keeps it from tasting cloying, and the belacan and anchovies give glutamate and salt. Tamarind is the acid here: the dish does not get a squeeze of lime, and the cucumber supplies the freshness.

  Rice. Rinsing removes surface starch so the grains stay separate despite the fat in the coconut milk. Salt goes in the cooking liquid so every grain is seasoned. Pandan carries most of the aroma; ginger and lemongrass are common in Malay home kitchens but not universal.

  Cleaning ikan bilis. Pull off the heads and the small dark gut along the belly, then split the fish in half. The gut tastes bitter after frying. Pre-split anchovies save 15 minutes.

  Storage and food safety. Leftover sambal keeps a week in a sealed glass jar in the fridge, with its oil on top, and freezes well. Nasi lemak is traditionally sold at room temperature, but cooked rice should not sit out more than 2 hours (Bacillus cereus spores survive cooking); refrigerate leftovers promptly and reheat until steaming.
