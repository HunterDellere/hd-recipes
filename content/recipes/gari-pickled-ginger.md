steps:
  - text: 'Slice the 250 g ginger as thin as you can, along the grain (lengthwise), on a mandoline or with a sharp knife. Aim for slices you can nearly see through, about 1 mm. Slicing along the grain keeps the fibers running the length of each slice, so the pickle bends instead of shredding.'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Salt draw. Toss the sliced ginger with the 5 g fine sea salt in a bowl and leave 30 minutes. Osmosis pulls water out of the cells so the slices take up more brine later and stay crisp.'
    time_min: 30
  - text: 'Make the brine while the ginger salts. Combine the 150 ml rice vinegar, 50 g sugar, and 2 g fine sea salt in a small saucepan and heat over medium just until the sugar dissolves and the brine is steaming, about 2 minutes. Do not boil it hard; you want to keep the bright top notes of the vinegar.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Blanch. Bring a small pot of water to a boil. Drop in the salted 250 g ginger for 15 seconds, then drain in a sieve. The quick blanch softens the raw gingerol burn and rinses off the excess surface salt, while staying short enough that the slices stay crisp.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Squeeze and pack. Spread the hot ginger on a clean towel for a minute to steam off, then squeeze it firmly in a fist, a handful at a time, to wring out water. Pack the warm 250 g ginger into a clean 500 ml jar and pour the hot brine over it. Packing warm ginger into warm brine pulls the seasoning in fastest as everything cools.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Cure. Cool to room temperature with the lid off, then cap and refrigerate. The pickle is usable in 2 hours and at its best after 24 hours. The 250 g young ginger will turn pale pink within the first day. Keeps 2 months refrigerated; always use a clean, dry utensil to take some out.'
    time_min: 5

notes: |
  What gari is for. At the sushi counter, gari is eaten between pieces, not on them. The sweet acid and the gingerol bite cut through fish oil and reset the palate before the next flavor. That job is why it should taste clean and sharp, with sugar only as a buffer for the vinegar.

  Why salt, then blanch. Raw ginger in brine stays fiery for weeks. The 2% salt draw pulls water out of the slices so they absorb brine, and the 15-second blanch softens the heat and rinses off excess salt. Gingerol converts slowly to milder zingerone with heat, so a longer blanch gives you milder gari, and a shorter one keeps more bite. Adjust to taste the second time you make it.

  Why the pink. Young ginger shoots carry anthocyanin pigments, which shift toward red under acidic conditions (the same chemistry behind red cabbage turning pink in vinegar). Mature ginger has lost most of these, which is why gari from supermarket ginger stays beige.

  Shelf life and spoilage. The brine is acidic enough to keep the pickle sound for about 2 months refrigerated. Discard it if the brine turns cloudy or ropy, smells yeasty or alcoholic, or grows any surface film; those are signs that a dirty utensil introduced yeast.
