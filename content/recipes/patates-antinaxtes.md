steps:
  - text: 'Crack the potatoes. Strike each of the 1000 g baby potatoes once with a meat mallet or the bottom of a heavy pan, hard enough to split the skin and open fissures in the flesh but not so hard that it falls apart. The cracked surfaces are where the browning and the wine will go.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Brown hard. Heat the 80 g / ⅓ cup olive oil in a 30 cm / 12 in skillet over medium-high until it shimmers. Add the potatoes in one layer, season with the 9 g / 1 tbsp kosher salt, and fry 10 to 12 minutes, turning occasionally, until deeply golden on most sides. They will not be cooked through; this is only for crust.'
    technique: 'shallow-frying'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'Bloom the coriander. Lower the heat to medium, scatter the 8 g / 1 tbsp cracked coriander seeds over the potatoes, and shake the pan for 30 seconds until fragrant. Coriander seed is rich in linalool, which reads as warm citrus once toasted in fat.'
    technique: 'blooming-aromatics'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Braise in wine. Pour in the 200 ml / ¾ cup red wine and 60 ml / ¼ cup water (it will hiss and spit), shake the pan, cover tightly, and simmer over medium-low 12 to 15 minutes until a knife slides into the largest potato without resistance. Shake the pan once or twice.'
    technique: 'braising'

notes: |
  Why crack instead of cut. A cut potato has a smooth face that browns evenly but takes up liquid only at the surface. A cracked potato has a fissured, irregular interior that gives more surface for browning and channels for the wine to soak in, and it holds together better than a halved potato through the shaking.

  The order matters. Browning first, braising second. If you add wine to raw potatoes, they steam and never brown; the Maillard crust only forms in oil at temperatures above 140 C. Once the crust is set it survives the braise and then re-crisps a little as the glaze reduces.

  Wine as the acid. The reduced red wine carries both tartaric acid and fruit sugars, which caramelize into the glaze. Cook off the raw alcohol fully in the reduction; under-reduced, the dish tastes boozy.
