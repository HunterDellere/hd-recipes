source:
  name: 'Milanese (Lombard) tradition, in bianco'
  note: 'The original Milanese osso buco is cooked without tomato; Milan was among the last Italian kitchens to adopt it, and the tomato-braised version (often with carrot-heavy soffritto and red wine) is a later variant that became the default outside Lombardy. The in bianco braise is lighter in color and lets the marrow, wine and gremolata carry the dish, and it is the version that belongs next to saffron risotto.'

before_you_start:
  - 'Salt the shanks ahead if you can: 12 to 24 hours uncovered in the refrigerator gives seasoning through the meat and a drier surface that browns faster. One hour ahead still helps; salting right before searing works but seasons only the surface.'
  - 'Get the stock hot in a small saucepan before you start searing, so it does not stall the braise when it goes in.'
  - 'If serving with risotto alla milanese, start the risotto when the shanks come out of the oven. The shanks hold happily, covered, for 30 minutes while the sauce reduces and the rice cooks; risotto does not hold at all.'

steps:
  - text: 'Tie and salt the shanks. Wrap a loop of kitchen twine snugly around the outside edge of each shank and knot it; the band keeps the meat on the bone once the collagen softens, so each piece comes out of the pot whole. With a small knife, nick the thin silver membrane around the edge in two or three places so it does not curl the shank as it tightens in the heat. Season the 1500 g shanks on all sides with the 15 g / 5 tsp kosher salt. Ideally refrigerate uncovered on a rack for 12 to 24 hours; otherwise rest at room temperature for 1 hour.'
    technique: 'dry-brining'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Heat the oven to 160 °C / 325 °F with a rack in the lower third. Pat the shanks dry, season with the 2 g / 1 tsp black pepper, then dredge in the 30 g / ¼ cup flour and tap off every bit of excess. A thin flour film browns quickly and later thickens the sauce slightly; a thick coat turns gummy.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Sear. Set a wide Dutch oven (about 28 cm / 11 in, large enough to hold the 4 shanks in one layer) over medium-high heat. Add the 15 g / 1 tbsp neutral oil and the 30 g / 2 tbsp searing butter. When the butter foam subsides and the solids turn hazelnut-brown, lay in the shanks and brown without moving them, about 4 minutes per side, until deep golden. Brown in two batches if they crowd; crowded meat steams. Move the shanks to a plate. If the fat in the pot has scorched black, pour it off and wipe out only the fat, leaving the brown fond.'
    technique: 'maillard-sear'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'Soffritto. Lower the heat to medium-low and melt the 20 g / 1½ tbsp soffritto butter in the same pot. Add the 150 g / 1 medium onion, 60 g / 1 small carrot and 60 g / 1 rib celery, all finely diced, with the 1 g / pinch kosher salt. The salt pulls water from the vegetables so they sweat rather than fry. Cook 8 minutes, stirring with a wooden spoon, until soft and translucent with no more than pale gold edges; the moisture they release loosens the fond.'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Deglaze. Raise the heat to medium-high, pour in the 180 ml / ¾ cup white wine and scrape the bottom with the wooden spoon until the brown fond dissolves. Boil until the wine has reduced by about half and no longer smells sharply of alcohol, about 3 minutes.'
    technique: 'deglazing'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Braise. Return the shanks in a single layer, marrow side up, with any juices from the plate. Add the 1 bay leaf and pour in the 500 ml / 2 cups hot chicken stock; it should come about halfway up the meat, not cover it, so the tops braise in steam and the sauce stays concentrated. Bring to a bare simmer, cover with the lid and move to the oven. Braise 1 hour, turn each shank, spoon sauce over the tops and braise about 1 hour more. The shanks are done when a fork slides into the thickest part with no resistance and the meat starts to pull away from the bone; the marrow should be soft and wobbling. If the liquid drops below a third of the way up the shanks, add a splash of hot water.'
    time_min: 125
  - text: 'Make the gremolata while the shanks finish. On a board, combine the 4 g / 1 tbsp lemon zest, 10 g / ¼ cup chopped parsley and 4 g / 1 clove grated garlic, and chop them together for a few seconds to bruise the parsley and distribute the garlic. Make it no more than 20 minutes ahead; the lemon oil and garlic fade quickly once cut.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Reduce and season the sauce. Lift the shanks onto a warm platter, snip and remove the twine, and tent loosely with foil. Discard the 1 bay leaf. Set the pot over medium heat and simmer the sauce until it lightly coats a spoon, about 5 minutes; the dissolved flour and gelatin from the shanks give it body without any extra thickener. Leave the vegetables in for a rustic sauce. Taste: it should be savory and rounded with a clean wine edge. Add kosher salt in small pinches only if needed, then stir in the 5 g / 1 tsp lemon juice to lift the richness.'
    time_min: 6
  - text: 'Finish. Return the shanks to the pot, spoon the sauce over them, scatter the gremolata (all of it, made from the 4 g lemon zest, 10 g parsley and 4 g garlic) across the tops, cover and leave off the heat for 1 minute. The heat releases the lemon and garlic aromas without cooking them flat. Serve each shank with plenty of sauce, next to risotto alla milanese, with a small spoon for the marrow.'
    time_min: 2

notes: |
  Why in bianco. Tomato pushes the dish toward sweet and acidic and covers the delicate flavor of veal. Without it, the sauce is built from browned flour and butter, a soft soffritto, reduced wine and the gelatin that the shanks release over two hours; the gremolata then supplies all the brightness. That balance is why this version sits so well next to saffron risotto, which is itself rich and mild.

  Umami and acid. There is no added glutamate ingredient here, and the tradition does not call for one: the depth comes from the browned meat, the reduced homemade stock and the marrow, which melts into the sauce as you eat. If the sauce tastes thin, the stock is the cause, not a missing ingredient. The acid comes in three layers: the reduced wine, a teaspoon of lemon juice in the finished sauce, and the raw lemon zest in the gremolata.

  The cut. Ask for hind shank cut 4 cm / 1½ in thick. Thinner pieces dry out before the collagen converts; thicker ones need much longer and cook unevenly. Veal shank is usually stocked at butcher counters and larger supermarkets, and can be ordered a few days ahead; frozen shanks work well if thawed overnight in the refrigerator.

  Doneness is about texture, not temperature. Shank is full of collagen, which converts to gelatin only after long time above about 70 °C / 160 °F. The meat passes well-done early in the braise and keeps improving for another hour as the connective tissue melts; judge by the fork test and the meat loosening from the bone.

  Gremolata timing. Lemon zest and raw garlic lose their sharp top notes within minutes of hitting a simmering sauce. Scattering the gremolata off the heat and covering for one minute warms it just enough to bloom the aromas.
