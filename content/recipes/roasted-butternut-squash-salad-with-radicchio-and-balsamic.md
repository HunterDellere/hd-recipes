steps:
  - text: 'Set two racks in the upper and lower thirds of the oven, slide a half-sheet pan onto each, and heat to 230 °C / 450 °F for at least 15 minutes. Meanwhile peel, seed, and cube the 1600 g / 2 medium butternut squash. Peel past the first pale layer until the flesh is solid orange; the greenish band under the skin stays fibrous no matter how long it roasts.'
    technique: 'high-heat-dry-roasting'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Make the vinaigrette base first so the shallot has time to mellow. In a jar, combine the 30 g / 1 medium minced shallot, the 50 g / 3 tbsp balsamic vinegar, the 15 g / 1 tbsp red wine vinegar, and the 3 g / ¾ tsp kosher salt. Let it sit at least 10 minutes while you prep. The acid and salt draw out and partially neutralize the sharp thiosulfinates, so the shallot reads sweet-allium instead of raw and hot, and the salt fully dissolves before any oil arrives to block it.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'In a large bowl, toss the cubed squash with the 40 g / 3 tbsp olive oil, the 10 g / 2½ tsp kosher salt, the 2 g / ½ tsp black pepper, and the 1 g / ½ tsp red pepper flakes until every face is glossed. Salting now, before heat, pulls a thin film of moisture to the surface that dissolves the salt into the flesh and evaporates within the first minutes of roasting, so the cubes season through rather than just on the crust.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Pull the hot pans and divide the squash between them in a single layer, cut faces down, with a finger-width gap between cubes. It should sizzle on contact. Roast 15 minutes undisturbed, then flip with a thin metal spatula and swap the pans top to bottom. Scatter the 6 g / about 20 sage leaves over the squash, pressing them flat into the oil on the pan.'
    technique: 'high-heat-dry-roasting'
    time_min: 16
  - text: 'Roast another 10 to 14 minutes, until the cubes are fork-tender with deep mahogany edges on at least two faces and the 6 g / 20 sage leaves are crisp and dark green (pull any leaves early if they start to brown). Do not stop at pale orange and soft: the browned surface is where the savory pyrazines and the bitter edge of caramelized sugar live, and that bitterness is half of what keeps this salad from tasting sweet.'
    technique: 'high-heat-dry-roasting'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'While the squash finishes, toast the 90 g / ¾ cup pecans in a dry skillet over medium-low, shaking often, 5 to 7 minutes until they smell buttery and the cut faces darken one shade. Off heat, toss with the 5 g / 1 tsp olive oil and the 1 g pinch of flaky salt. The oil gives the salt something to stick to; salted rather than candied pecans are a deliberate choice here.'
    technique: 'dry-toasting'
    time_min: 7
  - text: 'Finish the vinaigrette. Add the 10 g / 2 tsp dijon mustard to the macerated shallot and stir. Add the 120 g / ½ cup + 1 tbsp olive oil, cap the jar, and shake hard for 20 seconds until thick and opaque; the mustard mucilage holds the emulsion. Taste it on a radicchio leaf, not a spoon. It should land sharp first, then fruity, then mellow. If it reads sweet or syrupy (common with flavored or thick aged balsamic), add red wine vinegar 5 g / 1 tsp at a time until it bites; if it reads harsh, a few more drops of olive oil.'
    technique: 'vinaigrette-emulsion'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'As soon as the squash comes out, crumble most of the crisp sage over it, then spoon 45 g / 3 tbsp of the vinaigrette over the hot squash on the pans and toss gently. Hot, porous squash absorbs the dressing into the outer layer the way warm potatoes do in a potato salad, so the acid seasons the flesh rather than sliding off the surface. Let it cool 10 minutes; hot squash wilts arugula on contact.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'In your widest bowl, toss the 250 g / 1 large head torn radicchio and the 120 g / 5 cups arugula with about 60 g / 4 tbsp of the remaining vinaigrette until lightly but completely coated. Leaves should glisten, not pool. Radicchio takes dressing well and stays crisp for an hour; arugula goes limp within 20 minutes of dressing, so do this right before it goes to the table.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Spread the dressed leaves on a platter. Lay the warm squash over them (do not toss it through, the cubes break and smear), then scatter the salted pecans, the 50 g / ½ cup parmigiano curls, and the reserved sage. Finish with the 1 g pinch of flaky salt and a last 15 g / 1 tbsp drizzle of vinaigrette over the squash. Put the leftover vinaigrette in a small pitcher on the table.'
    time_min: 3

notes: |
  Where the sweetness is, and how it stays in check. Roasted butternut is already about 4 to 5 percent sugar, and high heat concentrates and caramelizes it. That is the sweet side of the dish, and it is enough. There is no maple, honey, dried fruit, or candied nut anywhere. Every other element pushes the other way: radicchio and the browned squash edges for bitter, red wine vinegar for acid, parmigiano and the salted pecans for salt and glutamate, and the chili flakes for a faint warmth on the finish. The parmigiano is the umami anchor; without it the plate reads as sweet and sour with nothing savory underneath.

  Using your flavored balsamics. Flavored balsamics range widely in sugar, so treat the 15 g of red wine vinegar as the adjustment knob, not a fixed amount. Good fits for this salad: fig, maple, apple or cider, pear, pomegranate, blood orange, and plain aged dark balsamic. They all echo the squash and work in autumn. Use with care: thick "condimento" style or very sweet dessert flavors (chocolate, raspberry, vanilla). If you use one, double the red wine vinegar to 30 g / 2 tbsp and taste on a leaf. Avoid savory-herb flavors that clash with sage (lemon-thyme is fine, garlic is redundant with the shallot). White balsamic works too and keeps the platter brighter in color, but it is usually sweeter than dark, so add the extra red wine vinegar.

  Why dress the squash hot. Starch-rich vegetables absorb liquid best while hot, because steam is still escaping from the surface and pulling liquid back in as it cools. Three tablespoons of vinaigrette on hot squash seasons it all the way through; the same amount on cold squash beads up and runs to the bottom of the bowl.

  Thanksgiving logistics. Every component except the dressed leaves holds well: roast the squash the day before, make the vinaigrette up to a week ahead (shake it again before using, the emulsion loosens in the fridge), and toast the nuts and crisp the sage that morning. Rewarm the squash for 8 minutes in any oven between 175 and 230 °C while the turkey rests. Leftover undressed squash and dressing make a good next-day lunch over the same leaves.
