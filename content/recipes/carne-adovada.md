steps:
  - text: 'Toast the chiles lightly. Heat a dry cast-iron skillet over medium-low heat. Press the 110 g New Mexico chiles flat against the pan a few at a time for 5 to 10 seconds per side, just until they soften, smell fruity, and darken very slightly. New Mexico pods are thin-skinned and scorch much faster than ancho; a single blackened pod makes the whole sauce bitter, so pull them early.'
    technique: 'dry-toasting'
    time_min: 6
  - text: 'Soak. Put the chiles in a heatproof bowl, pour over the 1000 ml boiling water, and weigh them down with a small plate. Soak 20 minutes until soft. Drain and discard the soaking water.'
    time_min: 20
  - text: 'Blend. Put the chiles in the blender with the 500 ml / 2 cups chicken stock, 30 g / 8 cloves garlic, 3 g / 1 tbsp Mexican oregano, 1 g / ½ tsp ground cumin, 30 ml / 2 tbsp apple cider vinegar, 15 g / 2 tsp honey, and 6 g / 1½ tsp kosher salt for the sauce. Blend on high for 2 full minutes. Press it through a medium-mesh sieve with a wooden spoon; New Mexico chile skins are tough and stay gritty otherwise.'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Fry the sauce. Heat the 30 g / 2 tbsp lard in a large Dutch oven over medium heat until it shimmers. Pour in the sauce (it will spatter) and cook, stirring constantly, 8 to 10 minutes, until it darkens from bright red to brick red and thickens enough to coat a spoon heavily. Frying cooks off the raw, grassy edge of the chile and develops its sweetness. Take the pot off the heat and let the sauce cool completely, about 30 minutes.'
    technique: 'blooming-aromatics'
    time_min: 40
  - text: 'Marinate. Toss the 1600 g cubed pork with the 12 g kosher salt for the pork, then stir it into the cooled sauce in the Dutch oven until every piece is coated. Cover and refrigerate overnight, or at least 6 hours. The salt and vinegar season the pork through and the chile stains the outside of every cube.'
    time_min: 480
  - text: 'Bake covered. Heat the oven to 150 °C / 300 °F. Take the pot from the fridge 30 minutes ahead. Stir in 120 ml / ½ cup water, which replaces the moisture the pork would otherwise pull from the sauce early on. Cover tightly and bake 2½ to 3 hours, stirring once halfway, until a cube falls apart under light pressure from a spoon. The pork is not seared first: the chile coating would burn before the meat browned, and the long bake in a thick sauce gives the flavor instead.'
    time_min: 170
  - text: 'Reduce uncovered. Uncover, stir, and bake 20 to 30 minutes more, until the sauce has reduced to a thick gravy that clings to the pork and the exposed edges have darkened. Taste a piece of pork with sauce and add up to 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt if it tastes flat.'
    time_min: 25
  - text: 'Serve with the 12 warm flour tortillas, some of the 100 g rinsed diced white onion, and the 2 limes, in wedges, for squeezing. Break the pork into chunks with a spoon as you serve; it should shred under the slightest pressure.'
    time_min: 5

notes: |
  One chile, done well. Texas chili blends several dried chiles for depth; New Mexico red sauce uses only one, and its flavor is the point: earthy, sweet, slightly tart, with a clean heat. That is why cumin is kept to half a teaspoon and there is no tomato, both of which would cover it.

  Heat level. New Mexico pods are sold by heat. All mild gives a sauce most people find gentle; a third to a half hot pods gives a solid medium; all hot is hot. Pods from the same bag vary, so taste the blended sauce before marinating.

  Vinegar and honey. Adovada comes from adobar, to marinate or cure, and vinegar is part of the tradition. It seasons the pork during the overnight marinade and balances the bitterness of the chile skins; honey rounds off the edge. Neither should be identifiable in the finished dish.

  Glutamate and acid. Dried chiles are rich in free glutamate, and chicken stock and the long-cooked pork add more. Vinegar is cooked in; lime at the table is the fresh acid.

  Better the next day. Like most braises it improves overnight in the fridge. Leftovers make excellent burritos, enchiladas, and breakfast plates with fried eggs.
