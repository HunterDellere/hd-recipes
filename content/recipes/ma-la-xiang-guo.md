before_you_start:
  - 'Steam 300 g / 1½ cups jasmine rice first. The dish is intensely seasoned and needs the neutral rice; it also cannot wait once the final toss starts.'
  - 'Tofu skin rolls (fresh or frozen yuba rolls, often sold as strips): if frozen, thaw overnight in the refrigerator. They need no soaking. Only dried fuzhu sticks need the 3-hour soak, and that swap is described under substitutions.'
  - 'The frying happens in one pool of oil in one sequence, ordered by temperature and by how much each item dirties the oil: shrimp, then taro, then tofu skin rolls, then battered flounder. Have a rack over a sheet pan and an instant-read thermometer beside the wok before the oil goes in.'
  - 'Wok size decides the final toss. A 30 to 33 cm wok cannot toss 1.7 kg of ingredients; the bottom steams while the top stays cold. Plan on two rounds: make the full base, lift half of it into a bowl, toss half of every component, plate, return the reserved base, and toss the second half. A 36 cm or larger wok on a strong burner can do it in one.'
  - 'Grind the green Sichuan peppercorn in the last 10 minutes, not earlier. Hydroxy-alpha-sanshool, the numbing compound, is volatile and fades within an hour of grinding.'
  - 'Wear gloves to peel the taro. Raw taro is full of calcium oxalate needles that itch and burn bare skin; cooking destroys them.'

steps:
  - text: 'Peel and slice the 300 g lotus root 3 mm thick, dropping the slices straight into 1 L cold water with the 15 g / 1 tbsp distilled white vinegar; the acid slows the polyphenol oxidase that browns cut lotus. Bring 1.5 L water with the 10 g / 2 tsp kosher salt to a rolling boil, drain the lotus, blanch 90 seconds, then shock in ice water. Drain and spread on a towel to dry; wet slices spit in the wok and refuse to take the base.'
    technique: 'ice-bath-shock'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Wearing gloves, peel the 340 g taro and cut it into 12 mm batons. Steam 12 minutes until a skewer slides in with slight resistance at the center. Spread on a plate to cool uncovered so the surface dries; a dry surface is what blisters in the oil.'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'While the taro steams, toss the 340 g shrimp with the 1.5 g / ¼ tsp baking soda and 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt and rest 15 minutes. The alkaline surface raises the pH of the outer muscle so the myofibrils do not contract as tightly under heat, which is the snap in restaurant shrimp. Rinse under cold water, then dry completely between towels.'
    time_min: 17
  - text: 'Cut the 340 g flounder into 4 cm pieces, season with the 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt, and rest 10 minutes, then blot. Whisk the 30 g egg white, 32 g / ¼ cup potato starch, 5 g / 1 tsp Shaoxing wine, and 1 g / ¼ tsp fine sea salt until the batter falls from the whisk in a continuous sheet. If it drips like water, add potato starch a teaspoon at a time; if it clumps, a teaspoon of cold water. Keep it cold and re-whisk before each batch, since the starch settles.'
    technique: 'velveting'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'Heat the empty, dry wok over the highest flame until a drop of water skates and vanishes. Add the 200 g shimeji in a single layer and leave them untouched 2 minutes, then toss and cook 2 minutes more until the edges are deeply browned and they smell faintly of shellfish. Only now add the 1.5 g / ½ tsp kosher salt; salting earlier pulls water out and the mushrooms steam instead of sear. Lift to a bowl.'
    technique: 'maillard-sear'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Pour the 320 g peanut oil into the wok and bring it to 150°C / 300°F. Slide in half of the shrimp (170 g) and stir gently 40 seconds until they turn opaque and curl to a loose C; an instant-read thermometer in the thickest one reads 49°C / 120°F. Lift to a rack over a sheet pan and repeat with the second half of the 340 g shrimp. They are underdone on purpose; the final toss finishes them.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Raise the oil to 170°C / 340°F. Fry the 340 g steamed taro in two batches, about 3 minutes each, until the edges blister and go golden; lift to the rack. Then fry the 250 g tofu skin rolls in two batches, 60 to 90 seconds each, until the surface pulls taut and blisters at the edges; lift. Let the oil recover to temperature between every batch, and skim out anything that floats.'
    technique: 'deep-fry'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Drop the oil to 165°C / 330°F. Drag each piece of the 340 g salted flounder through the batter, let the excess sheet off, and lay it in away from you, 5 or 6 pieces per batch in the 320 g oil. Fry 90 seconds on the first face and 60 seconds on the second, until the shell is pale gold and crisp and the center reads 54°C / 130°F. Drain on the rack. Skim the batter debris between batches, since it burns and turns the oil bitter.'
    technique: 'batter-frying'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Pour the 320 g frying oil through a fine sieve into a heatproof container. Measure 40 g / 3 tbsp of it back into the wok and set the rest aside for another day. Wipe the rim of the wok.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Add the 40 g / 3 tbsp beef tallow to the 40 g reserved oil and heat over medium until it reaches 120°C / 250°F, a bare shimmer. Add the 2 g star anise, 3 g cassia bark, 2 bay leaves, 3 g / 1 tsp fennel seed, and the black cardamom husk. Swirl 30 seconds until the fat smells of the spices; their aromatics are lipid-soluble and only the fat can pull them out.'
    technique: 'blooming-aromatics'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Add the 40 g / 2½ tbsp doubanjiang and 12 g / 1 tbsp douchi.     time_min: 3
  - text: 'Add the 30 g ginger coins, 40 g smashed garlic, and the 40 g scallion whites. Stir 60 seconds until the garlic edges take a little color and the ginger is fragrant.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Add the 15 g dried erjingtiao chilies and 6 g / 1 tbsp red Sichuan peppercorns.     technique: 'dry-toasting'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Add the 8 g / 2 tsp crushed rock sugar, 30 g / 2 tbsp Shaoxing wine, 16 g / 1 tbsp light soy sauce, and 20 g / 1 tbsp oyster sauce. Bubble 60 seconds until glossy and thick enough to cling to a spoon. Taste a drop: it should be intensely salty, sweet, and savory on its own, because it is seasoning 1.7 kg of ingredients.     time_min: 1
  - text: 'Raise the heat to high.     time_min: 2

notes: |
  Why tallow. Chongqing hot pot is built on beef tallow, and xiang guo is hot pot with the broth removed. Tallow is solid at room temperature and clings to every surface it coats, so the base stays on the ingredients instead of pooling on the platter, and its rendered-beef flavor is the register that separates a dry pot from an ordinary spicy stir-fry. A 1:1 blend with the strained frying oil keeps it fluid in the wok while retaining the flavor.

  Why everything is cooked separately first. Taro, lotus root, shrimp, and battered fish have nothing in common thermally: taro needs 15 minutes of moist heat and then a blistering fry, shrimp are done in 40 seconds, fish crust needs oil at 165°C, and lotus root wants a 90-second blanch to lose its raw starch while staying crisp. Cooking them together in the base would mean choosing one of those and ruining the rest. The final toss lasts under two minutes and is only there to coat and reheat.

  Why the frying order. Shrimp go first at the lowest temperature because they need the cleanest oil and the gentlest heat. Taro and tofu skin rolls shed almost nothing and tolerate 170°C. Battered flounder goes last because it sheds starch that would burn under everything after it. Straining the oil at the end and reusing 40 g in the base carries a little of the shrimp and fish flavor into the fat.

  Why potato starch in the batter. Potato starch has larger granules and more amylose than cornstarch, gelatinizes at a lower temperature, and fries to a thinner, glassier shell that stays crisp longer once it meets the wet base. Cornstarch works, but the crust softens sooner in the toss.

  Why the chilies go in after the paste. Doubanjiang needs three minutes of frying to release its color and depth; dried chilies scorch in one. Frying the paste first and adding the chilies and red peppercorn for the last 30 to 45 seconds gets both to their best point, and the paste-loaded wok keeps the fat temperature down while they toast.

  Why two kinds of peppercorn. Red Sichuan peppercorn in the fat early gives a rounded, warm má that saturates the base. Green peppercorn ground at the end gives a sharper, citrus-edged tingle that fades within an hour of grinding, so it goes in during the final toss and half of it stays whole for texture.

  Why the vinegar goes down the wok wall off heat. Chinkiang vinegar has harsh low-boiling volatiles and a fruity, malty body. Hitting the hot metal flashes off the harsh fraction and leaves the body, and doing it off heat keeps the aromatics that survive from cooking away before the dish reaches the table.
