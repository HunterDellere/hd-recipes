notes: |
  How it works. Cultured buttermilk carries mesophilic lactic acid bacteria, which grow well at room temperature. In the cream they ferment lactose into lactic acid; as the pH drops toward about 4.6, the casein proteins lose their charge and link into a loose gel that thickens the cream. Time and temperature are the two dials: longer or warmer gives a thicker, more sour crema.

  Why not ultra-pasteurized cream. Ultra-high-temperature processing denatures the whey proteins and changes how the casein sets, so the cream cultures slowly and stays thin. Regular pasteurized cream sets properly.

  Crema versus sour cream. Commercial sour cream is cultured light cream at about 20% fat, often thickened with gums. Crema is cultured heavy cream at about 36% fat. The extra fat surrounds the proteins and keeps them from clumping when they meet heat or acid, so crema can be stirred into hot stew or drizzled over salsa without curdling.

  When to throw it out. A successful batch smells tangy and clean. Discard it if it turns pink, orange, or gray, grows fuzzy spots, or smells sharp, yeasty, or rotten; that means other organisms outcompeted the culture.
