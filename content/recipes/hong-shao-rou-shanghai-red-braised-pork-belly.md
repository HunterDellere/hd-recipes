steps:
  - text: 'Cut the 800 g pork belly into 3 cm cubes, each with skin, fat, and lean. Even sizes matter more than exact sizes: every cube needs to finish tender at the same moment.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Blanch. Put the pork in a pot, cover with cold tap water by 3 cm, and add the 10 g / 3 coins blanching ginger and the 15 g / 1 tbsp blanching Shaoxing wine. Bring to a boil over high heat and boil 3 minutes; gray scum (coagulated blood proteins) will rise. Drain, discard the ginger, rinse each cube under warm water to remove clinging scum, and dry well with a towel. Starting cold lets the proteins release slowly and float off instead of setting on the meat, which keeps the final sauce clean and glossy rather than murky.'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'Make the caramel. Wipe out the pot (or use a wok or a heavy 3 to 4 L pot with a lid). Over low heat, add the 15 g / 1 tbsp neutral oil and the 45 g crushed rock sugar. Stir with a wooden spoon as the sugar melts: it will look grainy, then turn to clear syrup, then to a light amber with large slow bubbles, about 4 to 6 minutes. Stop at the color of dark honey. Past mahogany it turns bitter, and there is no saving it.'
    technique: 'dry-caramel'
    time_min: 6
  - text: 'Glaze the pork. Add the dried pork cubes all at once (stand back; the caramel spits on contact with any moisture) and raise the heat to medium. Toss for 2 to 3 minutes until every cube is coated in a thin amber film and the fat edges begin to render and sizzle.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Build the braise. Add the 20 g braising ginger coins, the 40 g / 4 scallions, and the 2 g / 1 star anise pod if using, and toss 30 seconds until fragrant. Pour the 150 g / ⅔ cup Shaoxing wine around the edge of the pot so it hits hot metal and sizzles; let it bubble 1 minute to drive off the sharpest alcohol. Add the 35 g / 2 tbsp + 1 tsp light soy sauce and the 15 g / 1 tbsp dark soy sauce, then the 450 ml / 2 cups hot water, enough to just cover the pork. Hot water keeps the caramelized surface from seizing and the fat from turning waxy. The light soy is your first seasoning: the liquid should taste pleasantly salty, like a broth you would sip, because it concentrates threefold by the end.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Braise low. Bring to a boil, then lower to the gentlest simmer, a few lazy bubbles breaking the surface. Cover and cook 90 minutes, turning the cubes every 30 minutes with a wooden spoon so the top layer does not dry out. A hard boil toughens the lean and emulsifies the fat into the sauce; a bare simmer lets the collagen in the skin and between the layers convert to gelatin while the lean stays intact. If the liquid drops below halfway up the pork, add 60 ml / ¼ cup hot water.'
    time_min: 90
  - text: 'Check doneness. A chopstick should slide through the skin and fat with no resistance, and the lean should yield when pressed. If the skin still grips, cover and give it 15 more minutes. Taste the liquid now: it should taste sweet-salty and rich, a little under-seasoned for a sauce. Hold back the 1 g / ¼ tsp kosher salt for now, because the reduction will concentrate it.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Reduce to a lacquer. Fish out and discard the ginger, scallion, and star anise. Uncover, raise the heat to medium-high, and boil, stirring and turning the pork every minute with a wooden spoon. Over 10 to 15 minutes the liquid thickens from broth to syrup; the bubbles shrink and turn glossy. Near the end, stir constantly, because the sugars catch fast. Stop when the sauce coats each cube and only a few spoonfuls of thick glaze remain in the pot, about 60 ml / ¼ cup. The gelatin from the skin is what gives the glaze its body; you are concentrating it, not thickening with starch.'
    time_min: 13
  - text: 'Season and serve. Taste a piece with glaze. It should be deeply savory, sweet without being candied, and well salted. If it tastes flat, stir in up to 1 g / ¼ tsp kosher salt. Serve at once over 600 g / 4 cups hot jasmine rice; the glaze stiffens as it cools.'
    time_min: 2

notes: |
  Why no finishing acid. Shanghai hong shao rou is a sweet-savory dish by design, and a splash of vinegar reads as a mistake to anyone who grew up with it. The Shaoxing wine brings some acidity of its own, and the balance comes from the table: plain rice, a sharp cold vegetable, pickles. That is where the acid belongs in this meal.

  Glutamate and color come from two different soys. Light soy seasons; dark soy is aged longer with molasses and adds color and depth with little salt. Using only light soy gives a pale, salty dish; using only dark soy gives a dark, bitter one. The rock-sugar caramel adds a second layer of red-brown color and most of the gloss.

  The skin is the point. Pork skin is mostly collagen. Ninety minutes at a bare simmer converts it to gelatin, which makes the skin trembling-soft and gives the reduced glaze its body without any starch. Skinless belly works but produces a thinner glaze.

  Pork belly reaches well above 90 °C during the braise, far past the USDA 63 °C / 145 °F guideline for whole-muscle pork; doneness here is about tenderness, not safety.
