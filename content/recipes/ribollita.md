before_you_start:
  - 'Ribollita is a two-day dish. Day one builds the soup and soaks the bread (about 2 hours including cooking); the pot then rests overnight in the refrigerator. Day two is a 20-minute reboil. Eaten on day one it is a good bean soup; the overnight rest is what turns it into ribollita.'
  - 'Cook the cannellini first, ideally a day earlier: 250 g dried beans, soaked overnight and simmered, give the 700 g cooked beans and 500 ml cooking liquid this recipe uses. Keep the beans in their liquid; that starchy liquid is part of the broth.'
  - 'Slice the bread and leave it out to dry for a few hours, or the night before, if it is not already stale. Fresh bread dissolves into paste instead of soaking up the soup.'

steps:
  - text: 'Day one, soffritto. In a large heavy pot (at least 6 L / 6 qt), warm the 60 g / ¼ cup olive oil over medium-low heat. Add the 200 g / 1 large onion, 120 g / 2 carrots and 120 g / 2 ribs celery, all finely diced, with the 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt. Cook gently, stirring with a wooden spoon every few minutes, for 15 minutes, until the vegetables are soft, sweet and just beginning to turn gold at the edges. This slow sweat is where most of the flavor of the soup comes from; rushing it over high heat browns the outside and leaves the inside raw.'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Add the 12 g / 3 cloves sliced garlic and the 1 g / ½ tsp red pepper flakes and cook 1 minute until fragrant, keeping the garlic pale. Stir in the 30 g / 2 tbsp tomato paste and cook 2 minutes, pressing it against the pot bottom, until it darkens from bright red to brick. Frying the paste in oil caramelizes its sugars and deepens its savory flavor.'
    technique: 'blooming-aromatics'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Greens and potato. Add the 300 g sliced cavolo nero and the 250 g sliced savoy cabbage by the handful, stirring each addition until it wilts enough to make room. Add the 200 g / 1 medium diced potato. Cook 5 minutes, turning everything through the soffritto so the greens coat in oil.'
    time_min: 7
  - text: 'Beans and broth. Purée half the cannellini (350 g / 2 cups of the 700 g) with 250 ml / 1 cup of the 500 ml bean cooking liquid in a blender or through a food mill until smooth. Add the purée to the pot with the remaining 350 g whole beans, the remaining 250 ml bean liquid, the 1000 ml / 4 cups water, the 60 g Parmigiano rind, the 2 g / 1 sprig rosemary and 6 g / 2 tsp of the soup salt. The purée thickens the broth and gives it a creamy body; the whole beans keep their shape.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Simmer. Bring to a boil, then lower to a gentle simmer, partly covered, for 45 minutes, stirring the bottom now and then with the wooden spoon because the bean purée can catch. The cavolo nero should be completely tender and the broth savory from the cheese rind. Remove the 2 g rosemary sprig. Taste and add the remaining 3 g / 1 tsp kosher salt and the 2 g / 1 tsp black pepper; it should taste fully seasoned now, since the bread will absorb salt overnight.'
    time_min: 45
  - text: 'Layer with bread. Fish out the Parmigiano rind (save it for the reboil). Spread a ladle of soup in the bottom of a large glass or ceramic baking dish or a clean pot, then a layer of the 250 g stale bread slices, then more soup; repeat until the 250 g bread and the soup are used, finishing with soup. Press the bread down with the back of the ladle so it soaks. Cool to room temperature, cover with a fitted lid or a beeswax wrap, and refrigerate overnight with the rind tucked into the dish.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Day two, reboil. Warm the 30 g / 2 tbsp reboil olive oil in a wide heavy pot or Dutch oven over medium heat. Add the soup and bread, with the rind, and break up the bread with the wooden spoon. Cook 15 minutes at a steady simmer, stirring and folding only occasionally so the bottom has time to catch lightly; folding those browned bits back through is part of the flavor. It is ready when it is thick enough that a spoon stands briefly before tipping. If it seizes into a stiff mass, loosen with a splash of water. Taste and adjust with a pinch of kosher salt if needed. Discard the rind.'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Serve. Ladle into warm bowls, scatter the 60 g / ½ small red onion sliced paper thin over the top, and pour about 7 g / 1½ tsp of the 45 g finishing olive oil over each bowl. The raw onion supplies the sharp bite that cuts the beans and bread; the raw oil supplies the peppery, green finish that cooked oil loses.'
    time_min: 3

notes: |
  Why two days. On day one the bread soaks up broth and the starch from the bread and puréed beans swells. Overnight, the starches set and the flavors of the cheese rind, tomato and greens even out. Reboiling in olive oil then breaks the bread down into the soup and lightly browns the bottom, so the result is thick, cohesive and slightly toasty, which no amount of day-one simmering produces.

  The bread. Tuscan bread is baked without salt, which is why Tuscan soups are salted assertively. A plain crusty country loaf or a mild sourdough works; avoid enriched, sweet or seeded breads. It must be dry and hard, or it turns to glue. If it is only a day old, dry the slices in a 120 °C / 250 °F oven for 15 minutes.

  Umami and acid. The Parmigiano rind and the fried tomato paste supply glutamate. Ribollita traditionally has no vinegar or citrus; the brightness at the table comes from raw red onion and raw new-season olive oil, whose bitterness and pepperiness do the job that acid does in other soups. If your bowl still tastes flat, it needs salt, not acid.

  Seasonal timing. Cavolo nero is at its sweetest after the first frosts, which is why ribollita is a late-autumn and winter dish in Tuscany; US markets carry it all winter. Savoy cabbage is also a cold-weather crop and easy to find from October to March.

  Storage. The soup keeps 4 days refrigerated and gets thicker each day; loosen with water when reheating. It freezes well before the bread is added; add the bread after thawing.
