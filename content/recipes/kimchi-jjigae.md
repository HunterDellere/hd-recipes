source:
  name: 'The Korean home-cooking tradition of kimchi jjigae'
  note: 'Kimchi jjigae (김치찌개) is the everyday Korean stew that exists to use kimchi past its prime for eating raw. Once napa kimchi has fermented for several weeks it turns sour and soft, which is a fault at the banchan table and exactly what a stew wants. The household method is consistent across Korea: fry the kimchi with fatty pork, add stock or water and some of the kimchi brine, simmer, and finish with tofu and scallion. Regional and family differences are mostly in the protein (pork, canned tuna, Spam) and whether gochujang is added; this version uses gochugaru only so the kimchi stays the lead flavor.'

before_you_start:
  - 'Use sour kimchi. Napa kimchi that has been refrigerated for at least 3 weeks after its room-temperature ferment (6 weeks or more is better) is what makes this dish; it should taste distinctly sour and the leaves should be soft and slightly translucent. Young, crunchy kimchi makes a flat, sweetish stew. Save the brine from the jar, you need 60 ml of it.'
  - 'Start the rice first if you are serving it fresh. Short-grain rice needs a 30-minute soak plus about 25 minutes of cooking and resting, which is longer than the stew. Leftover rice reheated with a splash of water is fine.'
  - 'The anchovy-kelp stock and the knife work run in parallel: put the stock on, then cut everything while it comes to a simmer and steeps.'

steps:
  - text: 'Build the anchovy-kelp stock. Put the 750 ml / 3 cups water, the 8 g / 8 cm square kombu, and the 15 g / 8 cleaned dried anchovies in a small pot and set it over medium heat. When small bubbles start rising from the bottom (about 8 minutes), lift out the kombu; boiled kelp turns slick and bitter. Simmer the anchovies 10 minutes more, then strain and discard them. You want about 600 ml of pale gold stock. Do the knife work in the next step while it heats.'
    time_min: 18
  - text: 'Prep while the stock simmers. Snip the 400 g / 2 cups kimchi into 3 cm pieces in a bowl with kitchen scissors and measure out 60 ml / ¼ cup of brine from the jar. Slice the 250 g / ½ lb pork belly into 5 mm by 4 cm pieces, the 100 g / ½ onion into 5 mm slices, the 30 g / 2 scallions on the bias, and the 300 g tofu into 1.5 cm slabs. Mince the 12 g / 3 cloves garlic.'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Render the pork. Set a heavy 3 L saucepan or Korean earthenware pot (ttukbaegi) over medium heat with the 5 g / 1 tsp neutral oil. Add the 250 g sliced pork belly in one layer and cook, stirring occasionally with a wooden spoon, until the fat has rendered and the edges are golden, about 4 minutes. The browned edges add roasted flavor and the liquid fat is the cooking medium for the kimchi.'
    technique: 'maillard-sear'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Stir-fry the kimchi. Add the 400 g cut kimchi, the 12 g / 3 cloves minced garlic, the 6 g / 1 tbsp gochugaru, and the 4 g / 1 tsp granulated sugar. Stir-fry 5 minutes over medium, until the kimchi turns translucent at the edges, the fat runs red, and the sharp vinegary smell softens into a toasty one. This is the step that separates kimchi jjigae from kimchi soup: frying caramelizes the cabbage sugars, mellows the raw acidity, and dissolves the fat-soluble color and aroma of the chili into the pork fat that will coat every spoonful.'
    technique: 'blooming-aromatics'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Add liquid and simmer. Pour in the 600 ml / 2½ cups strained anchovy-kelp stock and the 60 ml / ¼ cup kimchi brine, scrape up anything stuck to the bottom, and add the 100 g sliced onion. Bring to a boil, cover with the lid slightly ajar, and hold at a lively simmer for 15 minutes. The kimchi softens fully, the onion sweetens the broth, and the pork becomes tender; thin slices simmered this long are far past the 63 °C / 145 °F safe point for pork.'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Season. Taste the broth. Kimchi varies widely in salt and sourness, so this is the stage that calibrates the pot. Stir in 5 g / 1 tsp of the fish sauce, taste again, and add the remaining 5 g / 1 tsp only if the broth still reads thin rather than savory. It should taste sour, spicy, and deeply savory at once; if the sourness is harsh, simmer 3 minutes more uncovered, which drives off some volatile acidity.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Add the tofu and scallion. Lay the 300 g / 1.5 cm tofu slabs over the surface and spoon broth over them. Simmer uncovered 5 minutes, until the tofu is hot through and has taken on color. Scatter the 30 g / 2 sliced scallions over the top and turn off the heat; they should stay bright green and sharp.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Serve. Bring the pot to the table bubbling and serve with the 600 g / 3 cups steamed short-grain rice in individual bowls. Each person spoons broth, kimchi, pork, and tofu over or beside their rice. The kimchi lactic acid is the finishing acid here; nothing needs squeezing over the top.'
    time_min: 1

notes: |
  Why aged kimchi. During a long cold ferment, lactic acid bacteria convert the cabbage sugars to lactic and acetic acid and break down the pectin in the cell walls, so the leaves soften and the flavor turns sour and complex. Raw, that is past its best; simmered, it becomes the whole backbone of the stew: the acid, the funk, much of the salt, and the glutamate from the fish sauce and salted seafood in the original paste. That is also why this recipe has no separate finishing acid. The kimchi supplies it, and adding vinegar to a properly sour pot only makes it sharp.

  Why fry before simmering. Stir-frying the kimchi in pork fat does three things water cannot. It caramelizes some of the remaining sugars for depth, it drives off part of the harsh volatile acidity so the sourness rounds out, and it dissolves capsanthin (the red, fat-soluble pigment in gochugaru) and the garlic aromatics into the fat, which then spreads through the broth as a red, flavorful slick. Skipping this and boiling everything together gives a thinner, sharper stew.

  The umami stack. Anchovy-kelp stock supplies inosinate from the anchovies and glutamate from the kombu, which amplify each other. The kimchi adds glutamate from its fish sauce and fermentation, the pork adds its own, and the fish sauce at the end corrects salt without diluting the broth. Season late: kimchi salt varies so much between batches that any salt added at the start is a guess.

  Fat matters. Pork belly is the standard because its fat renders into the broth and carries the chili. Lean pork loin turns dry and stringy in the simmer. If you prefer less fat, use shoulder, not loin.

  Leftovers. Kimchi jjigae is better the next day, when the tofu and pork have soaked in the broth. Refrigerate up to 3 days in a glass container and reheat to a full simmer. Add fresh scallion when reheating.
