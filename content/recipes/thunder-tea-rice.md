before_you_start:
  - 'Start the rice first. It needs about 35 minutes including the rest, which is exactly the window for the toppings and the paste.'
  - 'The bowl is six small components cooked one after another in the same wok. Chop every vegetable, cube the tofu, and rinse the preserved radish before you light the burner; there is no gap to prep between stir-fries.'
  - 'Make the tea paste last, after the toppings. Pounded herbs oxidize and dull within about 30 minutes, and the soup is at its brightest the moment the hot water hits it.'
  - 'A stone mortar of at least 750 ml capacity is ideal. A small food processor or blender works (see modifications), at the cost of a smoother, slightly more bitter paste.'

steps:
  - text: 'Cook the rice. Rinse the 300 g / 1½ cups jasmine rice in three changes of cold water until the water runs nearly clear, drain well, and combine with the 420 ml / 1¾ cups water in a heavy saucepan. Bring to a boil, cover, drop to the lowest heat for 15 minutes, then pull off the heat and rest covered 10 minutes. Rinsing strips surface amylose so the grains stay separate under the soup instead of turning to porridge.'
    time_min: 30
  - text: 'Toast the peanuts, then the sesame. In a dry wok over medium-low, stir the 100 g / ⅔ cup raw peanuts constantly for 8 to 10 minutes until evenly golden and they smell like peanut butter; tip onto a plate to cool (they crisp as they cool). In the same dry wok, toast the 40 g / ⅓ cup sesame seeds for 2 to 3 minutes until a shade darker and the first seeds start to pop, then tip out. Set aside 40 g of the peanuts for the paste and roughly chop the remaining 60 g for topping.'
    technique: 'dry-toasting'
    time_min: 13
  - text: 'Fry the preserved radish. Wipe the wok, heat 15 ml / 1 tbsp of the neutral oil over medium, and add the 60 g / ½ cup squeezed preserved radish with about 4 g / 1 clove of the minced garlic. Stir-fry 3 to 4 minutes until the radish edges turn golden and the garlic is fragrant, sprinkle in the 4 g / 1 tsp granulated sugar, and toss 30 seconds more to glaze. No salt here; the radish is cured in it. Transfer to a small bowl.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Pan-fry the tofu. Add 15 ml / 1 tbsp more neutral oil to the wok over medium-high, spread the 300 g / 10 oz cubed tofu in a single layer, and leave it undisturbed 2 minutes before turning. Fry 6 to 8 minutes in total until most faces are golden and the cubes feel firm and springy. Season with 2 g / ¾ tsp of the kosher salt while hot so it adheres, then remove.'
    technique: 'shallow-frying'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Stir-fry the long beans. Heat 15 ml / 1 tbsp neutral oil over high, add about 8 g / 2 cloves of the minced garlic for 10 seconds, then the 200 g / 10 cut long beans. Toss 1 minute, add 30 ml / 2 tbsp water from the kettle to steam them through, and stir-fry until the water is gone and the beans are bright green and just tender with a faint snap, about 3 minutes. Season with 2 g / ¾ tsp kosher salt and remove.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Stir-fry the choy sum. Heat the last 15 ml / 1 tbsp neutral oil over high, add the remaining 8 g / 2 cloves minced garlic for 10 seconds, then the sliced choy sum stems from the 300 g / 1 bunch for 1 minute before adding the shredded leaves. Toss until just wilted and glossy, 1 to 2 minutes, season with the last 2 g / ¾ tsp kosher salt, and remove. Cook every vegetable to bright and crisp-tender: each topping is eaten at room temperature under hot soup, which will soften it further.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Grind the dry base of the paste. Bring a kettle of water to a boil and let it fall to about 85°C / 185°F (2 to 3 minutes off the boil). Pour 30 ml / 2 tbsp of it over the 6 g / 2 tbsp green tea leaves to soften them for 1 minute. Meanwhile, in a mortar pound the reserved 40 g toasted peanuts and the 40 g / ⅓ cup toasted sesame seeds to a coarse, oily meal. This is the "thunder" of the name: the rhythmic pounding (lei) of the tea (cha) bowl.'
    technique: 'mortar-pounding'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Pound in the tea and herbs. Add the softened tea leaves with their liquid and the 7 g / 2½ tsp kosher salt for the paste (the salt crystals work as an abrasive) and pound to a dark green mass. Add the 25 g / 1 cup Thai basil, 20 g / 1 cup mint, 15 g / ¾ cup laksa leaf, and 15 g / ¾ cup cilantro in two or three handfuls, grinding each into the paste before adding the next, until no whole leaf pieces remain, 6 to 8 minutes. Work in the 1 g / ½ tsp white pepper. The result should look like a thick, slightly grainy pesto.'
    technique: 'mortar-pounding'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Make the soup and correct the seasoning. Whisk the 1000 ml / 4 cups hot water (still at about 85°C / 185°F, not boiling) into the paste a ladle at a time until smooth, then pour into a jug or serving bowl. Taste it on its own: it should be savory, nutty and herbal with a clean green-tea bitterness at the back. If it tastes thin or harshly bitter, it needs salt, not more tea; add kosher salt a pinch at a time until the bitterness reads as depth rather than astringency. Water at a full boil cooks the herbs grey and pulls extra catechins from the tea.'
    technique: 'steeping-tea'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Assemble and serve. Divide the rice among four wide bowls. Arrange the long beans, choy sum, tofu, and preserved radish in separate wedges over each mound, then scatter with the 60 g chopped toasted peanuts. Serve the soup alongside in small bowls (about 250 ml / 1 cup each). Eaters either pour the soup over the rice and mix it all together, or alternate spoonfuls of rice with sips of soup; both are correct.'
    time_min: 3

notes: |
  What it is. Lei cha (擂茶, "pounded tea") is a Hakka dish that travelled with Hakka migrants from Guangdong and Fujian into Taiwan, Malaysia and Singapore, splitting into distinct traditions along the way. The Malaysian and Singaporean form drafted here is a savory meal: an herb-and-tea soup ground in a ridged clay bowl (lei bo) with a guava-wood pestle, served with rice and a spread of stir-fried vegetables. Taiwanese Hakka lei cha is a different thing entirely, a thick sweet or lightly salted drink of ground tea, sesame, peanuts and grains, often topped with puffed rice.

  Why it works. The bowl is built on contrast. The soup is bitter (tea catechins, herb polyphenols), cool (menthol from mint), and rich (the oil released when sesame and peanuts are pounded, which emulsifies with the water and carries the herb aromatics). The toppings supply the counterweight: salt from the seasoned vegetables, sweet-salt glutamate from the caramelized preserved radish, browned-protein savoriness from the fried tofu, and crunch from the peanuts. Because there is no stock or meat, the preserved radish is the umami anchor of the bowl; do not skip it.

  On acid. There is no finishing acid in lei cha, and adding lime or vinegar is a Western correction that muddies the tea. The bitterness of the green tea performs the same job acid usually does, cutting the fat of the toasted seeds and resetting the palate between bites. Season the soup with salt until the bitterness reads clean; that calibration is the whole art of the dish.

  Temperature matters twice. Water at about 85°C keeps the chlorophyll in the herbs bright and limits catechin extraction from the tea, so the soup stays green and pleasantly bitter rather than grey and astringent. Toppings are cooked hot but served at room temperature; the hot soup warms them at the table.
