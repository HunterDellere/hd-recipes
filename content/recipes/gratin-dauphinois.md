steps:
  - text: 'Prepare the dish. Heat the oven to 160 °C / 325 °F with a rack in the middle. Rub the inside of a 2 L / 2 qt shallow gratin or baking dish (about 20 by 30 cm / 8 by 12 in) all over with the cut sides of the 4 g / 1 halved garlic clove, pressing so the clove breaks down and leaves its juice on the surface. Let it dry for a minute, then coat the dish with the 15 g / 1 tbsp softened butter.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Infuse the dairy. In a wide heavy saucepan or Dutch oven, combine the 500 ml / 2 cups milk, 250 ml / 1 cup cream, 12 g / 3 smashed garlic cloves, 1 bay leaf, 10 g / 1 tbsp plus ¼ tsp kosher salt, 1 g / ½ tsp white pepper and 0.5 g / ¼ tsp grated nutmeg. Bring to a bare simmer over medium heat, then take off the heat and steep 10 minutes. The fat in the cream extracts the garlic flavor gently, without the harsh edge of raw garlic. Taste: the liquid should be noticeably saltier than you would want to drink, because the potatoes will absorb it.'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Slice the potatoes while the dairy steeps. Peel the 1000 g / 2¼ lb Yukon Golds and slice them 3 mm / ⅛ in thick on a mandoline, straight into a dry bowl. Do not rinse them. Even thickness matters more than exact thickness: uneven slices give a gratin that is both crunchy and mushy.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Simmer the potatoes. Remove the 12 g garlic cloves and the 1 bay leaf from the dairy. Add the 1000 g sliced potatoes to the pot, separating any slices stuck together, and bring back to a gentle simmer over medium heat. Cook 10 minutes, stirring often and scraping the bottom with a wooden spoon (milk scorches easily), until the liquid has visibly thickened and the slices are flexible but still firm in the center. The starch released from the slices thickens the dairy here, and that starch also protects the milk proteins from curdling in the oven.'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'Assemble. Tip the potatoes and their liquid into the prepared dish. Shake the dish and use a metal spatula to settle the slices into roughly even, flat layers, laying the top layer flat and slightly overlapping for a neat surface. The liquid should come just to the top layer, not over it; the exposed potato edges are what brown.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Bake. Set the dish on a sheet pan to catch any bubbling over and bake at 160 °C / 325 °F for 75 minutes, until the top is deep golden in patches and a paring knife slides through all the layers with no resistance. If the top is pale after 60 minutes, move the dish to the top rack for the last 15. Low heat keeps the dairy at a gentle bubble, so it reduces and thickens rather than boiling hard and splitting into curds and fat.'
    time_min: 75
  - text: 'Rest and serve. Let the gratin rest 15 minutes before serving. Cut into squares and lift out with a metal spatula, or spoon it straight from the dish at the table.'
    time_min: 15

notes: |
  The potato. Gratin dauphinois needs a potato in the middle of the starch range. Yukon Gold slices hold their shape through simmering and baking, and they release enough surface starch to thicken the milk and cream into a sauce. Russets release too much starch and their slices fall apart, so the gratin turns grainy and heavy. Red, new and fingerling potatoes release so little starch that the liquid stays thin and tends to separate. In French markets the usual choices are Charlotte or Bintje; Yukon Gold is the closest widely available US equivalent, and it is in good supply all winter.

  Why simmer in the dairy first. Raw slices baked in cold dairy take nearly two hours, and the milk often curdles before the potatoes are tender, because milk proteins clump with prolonged heat and the acid in potatoes. Simmering the slices in the dairy first does three things: it releases starch that thickens the liquid and coats the milk proteins so they do not clump, it seasons the potatoes through, and it cuts the oven time roughly in half.

  No cheese. The traditional gratin of the Dauphiné has no cheese; the browned top comes from the milk sugars and proteins at the surface. Cheese makes a different dish and masks the garlic and nutmeg.

  Umami and acid. Neither is added, by design. The gratin is a side for roast lamb, beef, chicken or a pan-sauced steak, and the main dish supplies the savory depth and the acid. If you serve it alone, a sharply dressed green salad is the classic companion.
