before_you_start:

  - 'Eggs must be at room temperature to whip to full volume. Cold eggs straight from the fridge go into a bowl of warm tap water for 5 minutes.'

steps:
  - text: 'Brush the inside of two 4-inch round cake pans with the 8 g / 2 tsp softened butter, covering the walls completely. Press a parchment circle onto each base, butter the parchment with what is left on the brush, then add the 5 g / 2 tsp Dutch-process cocoa, rotate to coat every surface, and knock out the excess. Cocoa instead of flour keeps the outside dark and adds no raw-flour taste. Set the pans aside.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Melt the 100 g / 7 tbsp butter in a small light-colored saucepan over medium heat. It foams, the foam subsides, and the milk solids on the bottom turn amber and smell of toasted hazelnut, about 4 to 5 minutes. Pull the pan off the burner the moment it reaches amber; the solids go from brown to burnt within 20 seconds.'
    technique: 'brown-butter'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Let the brown butter stand off heat for 2 minutes so it drops below the scorch range for chocolate. Add the 120 g / 4¼ oz chopped dark chocolate and leave it untouched for 1 minute, then stir slowly with a wooden spoon until smooth and glossy. Dissolve the 1 g / ½ tsp instant espresso powder in the 2 g / ½ tsp vanilla extract and stir it in. The mixture should read 45 to 50°C (113 to 122°F): warm to the touch, not hot. If any chocolate remains unmelted, set the saucepan over the lowest heat for 10-second bursts.'
    technique: 'chocolate-butter-emulsion'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'In a medium bowl, beat the 2 room-temperature eggs, 2 egg yolks, 50 g / ¼ cup granulated sugar, and 1.5 g / ¼ tsp fine sea salt with an electric hand mixer on high for 3 to 4 minutes (or a balloon whisk for about 6). Stop at the ribbon stage: the mixture is pale, roughly tripled, and a trail dropped from the beaters holds on the surface for 3 seconds. This foam is the only lift the cake gets, and the dissolved salt seasons the chocolate from the inside.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Pour the warm chocolate mixture down the side of the bowl in two additions, folding with a large metal spoon after each: cut down through the center, sweep along the bottom, and turn the batter over. Then sift the 14 g / 1½ tbsp all-purpose flour over the surface and fold just until no dry streaks remain. Some loss of volume is normal; stop folding as soon as the batter is uniform.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Divide the batter between the prepared pans, about 210 g each, which fills them roughly 1 inch deep. Set the pans on the preheated sheet tray.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Bake 11 to 13 minutes (13 to 15 if the batter was refrigerated). Pull when the outer ring of about ¾ inch is set, risen, and matte, and has begun to pull away from the wall, while the center circle of about 2 inches is still slightly glossy and wobbles like a just-set custard when you nudge the tray. Check at 11 minutes the first time you bake this in your oven; 60 seconds over turns the center from liquid to fudge.'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'Rest the cakes in their pans for exactly 1 minute so the crust firms enough to hold. Run a thin knife around each edge, set a plate upside down over the pan, invert, wait 10 seconds, and lift the pan straight up. Peel off the parchment.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Sprinkle each cake with a 0.25 g pinch from the 0.5 g flaky sea salt and set one scoop from the 130 g vanilla bean ice cream beside it, not on top, so the cold does not set the lava before it reaches the plate. Serve immediately; the center keeps cooking from residual heat and is fully liquid only for the first 5 minutes or so.'
    time_min: 1

notes: |
  Why the center stays liquid. A lava cake is a timing trick, not a different batter. Heat moves inward from the pan walls and the hot sheet tray, and egg proteins set progressively from the outside: yolks thicken from about 65°C and whole-egg custard firms near 80°C. At 425°F the rim passes that threshold in minutes while the center is still below it. Pulling the cake in that window, then resting only 60 seconds, captures a cake that is set at the edge and a warm custard at the core. A lower oven gives a more even cake and no lava.

  Why the extra yolks. Yolks bring emulsifiers and fat without the extra water and stiff-setting proteins of the whites. Two whole eggs plus two yolks makes a center that is thick and glossy, like a pudding, instead of thin and runny.

  Why brown butter. Browning drives off the butter's water and builds toasted, nutty compounds from the milk solids. Against 70% chocolate those notes read as depth rather than as a separate flavor. The hot butter also melts the chocolate on its own, so no double boiler is needed.

  Seasoning. Salt goes into the egg foam, where it dissolves completely, and a few flakes go on top at the end. The first suppresses bitterness and makes the chocolate taste more like itself; the second gives bright contrast against the sweet ice cream. Espresso at 1 g is under the threshold where coffee is recognizable.

  Why 4-inch pans, and how full. A 4-inch pan filled about 1 inch deep gives a wider liquid zone than a deep ramekin, which makes the lava spill across the plate when you cut it. Filling deeper than about 1¼ inches pushes the bake time up and risks a dome that cracks before the edge sets. For a fully baked cake in the same pans, the Individual decadent chocolate cakes recipe uses the same chocolate-butter base with more flour.

  If it fails. A cake that collapses into a puddle on inversion came out 1 to 2 minutes early, or rested too briefly. A cake that is fudgy all the way through baked too long. Both are still very good with ice cream; adjust the time by 1 minute next round and note it for your oven.
