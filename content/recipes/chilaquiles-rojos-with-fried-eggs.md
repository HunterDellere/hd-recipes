before_you_start:
  - 'The tortillas need to be dry before they hit the oil, or they absorb fat and turn leathery instead of crisp. Either leave the cut wedges spread on a sheet pan uncovered overnight, or dry them in a 120 °C / 250 °F oven for 15 to 20 minutes while you make the salsa. Freshly made tortillas need the oven step.'
  - 'Make the salsa first and keep it at a bare simmer. The totopos, the eggs, and the toss all happen in the last 10 minutes, and the chips must go into hot salsa and onto the plate immediately.'

steps:
  - text: 'Dry the tortillas. Cut the 240 g / 8 tortillas into 6 wedges each. If they are not already a day old and leathery, spread the wedges in one layer on a sheet pan and dry them in a 120 °C / 250 °F oven for 15 to 20 minutes, until they feel stiff and papery but have not colored. Surface moisture is what makes a fried chip greasy: water flashing to steam lets oil into the gaps, while a dry tortilla sets crisp almost instantly.'
    time_min: 20
  - text: 'Toast the chiles. Heat a dry comal or heavy skillet over medium heat. Open the 24 g / 4 guajillo chiles and the 12 g / 1 ancho chile flat and press them onto the hot surface with a metal spatula for 10 to 15 seconds per side, until they blister slightly, turn a shade darker, and smell like dried fruit. Pull them the moment you smell any sharp smoke; burnt chile turns the whole salsa bitter and cannot be fixed. Drop the chiles into a bowl, cover with just-boiled water, and soak 15 minutes until soft.'
    technique: 'dry-toasting'
    time_min: 17
  - text: 'Char the vegetables. On the same comal over medium-high heat, roast the 300 g / 3 Roma tomatoes, the 40 g slice of white onion, and the 8 g / 2 unpeeled garlic cloves, turning occasionally. Pull the garlic after about 8 minutes, when it is soft and spotted black; the tomatoes and onion take 10 to 12 minutes, until the skins are blackened in patches and the tomatoes slump. The char adds a smoky bitterness that the chiles need as a counterweight to their sweetness.'
    technique: 'comal-charring'
    time_min: 12
  - text: 'Blend. Peel the garlic. Drain the chiles and discard the soaking water, which carries bitterness from the skins. Blend the chiles, tomatoes (skins and all), onion, garlic, the 240 ml / 1 cup chicken stock, the 0.5 g / ½ tsp Mexican oregano, and the 5 g / 1½ tsp kosher salt on high for a full minute until completely smooth. Push the salsa through a fine-mesh sieve with a wooden spoon to remove the guajillo skins, which otherwise stay as tough flecks.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Fry the salsa. Heat the 15 ml / 1 tbsp neutral oil in a wide skillet over medium-high heat until it shimmers. Pour in the salsa all at once (it will spatter hard; hold the lid as a shield). Stir with a wooden spoon and cook at a lively simmer for 6 to 8 minutes, until it darkens from orange-red to brick red and thickens to the consistency of heavy cream. Frying the purée in hot fat cooks out the raw chile and tomato flavor and concentrates the glutamates in the tomato. Taste and adjust with up to 1 g / ¼ tsp more kosher salt; the chips carry a little salt of their own. Turn the heat to its lowest setting and keep it warm. If it thickens past heavy cream while you fry the chips, loosen it with 15 ml / 1 tbsp water.'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Fry the totopos. Heat the 480 ml / 2 cups neutral oil in a heavy saucepan to 175 °C / 350 °F. Fry the dried wedges in 3 or 4 batches, stirring with a slotted spoon, for 1½ to 2 minutes per batch, until golden and the vigorous bubbling has almost stopped (the bubbling is the last water leaving; when it quiets, the chip is crisp). Lift onto a rack or paper-lined tray and sprinkle each batch with a share of the 1 g / ¼ tsp fine sea salt while still glossy, so it sticks.'
    technique: 'deep-fry'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Fry the eggs. Heat the 15 ml / 1 tbsp neutral oil in a small skillet over medium-high heat until it shimmers. Crack in the 2 eggs and fry for about 2 minutes, spooning hot oil over the whites near the yolk, until the edges are brown and lacy and the whites are fully set while the yolks stay runny. Season with the 0.5 g pinch of kosher salt.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Toss and plate. Raise the heat under the salsa to medium so it bubbles. Add all the totopos, fold them through with a wooden spoon for 30 seconds until every piece is coated, and plate immediately. Thirty seconds gives a mix of soft edges and crisp centers; a minute or more turns them to a soft casserole, which some cooks prefer (see modifications).'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Top. Lay a fried egg on each plate. Drizzle over the 60 g / ¼ cup Mexican crema, then scatter the 40 g / ⅓ cup crumbled queso fresco, the 30 g / ¼ cup paper-thin white onion, and the 5 g / ¼ cup chopped cilantro. Serve at once; chilaquiles keep their texture for about five minutes.'
    time_min: 2

notes: |
  Why fry the salsa. Blended chile and tomato taste raw and slightly bitter. A few minutes in very hot oil (the "salsa frita" step common to Mexican cooked sauces) caramelizes the sugars in the tomato and chiles, evaporates water, and lets the fat-soluble aromas of the chiles dissolve into the oil, which is why the color deepens. A salsa that is only simmered never reaches the same depth.

  Glutamate and acid. The glutamate comes from the ripe tomato, the chicken stock, and the aged-milk tang of queso fresco. There is no squeeze of lime in the traditional dish and none is needed: charred tomato brings its own acidity, and the crema adds a lactic sourness at the table. If your tomatoes are flat, add 5 ml / 1 tsp lime juice to the salsa at the end of frying rather than at the table, so it does not land on the egg.

  Why dry tortillas. A moist tortilla in hot oil steams first, and the steam channels let oil soak in; a dry tortilla sets its surface immediately. The same logic explains why chilaquiles are the traditional use for stale tortillas.

  The frying oil. Let it cool, strain it through a fine sieve into a glass jar, and reuse it two or three times for frying tortillas.
