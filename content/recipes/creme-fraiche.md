before_you_start:
  - 'Start 2 to 3 days before you need it. The cream cultures 18 to 36 hours at room temperature, then needs at least 12 hours in the fridge to firm up.'
  - 'Read the labels. The buttermilk must say "cultured" or "live and active cultures". The cream should be pasteurized, not ultra-pasteurized, if you can find it. Ultra-pasteurized cream works, but it sets thinner and can take the full 36 hours.'
  - 'The culturing temperature matters more than the time. At 20 to 24 °C / 68 to 75 °F it takes about 24 hours. A cold kitchen below 18 °C can stall it; set the jar near, not on, a warm appliance.'

steps:
  - text: 'Take the chill off the cream. Pour the 480 g / 2 cups heavy cream into a clean 750 ml / 3-cup glass jar and let it stand on the counter 30 minutes, or warm it in a saucepan over low heat to 24 °C / 75 °F, no higher. The starter bacteria are mesophilic: they grow best between 20 and 30 °C, and cream straight from the fridge gives them a slow start.'
    time_min: 30
  - text: 'Inoculate. Stir in the 30 g / 2 tbsp cultured buttermilk with a clean metal spoon for 10 seconds. That is about 6% starter, which is enough live Lactococcus bacteria to outnumber anything else in the cream from the first hour.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Culture. Cover the jar with a clean cloth held by a rubber band, or rest the lid on top without screwing it down, so gas can escape. Leave it at room temperature, away from direct sun, 18 to 36 hours. The bacteria convert lactose into lactic acid; as the pH falls toward 4.6 the milk proteins (caseins) lose their charge and link into a soft gel, which is what thickens it. Some strains also make diacetyl, the compound that smells of butter.'
    time_min: 1440
  - text: 'Check it. From 18 hours, tilt the jar. It is ready when the cream moves as one mass, like thick yogurt, and smells clean, nutty and sour. If it is still pourable, leave it and check every 6 hours. Discard it if you see pink, orange or fuzzy growth, or if it smells putrid or yeasty rather than sour: that means other microbes won the race, usually because the starter cultures were not live.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Chill and set. Stir once, screw the lid on, and refrigerate at least 12 hours. Cold firms the fat and the gel together, and it thickens noticeably overnight. It keeps 10 to 14 days in the fridge and gets more sour each day. Save 30 g / 2 tbsp of a good batch as the starter for the next one.'
    time_min: 720

notes: |
  Why crème fraîche does not curdle when you boil it: it is around 30 to 40% fat and relatively low in protein. The casein that curdles sour cream (about 20% fat) in a hot sauce is diluted by so much fat that it cannot form visible curds.

  Why it whips well with cream: the acid-set gel stabilises the foam, so a whipped cream made with a third crème fraîche holds its shape for hours in the fridge instead of weeping. That makes it the right topping for a dessert that is finished in the morning and served in the evening.
