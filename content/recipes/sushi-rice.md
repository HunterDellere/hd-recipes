before_you_start:
  - 'Build in 30 minutes of passive soak between washing and cooking. Start the rice about 80 minutes before you plan to eat; the rice is the longest pole in any sushi meal, so prep fillings while it soaks, cooks, and rests.'
  - 'Have the seasoning (awasezu) dissolved and a wide, shallow bowl wiped damp before the rice finishes resting. The vinegar has to hit the rice within the first minute out of the pot, while the grain surfaces are still hot and porous enough to absorb it.'
  - 'Sushi rice is served at body temperature and never refrigerated. Plan to use it within 4 hours of seasoning; hold it covered with a damp towel on the counter in the meantime.'

steps:
  - text: 'Wash the 450 g rice. Put it in a large bowl, cover with cold tap water, swirl once with your hand, and pour the water off immediately. Dry rice absorbs the first water fastest, so do not let it sit in the cloudy starch. Add fresh water, gently rake and rub the grains with spread fingers for 10 seconds (polishing, not crushing), and pour off. Repeat 4 or 5 times until the water is only faintly translucent. Drain in a fine-mesh sieve.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Soak. Combine the drained rice with the 560 ml water and the 5 g kombu square in the rice-cooker bowl or a heavy lidded saucepan. Leave for 30 minutes. The cold soak hydrates the core of each grain so it cooks evenly from center to surface, and pulls glutamate out of the kombu into the water the rice will absorb.'
    time_min: 30
  - text: 'Make the awasezu while the rice soaks. Combine the 90 ml rice vinegar, 30 g sugar, and 9 g fine sea salt in a small saucepan and warm over low heat, stirring, just until the crystals dissolve, about 1 minute. Do not let it simmer: acetic acid is volatile, and boiled vinegar loses the bright top notes you are seasoning with. (A microwave works too: 20 seconds, then stir.) Set aside.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Cook. Pull out the 5 g kombu square just before cooking (cooked kombu goes slick and can turn the rice faintly bitter). Rice cooker: run the sushi or white-rice setting. Stovetop: cover the saucepan, bring to a boil over medium-high (you will hear it rattle and see steam jet from the lid, about 6 minutes), then drop to the lowest heat and cook 13 minutes without lifting the lid.'
    time_min: 20
  - text: 'Rest, covered and off heat, for 10 minutes. The rest lets residual steam finish the surface of each grain and equalizes moisture from the bottom of the pot to the top. Meanwhile, wipe the inside of a wide, shallow bowl (a wooden hangiri is ideal; a wide glass or ceramic bowl or a sheet pan works) with a towel dampened with water so the rice does not stick to it.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Season. Turn the hot rice into the damp bowl in one motion and spread it loosely. Pour all of the awasezu (about 115 ml) over a rice paddle or flat spatula held above the rice, moving it across the surface so the seasoning rains down evenly instead of pooling in one spot.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Cut and fold. Hold the paddle at a 45-degree angle and slice through the rice in parallel strokes, then lift and turn portions over from the bottom. Never stir or mash: stirring ruptures the softened grain surfaces and turns the rice into paste. Work for about 1 minute, until no wet patches remain at the bottom of the bowl.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Fan to body temperature. Keep folding gently while fanning the rice with a fan, a folded magazine, or a hair dryer on its cool setting, about 4 minutes. Fast evaporative cooling drives off surface moisture and excess acidity and sets the gloss; slow cooling leaves the grains wet and dull. Stop when the rice feels just warm to the back of your hand, around 35 °C / 95 °F.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Taste and hold. A few grains should read clearly sweet, sour, and salted all at once, slightly louder than tastes right on their own, because nori, fish, and vegetables will dilute it. If it reads flat, sprinkle 1 g / ¼ tsp more fine sea salt and fold once more. Cover the bowl with a damp, wrung-out towel and keep at room temperature. Use within 4 hours.'
    time_min: 1

notes: |
  Why wash. Milled rice carries a film of loose surface starch and bran dust. Left on, it gelatinizes into a paste that glues grains together and dulls the seasoning. Sushi rice wants the opposite: grains that hold their shape individually but cling to each other under light pressure. Washing until the water runs nearly clear is what gives you that.

  Why drier than table rice. The roughly 115 ml of awasezu is additional liquid that the grain surfaces absorb after cooking. Rice cooked at the normal table ratio and then seasoned turns soft and sticky. The 1.05 × volume water ratio here leaves room for the vinegar.

  The seasoning ratio. This awasezu is 20% of the raw rice weight in vinegar, with sugar at a third of the vinegar and salt at about a tenth. That is a middle-of-the-road home ratio: assertive enough to stand up to soy, nori, and rich fillings in hand rolls. Edo-style sushi shops run less sugar and more salt; many American restaurants run noticeably sweeter. Adjust the sugar to taste once you have made it once, but keep the salt; under-salted sushi rice is the most common reason homemade sushi tastes flat.

  Why hot rice, warm seasoning. Starch granules on the surface of freshly cooked rice are swollen and open; they take up seasoning in the first minute and then begin to seal as they cool. Pour vinegar on cool rice and it slides off and pools. Warming the awasezu just enough to dissolve the sugar keeps the temperature of the rice up during seasoning.

  Why fan. Evaporation pulls off surface water and some of the sharpest acetic acid at the same time, leaving a glossy, slightly tacky surface and a rounder vinegar flavor. It also brings the rice to body temperature quickly, which is the serving temperature: warm enough to stay soft and aromatic against cold fish, cool enough not to wilt nori or warm the fish.

  Never refrigerate. Cooked starch retrogrades fastest at refrigerator temperatures: the amylopectin recrystallizes and the grains go hard, dry, and chalky within an hour. This is why sushi rice is made the same day, held under a damp towel, and used within a few hours.
