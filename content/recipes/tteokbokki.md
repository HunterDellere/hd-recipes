source:
  name: 'The Korean street-food (bunsik) tradition of red tteokbokki'
  note: 'The red, gochujang-based tteokbokki sold from street stalls and bunsik snack shops dates to the 1950s in Seoul, and is a newer, spicier relative of gungjung tteokbokki, the soy-braised royal-court dish. The street version is a simple sauce of gochujang, gochugaru, and sugar in anchovy-kelp stock, with fish cakes (eomuk) as the usual companion and boiled eggs as the common addition. Its texture comes from the rice cakes themselves: their surface starch thickens the sauce as they simmer.'

  - 'Boil the eggs while the stock simmers; both take about 10 minutes and use separate pots.'

steps:
  - text: 'Make the stock. Put the 700 ml / 3 cups water, the 8 g / 8 cm square kombu, and the 15 g / 8 cleaned dried anchovies in a pot over medium heat. When it reaches a simmer, lift out the kombu so the stock does not turn slick and bitter, then simmer the anchovies 10 minutes more. Strain; you need 500 ml / 2 cups. Top up with water if you are short.'
    time_min: 18
  - text: 'Boil the eggs. While the stock simmers, bring a small pot of water to a boil, lower in the 3 large eggs with a spoon, and boil 10 minutes for fully set yolks. Move them to a bowl of ice water for 5 minutes, then peel. Hard-cooked yolks are the right choice here: they crumble into the sauce and thicken it when you cut the eggs open in the bowl.'
    technique: 'ice-bath-shock'
    time_min: 15
  - text: 'Prep the rice cakes, fish cakes, and scallions. Pull apart the 450 g / 1 lb rice cakes so none are stuck together, soaking them now if they are hard. Cut the 200 g / 7 oz fish cakes into 4 cm triangles and the 60 g / 4 scallions into 5 cm lengths, keeping the whites and greens apart.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Build the sauce. In a wide 28 cm / 11-inch skillet or shallow pot, whisk the 500 ml / 2 cups strained stock with the 60 g / 3 tbsp gochujang, the 6 g / 1 tbsp gochugaru, the 19 g / 1½ tbsp granulated sugar, the 10 g / 2 tsp light soy sauce, and the 8 g / 2 cloves minced garlic until the paste dissolves. Bring to a boil over medium-high and taste. It should taste a little too strong, sweet and spicy and salty at once, because the rice cakes are unseasoned and will absorb it.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Simmer the rice cakes. Drain the 450 g rice cakes if soaked and add them to the boiling sauce. Simmer over medium for 8 minutes, stirring every minute with a wooden spoon and scraping the bottom, since the starch they shed makes the sauce catch. They are ready for the next step when a cut piece is soft all the way to the center and the sauce has begun to thicken.'
    time_min: 8
  - text: 'Add the fish cakes, eggs, and scallion whites. Stir in the 200 g / 7 oz cut fish cakes, the 3 peeled eggs, and the 30 g white parts of the scallions. Simmer 4 minutes more, rolling the eggs in the sauce, until the sauce is glossy and coats the back of a spoon. If it thickens before the rice cakes are tender, add water 30 ml / 2 tbsp at a time. Taste once more: if it reads flat, add 2 g / ½ tsp more of the soy; if it reads harsh, a little more simmering rounds it.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Finish and serve. Stir in the 30 g green parts of the scallions and turn off the heat. Serve immediately, cutting the eggs in half in the bowl so the yolk mixes into the sauce. Rice cakes firm up as they cool, so tteokbokki is best eaten within 10 minutes; leftovers revive with a splash of water over low heat.'
    time_min: 1

notes: |
  How the sauce thickens. Garae-tteok are made from steamed, pounded non-glutinous rice, so their surface is loose gelatinized starch. As they simmer, that starch dissolves into the liquid and thickens it, which is why the sauce turns from a thin red broth to a glaze without any added starch. It is also why the pot needs frequent stirring: the starch settles and scorches on the bottom. Too long a simmer overshoots, so the rice cakes turn mushy on the outside and the sauce goes pasty; pull it when the sauce coats a spoon.

  The balance. Tteokbokki is meant to be sweet, spicy, and savory in roughly equal measure. Gochujang supplies sweetness, heat, salt, and fermented depth; gochugaru adds a sharper top note and a brighter red; sugar balances the bitterness of the chili. The glutamate comes from the anchovy-kelp stock, the gochujang, the soy, and the fish cakes, which release their own seasoning into the sauce as they warm.

  Why no finishing acid. The street-food tradition does not add vinegar or citrus, and the dish does not need it: gochujang carries a mild fermented acidity, and the sugar-heat balance does the job that acid does in a heavier savory dish. Adding vinegar makes it taste like a different sauce. If you want contrast, serve it the bunsik way, with danmuji (yellow pickled radish) or kimchi on the side.

  Heat level. This version is moderately hot. For milder, reduce the gochugaru to 3 g / ½ tbsp; for hotter, add 1 sliced cheongyang or serrano chili with the fish cakes rather than more gochujang, which would also add more sweetness and salt.
