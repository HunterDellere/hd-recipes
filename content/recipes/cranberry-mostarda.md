steps:
  - text: 'Soak the seeds. In a small saucepan, combine the 10 g / 1 tbsp yellow mustard seeds, the 10 g / 1 tbsp brown mustard seeds, the 120 g / ½ cup red wine vinegar and the 60 g / ¼ cup water. Let them soak 10 minutes off the heat while you mince the 50 g / 1 large shallot; the head start lets the seed coats begin to hydrate.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Build the syrup. Add the shallot, the 150 g / ⅔ cup packed light brown sugar and the 2 g / ¾ tsp kosher salt, and bring to a simmer over medium heat, stirring with a wooden spoon until the sugar dissolves.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Cook the berries. Add the 340 g / 12 oz cranberries and the orange zest (about 3 g / 1 tbsp). Simmer over medium heat, stirring every minute, until about half the berries have burst and the liquid thickens to a loose syrup that coats the spoon, 8 to 10 minutes. Cranberries are rich in pectin and the syrup sets as it cools, so stop while it still looks a little loose.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Bloom the mustard powder cold. While the berries cook, whisk the 6 g / 2½ tsp dry mustard powder into the orange juice (about 60 g / ¼ cup) until smooth and let it stand 10 minutes. Take the pan off the heat and let the mostarda cool below 50 °C / 120 °F, 15 to 20 minutes (spread it on a plate to speed this up), then stir in the bloomed mustard.'
    time_min: 20
  - text: 'Taste and store. It should taste sweet first, then sharply sour, then hot in the nose. If it tastes only sweet, add red wine vinegar 5 g / 1 tsp at a time. Transfer to a glass jar, press a round of parchment onto the surface, lid it and refrigerate at least 12 hours, up to 7 days.'
    time_min: 5

notes: |
  Why the mustard powder goes in cold. Dry mustard has no heat until water wakes up its enzyme (myrosinase), which turns the seed glucosinolates into allyl isothiocyanate, the volatile compound that makes mustard hot. Heat above about 60 °C destroys that enzyme. Stirred into hot fruit, the powder gives color and almost no heat; bloomed in cold juice and added to fruit below 50 °C, it keeps its bite.

  Why it works with rich meat. Fat coats the palate; acid, sugar and pungency cut through it. That is the logic of mint sauce with lamb and mustard with ham, and the mostarda does all three at once while carrying the cranberry that a Thanksgiving table expects.

  It fades slowly. The heat is volatile, so a week-old jar has less bite. To refresh it, bloom 2 g / 1 tsp dry mustard in 5 g / 1 tsp cold water for 10 minutes and stir it in.
