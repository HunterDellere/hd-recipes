source:
  name: 'The Korean bibimbap tradition, served in the dolsot (stone bowl) style'
  note: 'Bibimbap (비빔밥, "mixed rice") is rice topped with seasoned vegetables, usually some beef, an egg, and gochujang, mixed together just before eating. The dolsot version serves it in a heated granite or agalmatolite bowl so that the rice touching the stone fries into nurungji, a browned, crackling crust. Each vegetable is seasoned separately as its own namul (seasoned vegetable side dish), which is what keeps the bowl from tasting uniform. The vegetable set shifts with the season; this version uses ingredients that are in good supply in American markets from autumn through spring.'

before_you_start:
  - 'Start two soaks first: rinse the rice and soak it 30 minutes, and soak the dried shiitake in warm water at the same time (or overnight in the refrigerator). Everything else happens while these soak and while the rice cooks.'
  - 'The namul are served at room temperature, so they can all be made while the rice cooks and rests, in this order: blanch the spinach and sprouts in one pot of salted water, then cook the carrot, zucchini, shiitake, and beef one after another in a single skillet without washing it, lightest flavor first.'
  - 'Stone bowl handling: a dolsot must be heated gradually from cold (low heat for 2 minutes before raising it), never set empty on high heat, never plunged into water while hot, and always set on a wooden board or trivet at the table. It stays hot enough to burn for 15 minutes. If you have no dolsot, a 25 cm / 10-inch cast-iron skillet makes one shared crust; see the modifications.'

steps:
  - text: 'Soak the rice and mushrooms. Rinse the 250 g / 1¼ cups short-grain rice in a bowl of cold water, swirling and draining 3 or 4 times until the water is only faintly cloudy; this removes loose surface starch that would make the rice gummy and keep it from crisping. Cover with fresh water and soak 30 minutes, then drain well in a sieve. At the same time, soak the 15 g / 6 dried shiitake in 250 ml / 1 cup warm water.'
    time_min: 30
  - text: 'Cook the rice. Put the drained rice and the 300 ml / 1¼ cups water in a heavy saucepan with a tight lid. Bring to a boil over medium-high, stir once, cover, and turn the heat to its lowest setting for 12 minutes. Take the pan off the heat and leave it covered 10 minutes to steam. Fluff with a rice paddle. The rice should be cooked through but slightly firm; overly wet rice steams against the hot stone instead of frying.'
    time_min: 25
  - text: 'Spinach namul. While the rice cooks, bring the 2000 ml / 8 cups blanching water and the 15 g / 1½ tbsp kosher salt to a rolling boil and set a bowl of cold water beside the stove. Blanch the 200 g / 7 oz spinach for 30 seconds, just until the stems wilt, then lift it with tongs into the cold water so it stops cooking and stays green. Keep the pot boiling. Gather the spinach into a ball and squeeze hard; it should lose about half its weight in water, which is what lets the seasoning cling instead of diluting. Cut the ball into 5 cm lengths, loosen it, and mix by hand with 1 g / a pinch of the fine sea salt, 3 g / 1 clove of the minced garlic for the namul, 4 g / 1 tsp of the toasted sesame oil for the namul, and 1 g / ¼ tsp of the sesame seeds for the namul. Taste: it should be well seasoned on its own.'
    technique: 'ice-bath-shock'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Soybean sprout namul. Add the 200 g / 7 oz soybean sprouts to the same boiling water and cook uncovered 5 minutes, until the yellow heads have lost their raw beany taste but the stems still snap. Drain, spread on a plate for a minute to steam off, then mix with 1 g / a pinch of the fine sea salt and the remaining 3 g / 1 clove garlic, 4 g / 1 tsp sesame oil, and 1 g / ¼ tsp sesame seeds for the namul. Do not squeeze the sprouts; their crunch depends on the water inside.'
    time_min: 7
  - text: 'Carrot and zucchini. Heat a wide skillet over medium-high with 4 g / 1 tsp of the neutral oil for the vegetables. Add the 100 g / 1 carrot of matchsticks and 0.5 g / a small pinch of the fine sea salt and toss 1 minute, until bright and just pliable but not limp. Move to a plate. Add the other 4 g / 1 tsp oil, the 150 g / 1 small zucchini of matchsticks, and 1 g / a pinch of the fine sea salt, and toss 2 minutes until the edges turn translucent. Plate it separately; keeping each vegetable apart is what lets each one taste of itself in the bowl.'
    time_min: 4
  - text: 'Shiitake. Squeeze the soaked shiitake gently, cut off the woody stems, and slice the caps 5 mm thick. In the same skillet over medium, add the sliced shiitake, the 5 g / 1 tsp light soy sauce for the shiitake, the 2 g / ½ tsp granulated sugar for the shiitake, and 30 ml / 2 tbsp of the mushroom soaking liquid. Toss 2 minutes until the liquid cooks away and the mushrooms look glazed. Plate them. The rehydrated caps carry guanylate, which multiplies the glutamate in the soy and beef.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Beef. Raise the heat to medium-high. Add the 150 g / ⅓ lb ground beef with the 10 g / 2 tsp light soy sauce for the beef, the 4 g / 1 tsp sugar for the beef, the 3 g / 1 clove minced garlic for the beef, and the 0.5 g black pepper. Cook 4 minutes, breaking the meat into small crumbles with a wooden spoon and letting it sit between stirs so it browns, until no pink remains (71 °C / 160 °F for ground beef) and the liquid has cooked off into a glaze. Off the heat, stir in the 4 g / 1 tsp toasted sesame oil for the beef.'
    technique: 'maillard-sear'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Mix the sauce. In a small bowl, stir together the 40 g / 2 tbsp gochujang, the 8 g / 2 tsp toasted sesame oil for the sauce, the 10 g / 2 tsp rice vinegar, the 4 g / 1 tsp sugar for the sauce, the 10 ml / 2 tsp water for the sauce, and the 2 g / ½ tsp sesame seeds for the sauce. Taste: it should be sweet, hot, savory, and clearly tangy. The vinegar is what keeps a bowl this rich from tasting heavy.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Build the bowls and start the crust. Brush the inside of each dolsot with 8 g / 2 tsp of the toasted sesame oil for the crust, coating the walls as well as the bottom. Put half the cooked rice in each bowl and press it gently into an even layer against the bottom and a little way up the sides. Arrange the spinach, sprouts, carrot, zucchini, shiitake, and beef in separate sectors on top, leaving a small space in the center. Set the bowls on burners over low heat for 2 minutes, then raise to medium. Cook 8 minutes, until the rice is crackling steadily and you can smell toasted sesame and rice. Check by sliding a spoon down the side: the crust should be golden brown and release with a little pressure.'
    time_min: 10
  - text: 'Fry the eggs. While the crust forms, heat the 8 g / 2 tsp neutral oil for the eggs in a small skillet over medium. Crack in the 2 large eggs and fry 3 minutes, until the whites are set and lightly crisp at the edges and the yolks are still runny. Slide one egg into the center of each bowl.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Serve and mix. Using oven mitts, set each sizzling bowl on a wooden board at the table with the sauce alongside. Add about half the sauce to each bowl to start, break the yolk, and mix thoroughly with a spoon, pressing the rice against the hot walls so more of it toasts. Leave the bottom crust for last and scrape it up once the rest is eaten; it keeps crisping as long as the stone stays hot. Add more sauce to taste.'
    time_min: 2

notes: |
  Why season each vegetable separately. The point of bibimbap is contrast inside one bowl: garlicky spinach, crunchy nutty sprouts, sweet carrot, soft zucchini, soy-glazed mushrooms, savory beef. Cooking them together would blur all of that into one flavor. Each namul is lightly seasoned so it tastes good alone, and the bowl ends up seasoned evenly once mixed. Salt them by taste rather than relying only on the sauce; underseasoned namul make a bowl that tastes of gochujang and little else.

  Why squeeze the spinach and not the sprouts. Blanched spinach holds a great deal of water between its leaves, and that water dilutes the sesame oil and garlic and would steam the rice. Soybean sprouts are the opposite case: their appeal is the crisp, juicy stem, which a squeeze would crush.

  How the crust works. The stone bowl is a heat reservoir. Rice pressed against oiled stone at high heat loses surface moisture, its starch gelatinizes and then dries, and the sugars and proteins at the contact surface brown. Sesame oil adds its own toasted aroma and keeps the crust from welding to the stone. Slightly dry rice, an even layer, and patience are the three things that matter; turning the heat to high to rush it scorches the bottom before the layer above it crisps.

  The umami and the acid. Glutamate comes from the soy sauce, beef, gochujang, and sesame, and the shiitake add guanylate, which multiplies the effect of glutamate. The rice vinegar in the sauce is the acid; with a fried yolk and two kinds of sesame in the bowl, it is what keeps the last bites tasting as bright as the first. Kimchi on the side adds more.

  Make-ahead. The namul, beef, and sauce keep 3 days refrigerated in separate glass containers. Bring them to room temperature before building the bowls; the hot stone will warm them through but not from fridge-cold. The rice is best cooked fresh, though day-old rice crisps very well if microwaved with a splash of water first to soften it.
