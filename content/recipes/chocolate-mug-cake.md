steps:
  - text: 'In a microwave-safe mug of at least 12 oz / 350 ml, microwave the whole milk (3 tbsp / 45 g) and neutral oil (1 tbsp / 14 g) for 20 seconds, until steaming. Whisk in the Dutch-process cocoa (2 tbsp / 10 g) with a fork until smooth and glossy. Hot liquid hydrates the cocoa''s starch and frees its fat-bound aromatics; a cold mix tastes dusty.'
    technique: 'blooming-cocoa'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Whisk in the light brown sugar (2 tbsp / 25 g), vanilla bean paste (¼ tsp / 1 g), and fine sea salt (1 pinch / 0.5 g) until the sugar dissolves. The molasses in brown sugar holds water, which matters in a microwave, where the cake loses moisture fast.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Add the all-purpose flour (3 tbsp / 25 g) and baking powder (¼ tsp / 1 g) and stir with the fork just until no dry pockets remain, scraping the bottom edge of the mug. Push the dark chocolate (½ oz / 15 g) down into the center until the batter closes over it.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Microwave on high for 60 to 75 seconds (1000 W; add 15 seconds at 700 W), until the cake has risen and the edges are set but the very top still looks slightly damp. Microwaves heat water directly, so once the starch has set, every extra second drives off moisture and the crumb turns rubbery. Undercook rather than over.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Rest 1 minute; carryover heat finishes the top and the chocolate center stays molten. Eat from the mug.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'The Adjustment: if the cake is rubbery or shrinks from the mug, cut 10 to 15 seconds next time. If the center is raw batter rather than molten chocolate, add 10 seconds. If it overflowed, the mug was too small. If it reads flat, add a second pinch (0.5 g) of fine sea salt; salt suppresses bitterness and lifts the chocolate.'

notes: |
  Why no egg. One egg is far too much for a single-serving batter, and half an egg is impractical. In a microwave, egg proteins set fast and tight, which is where the rubbery, spongy texture of most mug cakes comes from. Without egg, the structure comes from starch gelatinization and the baking powder''s lift, and the crumb stays tender.

  Why it goes rubbery. A microwave heats the water in the batter directly and evenly instead of from the outside in. The cake sets in under a minute; past that, steam keeps escaping and the starch network dries and toughens. Stopping at a damp top and resting a minute lets residual heat finish the set without the moisture loss.

  Oil, not butter. Liquid oil coats flour proteins and limits gluten, and it stays soft as the cake cools. It also means no melting step.
