safety_notes:
  - for: 'Serving the top layer of egg barely set, around 63 to 68 °C / 145 to 155 °F, below the 71 °C / 160 °F USDA target for egg dishes'
    ref: 'safety/egg-doneness#conditionally-safe-runny-yolk-set-white'
    why: 'The second egg pour is meant to stay soft and glossy, which means it does not reach the 71 °C / 160 °F that USDA gives for cooked egg dishes. The risk from clean, refrigerated US shell eggs is low for healthy adults (on the order of 1 in 20,000 eggs carries Salmonella), and soft-set eggs are eaten this way throughout Japan. For anyone pregnant, immunocompromised, very young, or elderly, use pasteurized in-shell eggs, or keep the lid on over low heat for 60 more seconds after the second pour so the top sets fully. The chicken itself is cooked to 74 °C / 165 °F before any egg goes in.'

before_you_start:
  - 'Start the rice first: it needs 30 minutes of soaking, about 15 minutes of cooking, and a 10-minute covered rest, while the oyakodon itself takes about 12 minutes. The bowl needs hot, fresh rice, so time the chicken to start when the rice goes into its rest. Leftover rice works if you reheat it until steaming.'
  - 'The egg stage takes about 90 seconds and cannot wait. Have the rice in the bowls, the eggs beaten, and the mitsuba cut before the first pour.'

steps:
  - text: 'Wash and cook the rice. Put the 300 g / 2 rice-cooker cups Japanese short-grain rice in a bowl, cover with cold tap water, swirl 10 seconds, and pour the cloudy water off at once. Repeat 3 or 4 times until the water is only slightly hazy. Drain, put the rice in a heavy saucepan with the 400 ml / 1⅔ cups cold water, and soak 30 minutes. Bring to a boil over high heat with the lid on, turn the heat to the lowest setting for 13 minutes, then turn it off and rest, covered, 10 minutes. Fluff with a wooden rice paddle and keep covered.'
    time_min: 58
  - text: 'Cut the chicken and onion. Hold the knife at a low angle and slice the 250 g chicken thighs into 3 cm pieces about 1 cm thick (sogigiri). Thin, wide pieces cook through in the short simmer and stay tender; thick cubes need longer and overcook the outside. Slice the 120 g / ½ onion 5 mm thick and cut the 6 g mitsuba into 3 cm lengths.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Make the warishita. In a 24 cm / 9 to 10 inch skillet with a lid, combine the 180 ml / ¾ cup dashi, 36 g / 2 tbsp soy sauce, 36 g / 2 tbsp mirin, 15 g / 1 tbsp sake, and 4 g / 1 tsp granulated sugar. Bring to a boil over medium-high heat and boil 30 seconds to drive off most of the alcohol. Taste it: it should be noticeably saltier and sweeter than a soup, because it seasons the chicken, the egg, and the rice underneath.'
    time_min: 2
  - text: 'Soften the onion. Add the 120 g sliced onion, spread it in an even layer, and simmer over medium heat 3 minutes, until translucent and limp. The onion releases sugars into the broth and forms a bed that keeps the chicken from sticking.'
    time_min: 3
  - text: 'Simmer the chicken. Lay the 250 g chicken pieces over the onion in one layer, skin side down if skin-on. Simmer 5 minutes, turning the pieces once and skimming off any gray foam, until no pink remains and the thickest piece reads 74 °C / 165 °F internal. The broth will reduce by about a third and turn glossy as the chicken juices and mirin concentrate.'
    time_min: 5
  - text: 'Beat the eggs. Crack the 4 large eggs into a bowl and cut through them with chopsticks 10 to 15 times, just until the yolks are broken and the whites are loosened. Stop while you can still see separate streaks of white and yolk. Underbeaten egg cooks into a two-tone custard with soft white and richer yellow patches; fully beaten egg sets into a uniform omelet.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'First pour. Raise the heat to medium-high so the broth bubbles all over. Pour about two-thirds of the beaten eggs (about 130 g, roughly 2½ eggs) in a slow spiral over the chicken and onion, working from the edge toward the center. Cover and cook 40 seconds, until the egg around the edges has set and puffed but the center is still runny. This layer binds the chicken and absorbs the broth.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Second pour. Pour the remaining 70 g (about 1½ eggs) of beaten egg over the center and any gaps, scatter the 6 g mitsuba over the top, cover, and turn off the heat. Let it stand 30 seconds. The top should be glossy and just set, trembling when you shake the pan, at around 65 °C; this soft layer is what makes oyakodon taste rich and creamy. If you want it firmer, see the safety note above.'
    time_min: 1
  - text: 'Assemble. Divide the hot rice between two deep bowls, about 330 g / 1⅔ cups each. Use a metal spatula to cut the egg and chicken down the middle, then slide one half onto each bowl, keeping the soft top intact. Pour the remaining broth from the skillet around the edges so it soaks into the rice. Serve immediately, with a pinch of the 0.5 g shichimi tōgarashi at the table if you like; its dried citrus peel and sansho pepper lift the sweet-savory broth.'
    time_min: 2

notes: |
  The broth ratio. Dashi to soy to mirin at about 5 to 1 to 1, with a little sake and sugar, is the standard donburi warishita: strong enough to season plain rice through, sweet enough to round the egg. Use hon-mirin (fermented rice wine with about 14% alcohol) rather than mirin-style seasoning, which is mostly glucose syrup with little alcohol and tastes flatly sweet.

  Glutamate and acid. Dashi supplies glutamate from kombu and inosinate from katsuobushi, which together taste much more savory than either alone; soy adds more. The dish takes no finishing acid, and that is deliberate: oyakodon is meant to be mild, sweet-savory comfort food, and a squeeze of citrus or vinegar would fight the mirin and flatten the gentle sweetness of the egg. The brightness comes from fresh mitsuba and, at the table, the citrus peel in shichimi.

  Why two pours. A single pour forces a choice between runny raw egg on top and a fully set, dry omelet. The first pour cooks into the broth and binds the chicken; the second, added with the heat off, sets gently from below and stays soft. Cold eggs and a covered pan make the timing forgiving: the egg cooks evenly from the steam, not only from the bottom.

  Chicken cut and doneness. Thigh stays juicy through the simmer where breast turns dry and stringy. Skin-on thigh gives a richer broth; take it off if you dislike soft skin. The thin slant-cut slices reach 74 °C in about 5 minutes, so the chicken is safely cooked before any egg is added.
