steps:
  - text: 'Toss the 120 g sliced green papaya and the 50 g sliced carrot with the 4 g / 1 tsp kosher salt. Rest 15 minutes, then squeeze firmly in your hands and discard the liquid. Salting draws out water so the slices stay crisp and absorb the broth instead of diluting it.'

  - text: 'While the vegetables drain, stir the 50 g / ¼ cup granulated sugar into the 360 g / 1½ cups hot water until fully dissolved. Add the 60 g / ¼ cup fish sauce, the 45 g / 3 tbsp rice vinegar, and the 15 g / 1 tbsp lime juice.'

  - text: 'Taste. Hanoi bun cha broth should taste light enough to sip: mildly salty, gently sweet, clearly sour, and never syrupy. If it reads strong, add 30 g / 2 tbsp more hot water; if flat, 5 g / 1 tsp fish sauce.'

notes: |
  How it differs from southern nuoc cham. The southern sauce is concentrated, sweeter, and lime-forward because it is drizzled over bowls and used for dipping. Hanoi bun cha broth is roughly three times more diluted and is eaten like a light soup: the pork sits in it, the noodles go through it, and the bowl is drained by the end. Making it at southern strength produces a bowl too salty to finish.

  Why vinegar. Rice vinegar gives a cleaner, more stable sourness than lime in a warm liquid; lime juice turns bitter and loses its aroma above about 60 °C. A splash of lime still goes in for the top note, but vinegar carries the acid.

  Storage. The broth without garlic, chili, or vegetables keeps 5 days refrigerated. Add the aromatics and the pickled vegetables on the day of serving; the papaya goes soft after about a day in the liquid.
